This week you learn the two kinds of math that ML uses most. Linear algebra is how models store and transform data: every embedding is a vector, every neural network layer is a matrix multiply. Probability and statistics are how we reason about uncertainty, evaluate models and run A/B tests. You do not need to prove theorems. You need to compute small examples by hand and know what each idea is for.

> **In simple words:** A vector is a list of numbers that describes something (a student, a word, a picture). A matrix is a table of numbers that transforms vectors. Probability measures how likely things are, and statistics tells you what your data really says.

## What each topic means

**Vector.** An ordered list of numbers. A student can be the vector [marks, attendance %, study hours] = [82, 90, 4]. In AI, a word or a document becomes a vector called an **embedding**.

**Magnitude (length).** How long a vector is: √(sum of squares). For [3, 4], it is √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.

**Dot product.** Multiply matching items and add them. [1, 2, 3] · [4, 5, 6] = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32. It is large when vectors point the same way.

**Cosine similarity.** The dot product divided by both magnitudes. It measures the angle between vectors, from −1 (opposite) to 1 (same direction). For [1, 0] and [1, 1]: dot = 1, lengths 1 and √2, so cosine = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707. Semantic search and RAG use this to find similar texts.

**Matrix.** A grid of numbers with rows and columns. A 2×3 matrix has 2 rows and 3 columns. A dataset of 100 students with 3 features is a 100×3 matrix.

**Shape.** The size of each dimension, written rows × columns. Checking shapes is the number-one debugging skill in ML.

**Matrix multiplication.** Each result cell is the dot product of a row from the first matrix and a column from the second. It needs the inner sizes to match: (2×3)·(3×4) works and gives 2×4; (2×3)·(2×3) does not work.

**Transpose.** Flip rows and columns. A 2×3 matrix becomes 3×2. Written Aᵀ, in NumPy `A.T`.

**Identity matrix.** A square matrix with 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere. Multiplying by it changes nothing, like multiplying a number by 1.

**Inverse.** The matrix that "undoes" another: A⁻¹·A = I. Not every matrix has one. In ML you rarely compute it yourself, but the idea appears in the closed-form solution for linear regression.

**W·x + b.** The core of every neural network layer: multiply the input vector x by a weight matrix W, then add a bias vector b. A layer with 3 inputs and 2 outputs has W of shape 2×3 and b of length 2.

**Linear transformation.** What a matrix does to vectors: stretch, rotate, squash or flip space, while keeping straight lines straight.

**Eigenvector and eigenvalue.** An **eigenvector** of a matrix is a direction that the matrix only stretches, without turning it. The **eigenvalue** is how much it stretches. If A·v = 3·v, then v is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3.

**Variance direction.** In a cloud of data points, the direction in which the points are most spread out. That direction carries the most information.

**PCA (Principal Component Analysis).** A method that finds the directions of most variance (the principal components) and keeps only the top few. It reduces 100 features to, say, 10 while keeping most of the information. The components are eigenvectors of the data's covariance matrix.

**Probability.** A number from 0 to 1 for how likely an event is. A fair coin landing heads has probability 0.5.

**Conditional probability.** The probability of A given that B happened, written P(A | B). P(rain | dark clouds) is higher than P(rain).

**Independence.** Two events are independent if one does not change the probability of the other: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Two coin tosses are independent.

**Bayes' theorem.** A rule to flip a conditional probability: P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) / P(B). Example: 1% of people have a disease, the test catches 90% of sick people, and wrongly flags 5% of healthy people. If you test positive, P(sick | positive) = (0.9 × 0.01) / (0.9 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99) = 0.009 / 0.0585 ≈ 0.15. Only about 15%, because healthy people are so many more.

**Expected value.** The long-run average of a random outcome. For a fair dice: (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) / 6 = 3.5.

**Mean, median, mode.** **Mean** is the average. **Median** is the middle value when sorted (not pulled by outliers; good for salaries). **Mode** is the most common value.

**Variance and standard deviation.** **Variance** is the average squared distance from the mean. For [2, 4, 6]: mean 4, squared distances 4, 0, 4, variance = 8/3 ≈ 2.67 (population version). **Standard deviation** is the square root, ≈ 1.63, in the same units as the data.

**Normal distribution.** The bell curve. Many natural measurements are roughly normal. About 68% of values lie within 1 standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within 2.

**Bernoulli and binomial.** **Bernoulli** is one yes/no trial (one coin toss, one click or no click). **Binomial** counts yeses in n trials (number of heads in 10 tosses).

**Uniform distribution.** Every value in a range is equally likely, like a fair dice.

**Sampling.** Measuring a small random part of a population to learn about the whole. Polls ask 2,000 people, not every voter.

**Central Limit Theorem (CLT).** Averages of many random samples follow a roughly normal distribution, whatever the shape of the original data. This is why so many statistical tests assume normality.

**Correlation vs causation.** **Correlation** means two things move together. **Causation** means one causes the other. Ice-cream sales and drowning are correlated (both rise in summer), but ice cream does not cause drowning.

**Hypothesis test.** A method to check if an effect is real or just chance. Start with the **null hypothesis** ("the new button changes nothing") and ask whether the data is surprising under it.

**p-value.** The probability of seeing data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. A small p-value (often below 0.05) means "this would be unusual by chance alone". It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

**A/B test.** Show version A to one random group and version B to another, then compare a metric (click rate, sales) with a hypothesis test. Used to decide if a new model or feature really helps.

**Confidence interval.** A range that likely contains the true value. "Conversion rate 4.2%, 95% CI 3.8% to 4.6%" describes how precise our estimate is.

**MLE (Maximum Likelihood Estimation).** Choose the model parameters that make the observed data most probable. If a coin gave 7 heads in 10 tosses, the MLE of P(heads) is 0.7. Many ML loss functions (like log loss) come from MLE.

**Derivative.** How fast something changes. The derivative of x² is 2x, so at x = 3 the slope is 6. You will use derivatives for gradient descent next week.

**Stack (DSA).** A last-in, first-out collection: you add and remove from the top. Python lists work as stacks with `append` and `pop`. Used for brackets, undo, and parsing.

**Monotonic stack (DSA).** A stack kept in increasing or decreasing order. It answers "next greater element" style questions for every item in one pass, O(n).

## End-of-week self-check

1. Compute the cosine similarity of [1, 2] and [2, 4]. What does the result mean?
2. A is 3×2 and B is 2×5. What is the shape of A·B? Does B·A work?
3. A layer takes a 512-number input and outputs 128 numbers. What are the shapes of W and b?
4. In one sentence: what does PCA do?
5. 1% of transactions are fraud. A model flags 80% of frauds and wrongly flags 2% of normal transactions. What fraction of flagged transactions are really fraud?
6. Why is the median often better than the mean for salaries?
7. Your A/B test gives p = 0.03. What does that mean, and what does it not mean?
8. What does a monotonic stack help you find in one pass?

**Answers**

1. Dot = 2 + 8 = 10. Lengths are √5 and √20. √5 × √20 = √100 = 10. Cosine = 10/10 = 1. They point in exactly the same direction (one is twice the other).
2. A·B is 3×5. B·A is (2×5)·(3×2): the inner sizes 5 and 3 do not match, so it does not work.
3. W is 128×512 (so W·x gives 128 numbers) and b has length 128.
4. PCA finds the directions in which the data varies most and projects the data onto the top few, reducing the number of features while keeping most of the information.
5. P(fraud | flagged) = (0.8 × 0.01) / (0.8 × 0.01 + 0.02 × 0.99) = 0.008 / (0.008 + 0.0198) = 0.008 / 0.0278 ≈ 0.29. Only about 29% of flags are real fraud.
6. A few very high salaries pull the mean up a lot, but the median (middle value) barely moves, so it better represents a typical person.
7. If the new version truly made no difference, there would be only a 3% chance of seeing a difference this large. It does not mean there is a 97% chance that B is better, and it says nothing about how big or useful the difference is.
8. For every element, the next (or previous) greater or smaller element. Examples: Daily Temperatures, Next Greater Element, Largest Rectangle in Histogram.

## If you fall behind

Eigenvectors can be kept at the intuition level; you will not compute them by hand in interviews. Confidence intervals and MLE can be read quickly. Do not skip dot product, cosine similarity, matrix shapes, Bayes' theorem or p-values: they come up again in ML, RAG and interviews.
