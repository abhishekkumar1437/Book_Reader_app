The first two days complete your portfolio. Then you switch fully to interview mode. You will not learn new topics now. Instead you practise saying what you know, clearly and quickly, the way an interviewer wants to hear it. Every question bank on the shelf is used this week.

> **In simple words:** Finish the demo and resume. Then answer hundreds of interview questions out loud, find your weak spots, and fix them. Practise talking about your projects until it feels natural.

How to work through a question bank (Days 143–146):

1. Read the question. Close your eyes or look away. Answer aloud in under 90 seconds.
2. Read the model answer. Mark yourself: ✓ (good), ~ (partly), ✗ (could not).
3. For every ~ and ✗, write the question in your weak list with the link to the chapter that teaches it.
4. At the end of the day, re-read only those sections, then answer the weak questions once more.

## What each topic means

**Demo video.** A short screen recording of your app in use with your voice explaining it: the problem (15 seconds), a live walk-through (90 seconds), the architecture and one technical highlight (30 seconds), and results (15 seconds).

**One-page resume.** For early-career roles, one page is the standard. Top: name, contact, GitHub, LinkedIn, portfolio links. Then projects (with numbers), skills, experience and education. Remove anything that does not support the AI Engineer role.

**ATS (Applicant Tracking System).** Software companies use to store and filter resumes. Use a simple layout (no tables or images for text), standard section names, and the keywords from the job post that honestly apply to you (Python, PyTorch, RAG, FastAPI, Docker, AWS).

**Project pitch (2 minutes).** Problem → what you built → architecture in one sentence → one hard challenge and how you solved it → result with a number → what you would improve.

**Project deep-dive (5–15 minutes).** The interviewer digs into one project: design choices, alternatives you considered, failures, evaluation, cost, scaling. They are checking whether you really built it and understand it.

**Trade-off.** A choice where gaining one thing costs another: accuracy vs latency, cost vs quality, simplicity vs flexibility. Strong candidates name the trade-off and say why their choice fits the requirements.

**Question bank.** A collection of likely interview questions with model answers. Starred (⭐) questions are the most frequently asked.

**Coding round.** One or two DSA problems in 45–60 minutes, solved while explaining your thinking. Sometimes replaced by a practical task, like "clean this data and train a model" or "build a small RAG endpoint".

**ML/LLM concepts round.** Spoken questions on fundamentals and their use: "explain overfitting", "how does attention work?", "how would you evaluate a RAG system?".

**System design round.** Design an AI product end to end in 45 minutes (see Weeks 18–19).

**Take-home assignment.** A small project to do at home in a few days. Treat it like a portfolio project: clean code, README, tests, evaluation and a short write-up of trade-offs.

**Behavioural round.** Questions about how you work: teamwork, conflict, failure, learning. Often run by a hiring manager or HR.

**STAR method.** A structure for behavioural answers: **S**ituation (the context), **T**ask (your goal), **A**ction (what you did, use "I"), **R**esult (what happened, ideally with a number, and what you learned). Keep each answer about 2 minutes.

**Six STAR stories to prepare.** A hard technical problem you solved; a time you failed and what you learned; learning something new fast (this 150-day plan is a great example); a disagreement with someone and how you handled it; a time you improved something with data; a project you are proud of.

**Company-style mock.** A mock set up like the target company's real round: their usual length, difficulty and format (for example a shared online editor, no code execution).

**DSA skeletons.** The 12 core templates (two-pointer sum, sliding window, prefix-sum counting, monotonic stack, linked list reversal, binary search for the first true, grid BFS, topological sort, union-find, house robber, coin change, LCS). Most interview problems are built from them.

## End-of-week self-check

1. Give your 2-minute pitch for the capstone. Did it include a number?
2. An interviewer asks: "Why did you use RAG and not fine-tuning in your chatbot?" Answer.
3. "How did you evaluate your RAG system?" Answer with your real metrics.
4. "What would you do if your app had 100 times more users tomorrow?"
5. Tell a STAR story about a failure.
6. Explain overfitting and three ways to reduce it, in under a minute.
7. "What happens, step by step, when a user asks your app a question?"
8. What is your answer to "Do you have any questions for us?"

**Answers**

1. Check your recording against: problem, solution, architecture in one sentence, one challenge, a measurable result, one improvement. If there was no number, add one (latency, eval score, cost per question, number of test users).
2. Example: "The knowledge is in documents that change often and are private, and users need citations. RAG lets me update knowledge by re-indexing instead of retraining, it grounds answers in sources, and it is cheaper to build. Fine-tuning is better for teaching a style or format, not for adding facts."
3. Example: "I built a golden set of 30 questions with expected answers and pages. I measured retrieval hit rate at 5, answer correctness, and faithfulness with an LLM judge that I checked against my own grading on 10 samples. Hybrid search raised hit rate from X to Y." Use your real numbers.
4. Example: measure first to find the bottleneck (usually LLM cost and latency, then the vector DB). Then: autoscale the API containers; move ingestion to a background queue; add prompt and semantic caching; route easy questions to a smaller model; move the vector store to a managed or scalable option; add rate limits per user; watch p95 and cost dashboards.
5. Check your story has all four parts, uses "I", is about 2 minutes, and ends with what you learned and changed afterwards.
6. Overfitting is when a model learns the training data, including its noise, so it does well on training data but badly on new data. You see it as a big gap between train and validation scores. Reduce it with more data or augmentation, a simpler model or regularization (L1/L2, dropout), and early stopping, checked with cross-validation.
7. Example for the capstone: the UI sends the question with the user's token → the API verifies the token → the question is embedded → the vector DB returns the top chunks for this user only → optional reranking → the prompt is built with the chunks and instructions → the LLM streams an answer with citations → the app logs latency, tokens and cost → the UI shows the answer and sources.
8. Have three ready, about the work and team: "What does a typical week look like for this role?", "How do you evaluate your AI features before release?", "What would success look like in my first 3 months?"

## If you fall behind

If the capstone is not finished, give it Days 141–142 fully and shorten the question-bank days to the starred (⭐) questions only. The glossary review is optional. Never skip the project deep-dive practice (Day 145) or the STAR stories; they decide many final-round results.
