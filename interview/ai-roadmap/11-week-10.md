## What each topic means

**Gradio and Streamlit.** Python libraries that turn a function into a small web app in a few lines, so people can upload an image and see the prediction. Good for demos, not for large production apps.

**Demo.** A way for others to try your project: a live link, a short screen recording, or both. A demo makes recruiters believe the project works.

**Error analysis.** Looking at the examples the model got wrong to find patterns (blurry photos, two similar classes). It tells you what to fix next.

**NLP (Natural Language Processing).** Making computers work with human language: classification (spam, sentiment), named entity recognition (finding names, places), translation, summarisation, question answering.

**Text cleaning.** Preparing text: lowercasing, removing extra spaces, HTML or URLs. For modern Transformers, keep cleaning light, because they learn from raw text.

**Stop words.** Very common words like "the", "is", "and". Classic methods often remove them; Transformers keep them.

**Stemming and lemmatization.** Reducing words to a base form. Stemming cuts endings roughly ("running" → "run", "studies" → "studi"); lemmatization uses a dictionary ("better" → "good").

**Bag of words.** Represent a document by counting each word, ignoring order. "Good movie, good songs" → {good: 2, movie: 1, songs: 1}.

**TF-IDF.** Term frequency × inverse document frequency. A word scores high if it is common in this document but rare across all documents. One common version: TF = count / words in doc; IDF = ln(N / docs containing the word). If "cricket" appears 2 times in a 10-word document and in 1 of 3 documents: TF = 0.2, IDF = ln(3) ≈ 1.10, TF-IDF ≈ 0.22. Libraries like scikit-learn use a slightly smoothed formula, so their numbers differ a little.

**n-gram.** A sequence of n words. "not good" is a 2-gram (bigram). Bigrams help bag-of-words models catch phrases like "not good".

**Token.** The unit of text a model reads. It can be a word, part of a word, or a character. LLMs read and are billed by tokens. In English, one token is roughly 3–4 characters, or about ¾ of a word on average.

**Tokenization.** Splitting text into tokens and mapping each to an ID number from a fixed **vocabulary**.

**Word, character and subword tokenization.** **Word-level** has huge vocabularies and cannot handle new words. **Character-level** has tiny vocabularies but very long sequences. **Subword** (the modern choice) keeps common words whole and splits rare words into pieces: "unbelievable" → "un", "believ", "able".

**BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding).** A subword method: start with characters, then repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token, until the vocabulary reaches the target size. GPT models use byte-level BPE.

**WordPiece.** A subword method similar to BPE but it chooses merges by likelihood. Used by BERT. Continuation pieces start with `##`, as in "play", "##ing".

**SentencePiece.** A tokenizer library that works on raw text including spaces (shown as ▁), so it suits languages without spaces between words. Used by T5, Llama and many others.

**Special tokens.** Tokens with special jobs, like `[CLS]` and `[SEP]` in BERT, `<pad>` for padding, and end-of-text tokens that tell the model where a text ends.

**Why tokens matter for cost.** LLM APIs charge per input and output token, and the context window is measured in tokens. Hindi and other non-Latin scripts often need more tokens per word than English, so the same text can cost more.

**One-hot problem.** In one-hot vectors every word is equally far from every other word, so "king" and "queen" look as different as "king" and "banana". They are also huge (one dimension per word).

**Word embedding.** A dense vector of, say, 300 numbers for each word, learned so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors.

**Word2Vec.** A method to learn embeddings by predicting words from their neighbours. **CBOW** predicts the middle word from the surrounding words; **skip-gram** predicts the surrounding words from the middle word.

**king − man + woman ≈ queen.** A famous result: directions in embedding space capture relationships, so vector arithmetic finds analogies.

**GloVe.** Another classic embedding method that learns from global word co-occurrence counts.

**Static vs contextual embeddings.** **Static** (Word2Vec, GloVe): one vector per word, whatever the sentence. **Contextual** (BERT, GPT): the vector depends on the sentence, so "bank" in "river bank" and "bank loan" gets different vectors.

**Recursion (DSA).** A function that calls itself on a smaller version of the problem, with a base case that stops it.

**Backtracking (DSA).** Build a solution step by step; when a choice leads nowhere, undo it ("backtrack") and try the next choice. Used for "generate all subsets/permutations/combinations" and puzzles like N-Queens.

## End-of-week self-check

1. In your image project, what is the difference between the feature-extraction and fine-tuning results, and why?
2. Why does TF-IDF give a low score to the word "the"?
3. Why do modern LLMs use subword tokenization instead of whole words?
4. Corpus words: "low", "lower", "lowest" split into characters. Which pair would BPE merge first?
5. Why might the same paragraph cost more tokens in Hindi than in English?
6. What is wrong with one-hot vectors for words?
7. What is the difference between CBOW and skip-gram?
8. What is the time complexity of generating all subsets of n items, and why?

**Answers**

1. Your own numbers. Usually fine-tuning wins when the new images differ from ImageNet and you have enough data, because the inner layers adapt; feature extraction is faster and safer with very little data.
2. "the" appears in almost every document, so IDF = ln(N / nearly N) is close to 0, which makes TF-IDF close to 0 however often it appears.
3. Whole-word vocabularies are huge and still miss new or rare words. Subwords keep common words as one token and build rare words from pieces, so any text can be represented with a vocabulary of tens of thousands of tokens.
4. "l" + "o" (or "o" + "w"; both pairs appear in all three words, 3 times each). A real implementation breaks the tie by a fixed rule; after merging, "lo" + "w" becomes the next frequent pair.
5. Tokenizers are trained mostly on English text, so English words are often single tokens while Hindi words are split into many small pieces. More tokens means more cost and less room in the context window.
6. They are huge (one dimension per vocabulary word), sparse, and every pair of words is equally different, so they carry no meaning or similarity.
7. CBOW predicts the centre word from its surrounding words. Skip-gram predicts the surrounding words from the centre word. Skip-gram tends to work better for rare words.
8. O(n · 2ⁿ). There are 2ⁿ subsets (each item is in or out), and copying each subset into the result takes up to n steps.
