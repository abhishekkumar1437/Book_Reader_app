Modern physics is class 12 chapters 11–13 (dual nature, atoms, nuclei) plus B.Sc. points: fine structure, Zeeman effect, nuclear models, elementary particles. TRE 1.0 asked four questions here (photoelectric stopping potential, fine structure, α-decay, "which reaction is not allowed") and TRE 2.0 asked about cosmic rays, beta rays and the best nuclear fuel.

> **In simple words:** Around 1900 physicists found that light sometimes behaves like particles (photons) and electrons sometimes behave like waves. Atoms have a tiny heavy nucleus with electrons in fixed energy levels; jumping between levels gives sharp spectral lines. The nucleus itself can break (radioactivity, fission) or join (fusion), turning a little mass into a lot of energy by E = mc².

**Cathode rays:** a stream of electrons from the cathode of a discharge tube at low pressure (≈ 0.01 mm of Hg). They travel in straight lines, cast shadows, are deflected by electric and magnetic fields (toward the positive plate), heat and ionise gases, and produce X-rays when stopped by a hard metal target. Their nature does not depend on the gas or cathode material.

Mass of electron mₑ = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg; proton is about **1836 times** heavier.

## 2. Photoelectric effect

When light of suitable frequency falls on a metal surface, electrons are emitted. Hertz (1887) noticed it; Hallwachs and Lenard studied it; **Einstein (1905)** explained it using photons (Nobel Prize 1921); **Millikan (1916)** verified the equation.

<div class="formula">

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Photon energy | E = hν = hc/λ ; h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s |
| Handy form | E (eV) = 1240 / λ (nm) |
| Einstein's equation | K_max = hν − φ = h(ν − ν₀) |
| Stopping potential | eV₀ = K_max, so V₀ (volts) = hν(eV) − φ(eV) |
| Threshold | φ = hν₀ = hc/λ₀ ; λ₀ (nm) = 1240/φ (eV) |
| Photon momentum | p = h/λ = E/c |

</div>

**Experimental laws (classic statements):**

1. There is a **threshold frequency** ν₀ for each metal; below it no emission, however intense the light.
2. Maximum kinetic energy (and stopping potential) depends on **frequency**, not on intensity.
3. Photoelectric **current (number of electrons)** is proportional to **intensity**.
4. Emission is **instantaneous** (< 10⁻⁹ s).

Wave theory could not explain 1, 2 and 4 — that proved the particle (photon) nature of light.

Uses of photocells: burglar alarms, automatic doors, light meters, solar cells (photovoltaic), reproduction of sound in cinema films.

**Compton effect (1923):** X-rays scattered by electrons come out with a **longer** wavelength; Δλ = (h/mₑc)(1 − cos θ), with h/mₑc = 0.00243 nm (Compton wavelength). Another proof that photons carry momentum.

## 3. Matter waves — de Broglie

**Louis de Broglie (1924):** every moving particle has a wave, **λ = h/p = h/mv**.

<div class="formula">

| Case | Formula |
|---|---|
| General | λ = h/(mv) = h/√(2mK) |
| Electron accelerated through V volts | λ = 1.227/√V nm (= 12.27/√V Å) |
| Thermal particle at temperature T | λ = h/√(3mkT) |

</div>

**Worked numerical 2.** Electron through 100 V: λ = 1.227/10 = **0.1227 nm ≈ 1.23 Å** — comparable to X-ray wavelengths and atomic spacing. Through 54 V (Davisson–Germer): λ = 1.227/√54 = 1.227/7.35 ≈ **0.167 nm**.

**Davisson and Germer (1927)** diffracted electrons from a nickel crystal (strong peak at 54 V, scattering angle 50°) — the first proof of matter waves. G. P. Thomson did electron diffraction through thin foils the same year. This wave nature is the basis of the **electron microscope** (much shorter λ than light → much higher resolution).

For the same kinetic energy, a heavier particle has a shorter λ (λ ∝ 1/√m). Everyday objects have absurdly small λ, so their wave nature is never noticed.

**Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (1927):** Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π — position and momentum cannot both be known exactly.

## 4. X-rays

Discovered by **Wilhelm Röntgen (1895)**, first Nobel Prize in Physics (1901). Produced when fast electrons hit a heavy, high-melting-point target (tungsten, molybdenum) in a **Coolidge tube**.

| Point | Fact |
|---|---|
| Nature | EM waves, λ ≈ 0.01 – 10 nm; not deflected by E or B fields |
| Continuous spectrum | Bremsstrahlung ("braking radiation") as electrons slow down |
| Cut-off wavelength | λ_min = hc/(eV) = 1240/V nm (V in volts) — Duane–Hunt law |
| Characteristic lines (Kα, Kβ) | Electron jumps into vacancies in inner shells; depend on target element |
| Moseley's law (1913) | √ν ∝ (Z − 1) — atomic number, not atomic mass, orders the periodic table |
| Hard vs soft X-rays | Hard: high energy, short λ, high accelerating voltage; intensity controlled by filament current |
| Crystal diffraction | Bragg's law 2d sin θ = nλ (W. H. and W. L. Bragg, 1913) |

**Worked numerical 3.** Tube at 50 kV: λ_min = 1240/50,000 nm = **0.0248 nm (0.248 Å)**.

## 5. Atomic models — Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr

- **Thomson (1904), "plum pudding":** positive sphere with electrons embedded; could not explain large-angle scattering.
- **Rutherford's α-scattering (gold foil ≈ 0.1 μm thick):** most α-particles passed straight through (atom is mostly empty), a few deflected through large angles, about 1 in 8000 bounced back → all positive charge and almost all mass is concentrated in a tiny **nucleus** (≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m, atom ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m). Number scattered N(θ) ∝ 1/sin⁴(θ/2). Distance of closest approach r₀ = 2kZe²/K (for α, charge 2e).
- **Rutherford's model failed** to explain atomic stability (an accelerating electron should radiate and spiral into the nucleus) and line spectra.

### Bohr model of hydrogen (1913)

**Postulates:** (1) electrons move in certain **stationary orbits** without radiating; (2) angular momentum is quantised, **mvr = nh/2π**; (3) a photon is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between orbits: **hν = E₂ − E₁**.

|---|---|
| Radius | rₙ = 0.529 n²/Z Å (Bohr radius a₀ = 0.529 Å) |
| Speed | vₙ = 2.18 × 10⁶ Z/n m/s (= c/137 for n = 1, Z = 1) |
| Energy | Eₙ = −13.6 Z²/n² eV |
| Kinetic / potential energy | K = −E ; U = 2E |
| Wavelength of line | 1/λ = R Z² (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ |
| Number of lines from level n | n(n − 1)/2 |

</div>

**Limitations of Bohr's model:** works only for one-electron species (H, He⁺, Li²⁺); cannot explain relative line intensities, **fine structure**, the Zeeman and Stark effects, or chemical bonding. It also violates the uncertainty principle (exact orbit). Sommerfeld added elliptical orbits (1916); quantum mechanics (Schrödinger, 1926) replaced it.

## 6. Fine structure, Zeeman effect and quantum numbers

- **Fine structure:** with a high-resolution spectroscope, each hydrogen line (e.g. Hα) is seen as a group of very close lines. Cause: **electron spin–orbit coupling** — the electron's spin magnetic moment interacts with the magnetic field produced by its orbital motion (plus a small relativistic correction). Its size is set by the **fine-structure constant α = e²/(4πε₀ħc) ≈ 1/137**. Example: the sodium **D-lines** D₁ (589.6 nm) and D₂ (589.0 nm) are a fine-structure doublet.
- **Hyperfine structure:** much smaller splitting due to **nuclear spin** interacting with the electron (e.g. the 21 cm radio line of hydrogen used in astronomy).
- **Electron spin** was proposed by Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit (1925); the Stern–Gerlach experiment (1922) showed space quantisation with silver atoms.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) asked: "The fine structure of atomic spectral lines arises from (A) electron spin–orbit coupling (B) nuclear spin (C) interaction between electron and nucleus." Worked answer: **(A) spin–orbit coupling.** Nuclear spin gives **hyperfine** structure — the trap.

| Effect | Splitting of spectral lines by |
|---|---|
| Zeeman effect (1896) | External **magnetic** field (normal: three lines; anomalous: due to spin) |
| Stark effect (1913) | External **electric** field |
| Raman effect (C. V. Raman, 28 Feb 1928 — National Science Day) | Scattering of light by molecules with a change of frequency |

**Quantum numbers (brief):** principal n (shell, energy); azimuthal l = 0 … n − 1 (subshell s, p, d, f); magnetic mₗ = −l … +l (orientation); spin mₛ = ±½. **Pauli's exclusion principle:** no two electrons in an atom have all four the same. (Chemistry side: [Atomic Structure](/interview/bpsc-tre4-science/chemistry/atomic-structure).)

**Lasers** (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation): based on **stimulated emission** (Einstein, 1917) and **population inversion**; first laser by T. H. Maiman (1960, ruby). Laser light is monochromatic, coherent, directional and intense.

## 7. The nucleus

Notation ᴬ_Z X: Z = atomic (proton) number, A = mass (nucleon) number, N = A − Z neutrons.

| Term | Same | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Isotopes | Z | ¹H, ²H (deuterium), ³H (tritium); ¹²C, ¹⁴C |
| Isobars | A | ⁴⁰Ar, ⁴⁰Ca |
| Isotones | N | ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N, ¹⁶O (N = 8 each) |
| Isomers (nuclear) | Z and A, different energy state | ⁹⁹ᵐTc |

<div class="formula">

| Quantity | Formula / value |
|---|---|
| Nuclear radius | R = R₀ A^(1/3), R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm (1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m) |
| Nuclear density | ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³, **same for all nuclei** (independent of A) |
| Atomic mass unit | 1 u = 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV/c² |
| Mass defect | Δm = [Z mₚ + N mₙ] − M_nucleus |
| Binding energy | BE = Δm × 931.5 MeV (Δm in u) |
| Mass–energy | E = mc² ; 1 kg ↔ 9 × 10¹⁶ J |

</div>

**Worked numerical 8.** Ratio of radii of ²⁷Al and ⁸Be? (27/8)^(1/3) = 3/2 = **1.5**. Radius of ²⁷Al = 1.2 × 3 = **3.6 fm**.

**Worked numerical 9.** Binding energy of ⁴He. Using atomic masses: 2 × 1.007825 (¹H) + 2 × 1.008665 (n) = 4.032980 u; mass of ⁴He atom = 4.002603 u. Δm = 0.030377 u. BE = 0.030377 × 931.5 ≈ **28.3 MeV**, i.e. **7.07 MeV per nucleon**.

**Worked numerical 10.** Energy from converting 1 g of mass fully: E = 10⁻³ × (3 × 10⁸)² = **9 × 10¹³ J**.

- Low for light nuclei, maximum ≈ **8.8 MeV near A = 56 (iron)** (strictly ⁶²Ni is a hair higher, but books say iron), then slowly falls to ≈ 7.6 MeV for uranium.
- Heavy nuclei gain stability by **splitting (fission)**; light nuclei by **joining (fusion)** — both move toward the peak and release energy.
- Roughly constant BE/A (≈ 8 MeV) for A = 30–170 shows the **saturation** (short range) of nuclear forces.

**Nuclear force:** strongest of all forces (strong interaction), **very short range (≈ 2–3 fm)**, attractive (repulsive below ≈ 0.8 fm), **charge-independent** (p–p, n–n, n–p about equal), not a central inverse-square force. Explained by exchange of **mesons (pions)** — Yukawa. Nuclear models: liquid-drop model (fission, semi-empirical mass formula — Bohr and Wheeler) and shell model (magic numbers 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126).

## 8. Radioactivity

Discovered by **Henri Becquerel (1896)** in uranium salts; Marie and Pierre Curie discovered **polonium and radium (1898)**. Radioactivity is a **nuclear** property — unaffected by temperature, pressure, chemical combination or physical state.

| Property | Alpha (α) | Beta (β⁻) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Helium nucleus ⁴₂He²⁺ | Fast electron (β⁺ = positron) | EM wave (photon) |
| Charge | +2e | −e | 0 |
| Deflection in E/B field | Yes (slightly) | Yes (strongly, opposite side) | **No** |
| Speed | ≈ 0.05c – 0.1c | up to ≈ 0.99c | c |
| Ionising power | Highest (≈ 100 × β) | Medium | Lowest |
| Penetrating power | Lowest — stopped by paper / few cm of air | Medium — few mm of aluminium | Highest — several cm of lead |
| Energy spectrum | Discrete (line) | **Continuous** (shared with antineutrino — reason Pauli proposed the neutrino) | Discrete |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Which of the following is not an electromagnetic wave? (A) gamma ray (B) X-ray (C) beta ray." **Official answer: (C) beta ray** — a stream of electrons.

### Displacement (Soddy–Fajans) laws

| Decay | Change in Z | Change in A | Inside the nucleus |
|---|---|---|---|
| α | −2 | −4 | Emits ⁴He |
| β⁻ | +1 | 0 | n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ |
| β⁺ | −1 | 0 | p → n + e⁺ + ν |
| Electron capture | −1 | 0 | p + e⁻ → n + ν |
| γ | 0 | 0 | Excited nucleus drops to lower energy |

## 9. Nuclear fission and fusion

**Fission** (Hahn and Strassmann, 1938; explained by Meitner and Frisch): a heavy nucleus splits after absorbing a neutron.

²³⁵U + ¹n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3 ¹n + ≈ 200 MeV

- **Energy ≈ 200 MeV per fission**; 2–3 neutrons released → **chain reaction**.
- **²³⁵U** (natural abundance only ≈ 0.72%) is fissile with **slow (thermal) neutrons** (≈ 0.025 eV); ²³⁸U is not (it is **fertile** → ²³⁹Pu). ²³²Th is fertile → ²³³U (India's three-stage programme uses thorium; India has large thorium reserves in monazite sands of Kerala).
- **Critical mass:** minimum mass for a self-sustaining chain reaction. Uncontrolled = atom bomb; controlled = reactor (multiplication factor k = 1).

| Reactor part | Material | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel | Enriched or natural U (²³⁵U), Pu-239 | Fission |
| **Moderator** | Heavy water (D₂O), graphite, ordinary water | **Slows down** fast neutrons |
| **Control rods** | **Cadmium**, boron | **Absorb** neutrons, control rate |
| Coolant | Water, heavy water, liquid sodium, CO₂ | Carries heat away |
| Shield | Concrete, lead | Stops radiation |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Which of the following is a better nuclear fuel? (A) Thorium 236 (B) Uranium 235 (C) Neptunium 239." **Official answer: (B) Uranium-235.** (Thorium used in reactors is ²³²Th, and it is only fertile; ²³⁹Np decays to ²³⁹Pu.)

**Fusion:** light nuclei combine at very high temperature (≈ 10⁷–10⁸ K, "thermonuclear"). Source of energy of the **Sun and stars** (proton–proton chain: 4 ¹H → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν + **26.7 MeV**) and of the **hydrogen bomb**. D + T → ⁴He + n + **17.6 MeV**. Fusion gives more energy **per unit mass** than fission (Sun's chain ≈ 6.7 MeV per nucleon, D–T ≈ 3.5, fission ≈ 0.85) and little long-lived waste. Research: tokamaks, **ITER** (France; India is a partner).

## 10. Cosmic rays

- Discovered by **Victor Hess (1912)** using balloon flights (ionisation increased with height); named "cosmic rays" by Millikan.
- **Primary cosmic rays** are **high-energy charged particles** from space: about **90% protons, ≈ 9% alpha particles, ≈ 1% heavier nuclei** and a little electrons. They come from the Sun (low energy) and from supernovae/galactic sources (high energy).
- Hitting the upper atmosphere they produce **secondary showers**: pions, **muons** (most cosmic particles reaching sea level), electrons, positrons, gamma photons, neutrinos. The positron (1932) and muon (1936) were discovered in cosmic rays.
- They are deflected by the earth's magnetic field (**latitude effect** — fewer near the equator), which proves they are charged.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Cosmic rays are (A) charged particles (B) uncharged particles (C) electromagnetic waves." **Official answer: (A) charged particles.** (Despite the name "rays", primaries are mostly protons.)

## 11. Elementary particles and conservation laws (B.Sc.)

| Family | Members | Feels |
|---|---|---|
| Leptons (spin ½) | e⁻, μ⁻, τ⁻ and their neutrinos | Weak (and EM if charged), not strong |
| Hadrons — baryons (3 quarks, spin ½, 3/2) | p (uud), n (udd), Λ⁰ (uds), Σ, Ξ, Ω⁻ | Strong |
| Hadrons — mesons (quark + antiquark, spin 0, 1) | π, K, η, ρ | Strong |
| Gauge bosons | photon (EM), W±, Z⁰ (weak), gluon (strong), graviton (hypothetical) | Carry forces |

Quark charges: u = +2/3, d = −1/3, s = −1/3 (strangeness −1). K⁺ = us̄ (S = +1); Λ⁰ and Σ have S = −1.

**Conservation laws used to judge "allowed / not allowed" reactions:**

| Quantity | Conserved in |
|---|---|
| Energy, momentum, angular momentum, **charge** | All interactions — always |
| **Baryon number** B (baryons +1, antibaryons −1, others 0) | All |
| **Lepton number** Lₑ, L_μ, L_τ (each family separately) | All |
| **Strangeness** S | **Strong and EM** interactions; can change by 1 in **weak** decays |
| Isospin | Strong only |

**How to check a reaction:** add up charge, baryon number, lepton number and (for a strong reaction) strangeness on both sides. If any one is not equal, the reaction is **not allowed**.

| Reaction | Charge | B | S | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| π⁻ + p → K⁰ + Λ⁰ | 0 → 0 | 1 → 1 | 0 → +1 − 1 = 0 | **Allowed** ("associated production" of strange particles) |
| π⁻ + p → K⁻ + p | 0 → 0 | 1 → 1 | 0 → −1 | **Not allowed** as a strong reaction (strangeness) |
| p → n + e⁺ + ν (free proton) | ok | ok | — | Not allowed for a free proton (energy: n is heavier); occurs only inside nuclei |
| n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ | 0 → 0 | 1 → 1 | lepton 0 → 1 − 1 = 0 | **Allowed** (β-decay) |
| ρ⁰ → π⁺ + π⁻ | 0 → 0 | 0 → 0 | 0 → 0 | **Allowed** (strong decay) |
| p + p → p + p + p̄ | 2 → 1 | 2 → 2 | — | **Not allowed** (charge); correct form p + p → p + p + p + p̄ |

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) asked "Which of the following reactions is not allowed?" with three particle reactions (involving π, K, Σ/Λ, n, p, ρ⁰). The symbols are garbled in our copy, so we do not state the option; solve it exactly as in the table above — the one that breaks charge, baryon number, lepton number or strangeness is the answer.

## Traps and exceptions

- Stopping potential and K_max depend on **frequency**, not intensity; **number** of photoelectrons depends on intensity.
- K_max–ν graphs for different metals are **parallel** (slope h).
- **Fine structure = spin–orbit coupling**; **hyperfine = nuclear spin**; Zeeman = magnetic field; Stark = electric field.
- Balmer series is the **visible** one; Lyman UV; Paschen, Brackett, Pfund IR.
- Bohr energies are **negative**; ionisation energy of H = +13.6 eV. Higher orbit = higher (less negative) energy but lower speed.
- **β-rays are electrons**, not EM waves; γ-rays are EM. **Cosmic rays are mostly charged particles (protons)** in spite of the name.
- In β⁻ decay, the electron comes **from the nucleus** (a neutron changes to a proton) — there are no electrons stored in the nucleus.
- α-decay: Z −2, A −4. β⁻: Z +1, A same. γ: no change.
- **Half-life does not depend** on amount, temperature or pressure. Mean life = 1.443 × half-life (longer, not shorter).
- Nuclear density is the **same** for all nuclei; nuclear radius ∝ A^(1/3), volume ∝ A.
- BE per nucleon peaks near **iron-56** (≈ 8.8 MeV); total BE keeps increasing with A.
- **Moderator slows**, **control rod absorbs** — cadmium/boron are control rods, not moderators.
- Fissile fuel is **U-235** (and Pu-239, U-233); Th-232 and U-238 are fertile, not fissile.
- Fusion gives more energy per kg than fission, but fission gives more energy **per reaction** (200 MeV vs 17.6 MeV).
- Strangeness is conserved in strong interactions but **not in weak** ones.

## Practice with answers

1. Energy of a photon of wavelength 620 nm? — **2 eV** (1240/620).
2. Threshold wavelength of a metal with φ = 3.1 eV? — **400 nm** (1240/3.1).
3. If light intensity is doubled (frequency same), stopping potential? — **Unchanged** (depends only on frequency).
4. de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through 400 V? — **≈ 0.061 nm** (1.227/20).
5. Who proved the wave nature of electrons? — **Davisson and Germer** (1927).
6. Minimum wavelength of X-rays from a 31 kV tube? — **0.04 nm** (1240/31,000).
7. Radius of the third Bohr orbit of hydrogen? — **4.76 Å** (0.529 × 9).
8. Energy of hydrogen electron in n = 3? — **−1.51 eV** (−13.6/9).
9. Which hydrogen series lies in the visible region? — **Balmer**.
10. Fine structure of spectral lines is due to? — **Spin–orbit coupling** (TRE 1.0).
11. Splitting of lines in a magnetic field is called? — **Zeeman effect**.
12. Ratio of nuclear radii of ⁶⁴Cu and ⁸Be? — **2** ((64/8)^(1/3)).
13. ²²⁶₈₈Ra emits an α-particle; product? — **²²²₈₆Rn** (Z −2, A −4).
14. After 4 half-lives, what fraction remains? — **1/16**.
15. Half-life is 693 s; decay constant? — **10⁻³ s⁻¹** (0.693/693).
16. Material used as control rods? — **Cadmium (or boron)** (absorbs neutrons).
17. Better nuclear fuel: Th-236, U-235 or Np-239? — **U-235** (TRE 2.0 official B).
18. Cosmic rays are mainly? — **Charged particles, mostly protons** (TRE 2.0 official A).
19. Which conservation law forbids π⁻ + p → K⁻ + p as a strong reaction? — **Strangeness** (0 → −1).
20. Energy source of the Sun? — **Nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium**.
