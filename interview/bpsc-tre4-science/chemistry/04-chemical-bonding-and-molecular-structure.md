This chapter explains why atoms join, what shapes the molecules take and how strong the bonds are. It runs from NCERT class 10 (ionic and covalent compounds) through class 11 ("Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure") to B.Sc. points: pπ–dπ bonds, MOT diagrams, formal charge and percent ionic character. TRE 2.0 alone asked three bonding questions: σ and π bonds in tetracyanoethylene, the species with equal σ and π bonds, and where hydrogen bonding is strongest.

> **In simple words:** Atoms bond to reach a stable (usually noble-gas) arrangement. Electron pairs around a central atom push each other as far apart as possible, and that push decides the shape. Every single bond is one σ bond; a double bond is σ + π; a triple bond is σ + 2π.

## 1. Octet rule and types of bonds

**Kossel–Lewis approach (1916):** atoms gain, lose or share electrons to complete eight electrons in the valence shell (two for H, Li, Be in some cases).

| Bond | How formed | Typical between | Properties of compounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic (electrovalent) | Complete transfer of electrons | Metal + non-metal (EN difference > 1.7) | Hard crystalline solids, high m.p., conduct in molten/aqueous state, soluble in water |
| Covalent | Sharing of electron pairs | Non-metals | Low m.p. (except network solids), poor conductors, soluble in organic solvents |
| Coordinate (dative) | Shared pair comes from one atom only | Donor with lone pair + acceptor with empty orbital | Shown by arrow: H₃N→BF₃, NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, CO, O₃ |
| Metallic | Positive ions in a "sea" of delocalised electrons | Metals | Conduct, lustrous, malleable, ductile |

**Exceptions to the octet rule:**
- **Incomplete octet:** BeCl₂ (4 e⁻), BF₃, BCl₃, AlCl₃ (6 e⁻) — act as Lewis acids.
- **Odd-electron molecules:** NO, NO₂, ClO₂ (paramagnetic).
- **Expanded octet** (central atom in period 3 or below uses d orbitals): PCl₅ (10), SF₆ (12), IF₇ (14), H₂SO₄.
- Noble-gas compounds exist: XeF₂, XeF₄, XeF₆, XeO₃ (first one, Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻, by Neil Bartlett, 1962).

**Formal charge** = (valence electrons) − (lone-pair electrons) − ½(bonding electrons). In O₃ the central O has formal charge +1 and one terminal O has −1. The best Lewis structure has the smallest formal charges.

### Ionic bond and lattice energy

**Lattice enthalpy** = energy needed to separate one mole of an ionic solid into gaseous ions. It increases with higher ionic charges and smaller ions (lattice energy ∝ q₁q₂/r).
- NaF > NaCl > NaBr > NaI (anion size rises).
- MgO (2+, 2−) ≫ NaCl (1+, 1−): MgO melts at 2852 °C, NaCl at 801 °C.
- **Born–Haber cycle** calculates lattice energy from sublimation, ionisation, dissociation and electron-gain enthalpies (Hess's law).

### Fajans' rules — covalent character in ionic bonds

A small, highly charged cation **polarises** (distorts) a large anion, giving partial covalent character. Covalent character increases when:
1. Cation is **small** (LiCl more covalent than NaCl; BeCl₂ covalent).
2. Anion is **large** (LiI > LiBr > LiCl > LiF in covalent character).
3. Charge on either ion is **high** (AlCl₃ > MgCl₂ > NaCl).
4. Cation has an **18-electron (pseudo-noble-gas) shell** rather than an 8-electron shell (CuCl more covalent than NaCl although Cu⁺ and Na⁺ have similar size; AgCl is less soluble than NaCl).

More covalent character → lower melting point and lower solubility in water: NaCl (801 °C) > CuCl (430 °C).

## 2. Bond parameters

| Parameter | Meaning | Trend |
|---|---|---|
| Bond length | Distance between nuclei of bonded atoms | Single > double > triple: C–C 154, C=C 134, C≡C 120 pm |
| Bond enthalpy | Energy to break 1 mol of bonds (gas phase) | Triple > double > single; N≡N 946, O=O 498, H–H 436 kJ mol⁻¹ |
| Bond angle | Angle between bonds at the central atom | Decided by hybridisation and lone pairs |
| Bond order | Number of bonds between two atoms | Higher order → shorter and stronger bond |

**Halogen bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹):** Cl₂ 242 > Br₂ 192 > **F₂ 158** > I₂ 151. F₂ is unusually weak because lone pairs on the two small F atoms repel each other.

## 3. VSEPR theory and molecular shapes

**VSEPR (Sidgwick–Powell 1940, Nyholm–Gillespie 1957):** electron pairs (bonding and lone) around the central atom repel each other and take positions of minimum repulsion. Repulsion order: **lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair**. Lone pairs squeeze bond angles. Multiple bonds count as one "electron domain".

**Bond-angle orders to remember**
- CH₄ (109.5°) > NH₃ (107°) > H₂O (104.5°) — more lone pairs, smaller angle.
- NH₃ (107°) > PH₃ (93.6°) > AsH₃ (92°) > SbH₃ (91°); H₂O (104.5°) > H₂S (92°) — larger central atom, less hybridisation, angle near 90°.
- NH₃ (107°) > NF₃ (102°) — electronegative F pulls bond pairs away, less bp–bp repulsion.
- OF₂ (103°) < H₂O (104.5°) < Cl₂O (111°).
- NO₂⁺ (180°) > NO₂ (134°) > NO₂⁻ (115°).

## 4. Valence bond theory and hybridisation

**Valence bond theory (Heitler–London 1927, Pauling):** a covalent bond forms when half-filled orbitals overlap. **σ (sigma) bond** — head-on (axial) overlap, cylindrical symmetry, free rotation possible. **π (pi) bond** — sideways overlap of p orbitals above and below the axis, weaker, no free rotation (causes geometrical isomerism). The first bond between two atoms is always σ.

**Hybridisation:** mixing of atomic orbitals of similar energy to form equal new orbitals. Number of hybrid orbitals = number of orbitals mixed.

**Quick formula:** H = ½ (V + M − C + A), where V = valence electrons of central atom, M = monovalent atoms attached, C = positive charge, A = negative charge. (Divalent atoms like O add zero.) H = 2 sp, 3 sp², 4 sp³, 5 sp³d, 6 sp³d², 7 sp³d³.

| Hybridisation | s-character | Geometry | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| sp | 50% | Linear | BeCl₂, C₂H₂, CO₂, HCN |
| sp² | 33.3% | Trigonal planar | BF₃, C₂H₄, benzene, graphite, CO₃²⁻ |
| sp³ | 25% | Tetrahedral | CH₄, diamond, NH₃, H₂O, NH₄⁺ |
| sp³d | 20% | Trigonal bipyramidal | PCl₅, SF₄, ClF₃, XeF₂, I₃⁻ |
| sp³d² | 16.7% | Octahedral | SF₆, XeF₄, BrF₅, [CoF₆]³⁻ |
| sp³d³ | — | Pentagonal bipyramidal | IF₇, XeF₆ |
| dsp² | — | Square planar | [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻, [PtCl₄]²⁻ |

**Worked numerical 1.** Hybridisation of XeF₄, I₃⁻, NH₄⁺ and XeO₃.
XeF₄: ½(8 + 4) = 6 → **sp³d², square planar** (2 lone pairs). I₃⁻: ½(7 + 2 + 1) = 5 → **sp³d, linear** (3 lone pairs). NH₄⁺: ½(5 + 4 − 1) = 4 → **sp³, tetrahedral**. XeO₃: ½(8 + 0) = 4 → **sp³, pyramidal**.

> **Remember:** More s-character → shorter bond, more electronegative carbon and more acidic C–H. Electronegativity of carbon: sp > sp² > sp³. That is why ethyne (HC≡CH) is weakly acidic but ethane is not (chapter 11).

## 5. Counting σ and π bonds

Rule: single = 1σ; double = 1σ + 1π; triple = 1σ + 2π. Count every bond including C–H. Ring compounds: draw all bonds.

| Molecule | σ | π |
|---|---|---|
| Ethane C₂H₆ | 7 | 0 |
| Ethene C₂H₄ | 5 | 1 |
| Ethyne C₂H₂ | 3 | 2 |
| Benzene C₆H₆ | 12 | 3 |
| Naphthalene C₁₀H₈ | 19 | 5 |
| CO₂ | 2 | 2 |
| HCN | 2 | 2 |
| Cyanogen (CN)₂, N≡C–C≡N | 3 | 4 |
| Malononitrile CH₂(CN)₂ | 6 | 4 |
| Acrylonitrile CH₂=CH–C≡N | 6 | 3 |
| **Tetracyanoethylene (NC)₂C=C(CN)₂** | **9** | **9** |
| SO₃ (three S=O) | 3 | 3 (one pπ–pπ + two pπ–dπ in the classical picture) |
| **XeO₄ (four Xe=O)** | **4** | **4** (pπ–dπ) |
| H₂SO₄ | 6 | 2 |

**Worked numerical 2 — tetracyanoethylene, C₆N₄.**
Bonds: one C=C (1σ + 1π), four C–C single bonds from the central carbons to the CN groups (4σ), four C≡N (4σ + 8π).
σ = 1 + 4 + 4 = **9**; π = 1 + 8 = **9**.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "How many σ and π bonds are in tetracyanoethylene molecule? (A) 9σ and 9π (B) 5σ and 9π (C) 9σ and 7π". Official answer: **(A) 9σ and 9π**.

**Worked numerical 3 — equal σ and π?**
(CN)₂: N≡C–C≡N → σ = 3, π = 4 (not equal). CH₂(CN)₂: 2 C–H + 2 C–C + 2 C≡N(σ) = 6σ, π = 4 (not equal). XeO₄: 4 Xe=O → **4σ and 4π (equal)**.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "In which of the following species number of σ and π bonds is same? (A) (CN)₂ (B) CH₂(CN)₂ (C) XeO₄". Official answer: **(C) XeO₄**.

## 6. Polarity and dipole moment

A bond between atoms of different electronegativity is **polar**. **Dipole moment μ = charge × distance**; unit debye (D), 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. It is a vector; a molecule is polar only if the bond moments do not cancel.

| Zero dipole moment (symmetric) | Non-zero dipole moment |
|---|---|
| CO₂, BF₃, CH₄, CCl₄, BeCl₂, PCl₅, SF₆, XeF₄, p-dichlorobenzene, trans-1,2-dichloroethene | H₂O (1.85 D), NH₃ (1.47 D), NF₃ (0.23 D), HCl (1.03 D), CHCl₃ (1.04 D), SO₂, o- and m-dichlorobenzene, cis-isomers |

- **NH₃ (1.47 D) > NF₃ (0.23 D):** in NH₃ the lone-pair moment adds to the N–H bond moments; in NF₃ the N–F bond moments point away from N and oppose the lone-pair moment.
- **Chloromethanes:** CH₃Cl (1.86) > CH₂Cl₂ (1.60) > CHCl₃ (1.04) > CCl₄ (0).
- **Dichlorobenzenes:** ortho > meta > para (= 0).
- **Hydrogen halides:** HF > HCl > HBr > HI.

**Worked numerical 4 — percent ionic character.** HCl: μ(observed) = 1.03 D, bond length 1.27 Å. If fully ionic, μ = 4.8 D Å⁻¹ × 1.27 Å = 6.10 D (since e × 1 Å = 4.8 D). % ionic = 1.03/6.10 × 100 = **16.9%**.

## 7. Resonance

When one Lewis structure cannot describe a molecule, the real structure is a **resonance hybrid** of several canonical forms. The hybrid is more stable than any single form (resonance energy). Canonical forms differ only in electron positions, not atom positions.

- Benzene: all C–C bonds 139 pm (between single 154 and double 134); resonance energy about 150 kJ mol⁻¹.
- CO₃²⁻: three equal C–O bonds, bond order 4/3. NO₃⁻: bond order 4/3. O₃: 1.5. SO₄²⁻: equal S–O bonds.
- **Bond order in a resonance hybrid** = (total number of bonds between the central atom and the equivalent outer atoms) ÷ (number of such atoms). CO₃²⁻: 4 bonds shared over 3 C–O positions = 1.33. ClO₄⁻: 7/4 = 1.75.

## 8. Molecular orbital theory (MOT)

**Mulliken and Hund (1932):** atomic orbitals combine (LCAO) to form molecular orbitals belonging to the whole molecule. Combining n atomic orbitals gives n MOs: **bonding** (lower energy, electron density between nuclei) and **antibonding** (* — higher energy, a node between nuclei).

**Energy order:**
- For **O₂, F₂, Ne₂** (Z > 7): σ1s < σ*1s < σ2s < σ*2s < **σ2p_z < π2pₓ = π2p_y** < π*2pₓ = π*2p_y < σ*2p_z.
- For **H₂ to N₂** (total ≤ 14 electrons): σ2s < σ*2s < **π2pₓ = π2p_y < σ2p_z** < π* < σ* (s–p mixing raises σ2p_z).

## 9. Hydrogen bonding

A **hydrogen bond** is the attraction between an H atom bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (**F, O, N**) and a lone pair on another such atom: X–H···Y. It is weaker than covalent bonds (about 10–40 kJ mol⁻¹) but stronger than van der Waals forces.

**Strength order:** F–H···F > O–H···O > N–H···N (follows EN of F > O > N). The [HF₂]⁻ ion has a very strong H-bond (~160 kJ mol⁻¹), so KHF₂ exists but KHCl₂ does not.

| Type | Meaning | Examples | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intermolecular | Between different molecules | H₂O, HF, NH₃, alcohols, carboxylic acids (dimers), **p-nitrophenol** | Raises b.p., viscosity, solubility in water |
| Intramolecular (chelation) | Within the same molecule | **o-nitrophenol**, salicylaldehyde, o-hydroxybenzoic acid | Lowers b.p., more volatile, less soluble |

**Consequences**
- Abnormally high b.p.: H₂O (100 °C) vs H₂S (−60 °C). Among hydrogen halides, **b.p. order is HF (19.5 °C) > HI > HBr > HCl** — HF is high because of H-bonds, HCl is the lowest.
- Ice is less dense than water (open cage structure, each O tetrahedrally H-bonded); water has maximum density at 4 °C.
- Ethanol is miscible with water; ether is only slightly soluble.
- Alcohols boil higher than ethers of the same formula: ethanol (78 °C) vs dimethyl ether (−24 °C).
- DNA double helix and protein secondary structure are held by H-bonds.
- **Steam distillation** separates o-nitrophenol (volatile, intramolecular H-bond) from p-nitrophenol (chapter 10).

**Which compound has the strongest hydrogen bonding?** It must have an O–H, N–H or F–H. Diethyl ether (C₂H₅OC₂H₅) has no H on O, so it cannot H-bond with itself. Triethylamine ((C₂H₅)₃N) has no N–H. Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) has an O–H, and O is more electronegative than N.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Hydrogen bonding is strongest in (A) C₂H₅OC₂H₅ (B) (C₂H₅)₃N (C) C₂H₅OH". Official answer: **(C) C₂H₅OH**.

## 10. Weak intermolecular forces (van der Waals forces)

| Force | Between | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Dipole–dipole | Polar molecules | HCl···HCl |
| Ion–dipole | Ion and polar molecule | Na⁺ in water (hydration) |
| Dipole–induced dipole (Debye) | Polar + non-polar | HCl and Ar |
| London dispersion (instantaneous dipole) | All molecules, the only force in non-polar ones | I₂, noble gases; increases with molar mass |

Boiling points of noble gases and halogens rise down the group because dispersion forces grow with size: F₂ and Cl₂ gases, Br₂ liquid, I₂ solid.

**Metallic bond:** electron-sea model (Drude–Lorentz). Strength rises with number of valence electrons: Na (m.p. 98 °C) < Mg (650 °C) < Al (660 °C); transition metals are very hard (W m.p. 3422 °C, highest among metals).

## Traps and exceptions

- **Every single bond is σ**; count C–H bonds too. Tetracyanoethylene = 9σ, 9π.
- **XeO₄ has equal σ and π (4 and 4)**; (CN)₂ has 3σ, 4π.
- **NH₃ has a larger dipole moment than NF₃**, though N–F is more polar.
- **B₂ and O₂ are paramagnetic; C₂ and N₂ diamagnetic.** O₂²⁻ is diamagnetic, O₂⁻ is paramagnetic.
- **F₂ bond enthalpy is lower than Cl₂** (lone-pair repulsion).
- **H-bonding needs H on F, O or N.** Ethers, tertiary amines and CHCl₃ do not H-bond with themselves (CHCl₃ can weakly H-bond with acetone).
- o-Nitrophenol is **more volatile** than p-nitrophenol (intramolecular H-bond).
- XeF₂ is **linear**, not bent (3 lone pairs equatorial). XeF₄ is square planar, not tetrahedral.
- SF₄ is see-saw; CF₄ is tetrahedral.
- PCl₅ has two longer axial bonds (more repulsion) than equatorial ones.
- Bond order of C₂ is 2 but it has no σ bond in the simple MOT picture (both are π).
- The b.p. order of hydrogen halides is HF > HI > HBr > HCl (HCl lowest, not HF).
