This chapter explains why the periodic table is arranged the way it is and how properties change along a period and down a group. It covers NCERT class 10 ("Periodic Classification of Elements"), class 11 ("Classification of Elements and Periodicity") and B.Sc. points such as effective nuclear charge, second ionisation enthalpy and lanthanide contraction. TRE 2.0 asked the order of second ionisation potential of C, N, O, F and the largest atom among Be, Mg, Ra; TRE 1.0 asked which pair has similar atomic radii (Mo and W).

> **In simple words:** Going **left to right**, the nucleus gains protons but electrons go into the same shell, so the pull increases: atoms shrink, hold electrons tighter, become more non-metallic. Going **down** a group, a new shell is added each time: atoms grow, lose electrons easily, become more metallic. Almost every trend and every exception follows from this.

## 1. History of classification

| Scientist (year) | Idea | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Döbereiner (1817/1829) | **Triads**: middle element's atomic mass ≈ average of other two (Li, Na, K; Ca, Sr, Ba; Cl, Br, I) | Only a few triads found |
| Newlands (1865) | **Law of octaves**: every 8th element resembles the 1st (like musical notes) | Worked only up to calcium |
| Lothar Meyer (1869) | Plotted atomic volume vs atomic mass — periodic curve | — |
| **Mendeleev (1869)** | **Properties are periodic functions of atomic masses**; 63 elements; left gaps and predicted eka-aluminium (Ga), eka-silicon (Ge), eka-boron (Sc) | Position of hydrogen, isotopes, some inverted pairs (Co–Ni, Ar–K, Te–I) |
| **Moseley (1913)** | **Modern periodic law: properties are periodic functions of atomic numbers** (from X-ray frequencies, √ν ∝ Z) | — |

> **Remember:** Mendeleev's table had no place for noble gases at first (they were discovered later, 1894–1898) and they were added as a zero group without disturbing the table, which was a strength of his scheme.

## 2. The modern (long-form) periodic table

- **18 groups** (vertical columns) and **7 periods** (horizontal rows). 118 elements are named (the last four — Nh 113, Mc 115, Ts 117, Og 118 — were named in 2016).
- Elements in a period: 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32.
- Period number = number of shells; group number of s/p elements = valence electrons (s-block) or 10 + valence electrons (p-block).
- Lanthanoids (Z 58–71) and actinoids (Z 90–103) are placed separately at the bottom.

| Block | Groups | General configuration | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| s | 1, 2 | ns¹⁻² | Reactive metals (alkali, alkaline earth) |
| p | 13–18 | ns² np¹⁻⁶ | Metals, metalloids, non-metals, noble gases |
| d | 3–12 | (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns⁰⁻² | Transition metals |
| f | — (group 3 by many tables) | (n−2)f¹⁻¹⁴ (n−1)d⁰⁻¹ ns² | Inner-transition metals |

**Group names:** 1 alkali metals, 2 alkaline earth metals, 13 boron family, 14 carbon family, 15 pnictogens (nitrogen family), 16 chalcogens (ore-forming), 17 halogens (salt-forming), 18 noble gases.

**Metalloids** (properties between metals and non-metals): B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te (Po and At sometimes added).

**Special facts:** only liquid non-metal — bromine; liquid metals at room temperature — mercury (gallium and caesium melt just above room temperature: Ga 29.8 °C, Cs 28.4 °C). Hydrogen has no fixed place (resembles both group 1 and group 17).

### IUPAC names for elements with Z > 100

Digits: 0 nil, 1 un, 2 bi, 3 tri, 4 quad, 5 pent, 6 hex, 7 sept, 8 oct, 9 enn; add "-ium".

| Z | Temporary name | Symbol | Official name |

**Worked numerical 1.** Locate Z = 17, 26 and 56.
Z = 17: 2, 8, 7 → **period 3, group 17** (Cl). Z = 26: [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² → **period 4, group 8** (Fe; 6 + 2 = 8). Z = 56: [Xe] 6s² → **period 6, group 2** (Ba).

## 3. Effective nuclear charge and shielding

Inner electrons **shield** (screen) outer electrons from the full nuclear charge. **Effective nuclear charge** Z_eff = Z − σ (σ = shielding constant). Shielding order of sub-shells: s > p > d > f (f-electrons shield very poorly, which causes lanthanide contraction).

**Slater's rules (B.Sc., short form):** for an ns/np electron — other electrons in the same group (n) shield 0.35 each (0.30 for 1s), (n − 1) shell 0.85 each, (n − 2) and lower 1.00 each.

**Worked numerical 2.** Z_eff for a 2p electron of nitrogen (1s² 2s² 2p³)?
σ = 4 × 0.35 (other four n = 2 electrons) + 2 × 0.85 (1s) = 1.40 + 1.70 = 3.10 → Z_eff = 7 − 3.10 = **3.90**.

Z_eff increases across a period (by about 0.65 per element for s/p electrons) and stays nearly constant down a group.

## 4. Atomic radius

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Covalent radius | Half the distance between nuclei of two bonded atoms | Cl–Cl 198 pm → r = 99 pm |
| Metallic radius | Half the distance between neighbouring atoms in a metal crystal | Cu 128 pm |
| van der Waals radius | Half the distance between non-bonded atoms of neighbouring molecules — the largest | Cl 180 pm |

Order for the same element: **van der Waals > metallic > covalent**.

- **Across a period:** radius decreases (Z_eff rises). Li 152 > Be 112 > B 88 > C 77 > N 74 > O 66 > F 64 pm. Noble-gas radii are van der Waals values, so they look large — do not compare them directly.
- **Down a group:** radius increases (new shell). Li 152 < Na 186 < K 231 < Rb 244 < Cs 262 pm.
- **Exception 1 — gallium:** Ga (135 pm) is slightly **smaller** than Al (143 pm), because poorly shielding 3d electrons raise Z_eff.
- **Exception 2 — lanthanide contraction:** 4f electrons shield poorly, so 5d elements are nearly the **same size as the 4d elements above them**: Zr (160) ≈ Hf (159), Nb ≈ Ta, **Mo ≈ W**. Hence Zr and Hf are very hard to separate.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Which of the following has the largest size? (A) Mg (B) Be (C) Ra". Official answer: **(C) Ra** (group 2, size increases down the group: Be < Mg < Ca < Sr < Ba < Ra).

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) asked: "The pair that has similar atomic radii is (A) Mn and Re (B) Mo and W (C) Sc and Ni". Correct choice: **(B) Mo and W**, due to lanthanide contraction (4d and 5d members of group 6). Mn (3d) is clearly smaller than Re. (No official key in our folder.)

### Ionic radius

- **Cation is smaller** than its parent atom (fewer electrons, sometimes a whole shell lost): Na 186 pm → Na⁺ 102 pm.
- **Anion is larger** than its parent atom (more electron–electron repulsion): Cl 99 pm → Cl⁻ 181 pm.
- For the same element, more positive charge → smaller: Fe²⁺ (78) > Fe³⁺ (65 pm); Pb²⁺ > Pb⁴⁺.
- **Isoelectronic species** (same electrons): **more protons → smaller**.

| Isoelectronic series (10 e⁻) | N³⁻ | O²⁻ | F⁻ | Na⁺ | Mg²⁺ | Al³⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Z | 7 | 8 | 9 | 11 | 12 | 13 |
| Radius (pm) | 171 | 140 | 133 | 102 | 72 | 54 |

Order: **N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺**. Similarly S²⁻ > Cl⁻ > K⁺ > Ca²⁺ (18 e⁻).

## 5. Ionisation enthalpy (ionisation potential)

**First ionisation enthalpy (IE₁)** = energy needed to remove the most loosely held electron from an isolated **gaseous** atom: M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻. Always positive (endothermic). Unit kJ mol⁻¹ or eV per atom (1 eV per atom = 96.49 kJ mol⁻¹).

Successive ionisation enthalpies always increase: **IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃** (removing an electron from a positive ion is harder). A very large jump shows that a noble-gas core is being broken.

**Factors:** larger size → lower IE; higher nuclear charge → higher IE; more shielding → lower IE; stable half-filled or fully-filled configuration → higher IE; penetration s > p > d > f → s electrons are harder to remove.

**Trends:** IE increases across a period, decreases down a group. Highest IE of all: **He (2372 kJ mol⁻¹)**; lowest of stable elements: **Cs (376 kJ mol⁻¹)**.

Order: **Li < B < Be < C < O < N < F < Ne**.

Two exceptions (repeat for period 3: Na 496 < Al 577 < Mg 738 < Si 786 < S 1000 < P 1012 < Cl 1251 < Ar 1521):

1. **Be > B** (and Mg > Al): in B the electron comes from 2p, which is higher in energy and better shielded than 2s; Be's 2s² is fully filled.
2. **N > O** (and P > S): N has a stable half-filled 2p³; in O (2p⁴) the paired electron feels extra repulsion and leaves easily.

### Second ionisation enthalpy — the TRE 2.0 question

For IE₂ we remove an electron from the **cation** M⁺, so look at the configuration of M⁺, not of M.

| Element | Configuration of M⁺ | IE₂ (kJ mol⁻¹) | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | C⁺ = 2s² 2p¹ | 2353 | Easy to remove lone 2p electron |
| N | N⁺ = 2s² 2p² | 2856 | — |
| **O** | **O⁺ = 2s² 2p³ (half-filled)** | **3388** | Breaking a stable half-filled set — highest |
| F | F⁺ = 2s² 2p⁴ | 3374 | Paired electron, slightly easier than O⁺ |

Order of IE₂: **O > F > N > C**.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "The decreasing order of second ionization potential of C, N, O and F is (A) O > F > N > C (B) C > N > O > F (C) O > N > F > C". Official answer: **(A) O > F > N > C**.

> **Memory trick:** For second IE, "shift every element one step to the right": O⁺ behaves like N (half-filled), so O gets the "N-type bump". Same idea: IE₂ of Na is huge (Na⁺ is neon-like), IE₂ of Mg is small.

- **IE₂ of alkali metals** is very high (noble-gas core). Among Na, Mg, Al, IE₂ is highest for **Na**. IE₂ of Cr (Cr⁺ = 3d⁵) > IE₂ of Mn (Mn⁺ = 3d⁵4s¹); IE₂ of Cu (Cu⁺ = 3d¹⁰) is high.

**Worked numerical 3.** An element has IE₁ = 738, IE₂ = 1451, IE₃ = 7733 kJ mol⁻¹. Which group?
Big jump between IE₂ and IE₃ → it has **2 valence electrons → group 2** (it is Mg).

**Worked numerical 4.** IE of H is 13.6 eV per atom. Convert to kJ mol⁻¹. 13.6 × 96.49 ≈ **1312 kJ mol⁻¹**.

## 6. Electron gain enthalpy (electron affinity)

**Electron gain enthalpy (Δ_egH)** = enthalpy change when an electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g). Usually negative (energy released). **Electron affinity** is the same magnitude with opposite sign convention in older books.

- Becomes more negative across a period, less negative down a group — but with exceptions.
- **Cl has the most negative value of all elements (−349 kJ mol⁻¹), more than F (−328)**: the F atom is so small that the incoming electron faces strong repulsion in the compact 2p sub-shell. Order: **Cl > F > Br > I**.
- Similarly **S (−200) is more negative than O (−141)**; P more than N.
- **Positive (or nearly zero)** Δ_egH for: noble gases (stable ns²np⁶), Be and Mg (filled ns²), N (half-filled 2p³, ≈ 0 or slightly positive).
- **Second electron gain enthalpy is always positive**: O⁻ + e⁻ → O²⁻ needs energy (+780 kJ mol⁻¹) because the electron is added to a negative ion. O²⁻ still forms in solids because lattice energy pays for it.

## 7. Electronegativity

**Electronegativity (EN)** = tendency of an atom **in a molecule** to attract the shared electron pair. It is not measurable directly and has no unit (a relative scale).

| Scale | Basis |
|---|---|
| Pauling (1932) | Bond energies; F = 4.0 (most common in exams) |
| Mulliken | Average of IE and EA: χ = (IE + EA)/2 |
| Allred–Rochow | Electrostatic force Z_eff e²/r² |

Pauling values (classic): **F 4.0 > O 3.5 > N 3.0 ≈ Cl 3.0 > Br 2.8 > S 2.5 ≈ C 2.5 > I 2.5 > H 2.1** … Cs 0.7 (Fr 0.7). Revised modern values: F 3.98, O 3.44, Cl 3.16, N 3.04.

- Most electronegative: **F**. Least: **Cs/Fr**.
- EN increases across a period and decreases down a group.
- Uses: difference in EN > 1.7 → mainly ionic bond; polarity of bond; acidic/basic nature of oxides; predicting oxidation states.

## 8. Metallic character, valency and oxides

- **Metallic character** (tendency to lose electrons) decreases across a period, increases down a group. Most metallic: Cs (Fr). Most non-metallic: F.
- **Valency** with respect to H or O: across period 3, valency with O rises 1 → 7 (Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₃, Cl₂O₇); with H it goes 1, 2, 3, 4, 3, 2, 1.
- **Nature of oxides across period 3:** Na₂O (strongly basic) → MgO (basic) → Al₂O₃ (**amphoteric**) → SiO₂ (weakly acidic) → P₄O₁₀ → SO₃ → Cl₂O₇ (**most acidic**).

| Oxide type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Basic | Na₂O, K₂O, CaO, MgO, BaO |
| Acidic | CO₂, SO₂, SO₃, P₄O₁₀, N₂O₅, Cl₂O₇, Mn₂O₇ |
| Amphoteric (react with both acid and base) | Al₂O₃, ZnO, BeO, SnO, SnO₂, PbO, PbO₂, Cr₂O₃, Ga₂O₃ |
| Neutral | CO, NO, N₂O, H₂O |

For one element, **higher oxidation state → more acidic oxide**: MnO (basic) < Mn₂O₃ < MnO₂ (amphoteric) < Mn₂O₇ (acidic); CrO basic, Cr₂O₃ amphoteric, CrO₃ acidic.

### Diagonal relationship and other anomalies

- **Diagonal relationship:** Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si show similar properties because of similar charge/size ratio (polarising power). Li and Mg both form nitrides, normal oxides and decomposable carbonates; Be and Al both form amphoteric oxides and covalent chlorides.
- **Anomalous first members** (Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F): small size, high EN, no d orbitals, so maximum covalency 4 and strong pπ–pπ multiple bonds (N≡N, O=O, C=C).
- **Inert pair effect:** down groups 13–15, the ns² pair resists bonding, so the lower oxidation state becomes more stable: Tl⁺ > Tl³⁺, Pb²⁺ > Pb⁴⁺, Bi³⁺ > Bi⁵⁺. (So PbO₂ and NaBiO₃ are strong oxidising agents.)

### Periodicity of other properties (useful one-liners)

| Property | Pattern | Example values |
|---|---|---|
| Melting point across period 3 | Rises to Si (giant covalent), then falls sharply | Na 98 °C, Mg 650, Al 660, **Si 1414**, P 44, S 115, Cl −101, Ar −189 °C |
| Melting point down group 1 | Decreases (weaker metallic bond) | Li 181 → Cs 28.4 °C |
| Melting point down group 17 | Increases (stronger dispersion forces) | F₂ −220 → I₂ 114 °C |
| Density down a group | Generally increases | Os and Ir are the densest elements (~22.6 g cm⁻³) |
| Reactivity of metals | Increases down groups 1 and 2 | Cs reacts explosively with water |
| Reactivity of halogens | Decreases down group 17 | F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ |
| Hydride bond strength | Decreases down a group | HF > HCl > HBr > HI (so HI is the strongest acid) |

**Worked numerical 5 (Mulliken electronegativity).** For an atom with IE = 17.4 eV and EA = 3.6 eV (close to fluorine's values), χ(Mulliken) = (17.4 + 3.6)/2 = **10.5 eV**. Dividing by about 2.8 converts it roughly to the Pauling scale: 10.5/2.8 ≈ **3.8**, close to Pauling's 4.0 for F.

## 9. Summary of trends

| Property | Left → right (period) | Top → bottom (group) |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | Decreases | Increases |
| Ionisation enthalpy | Increases (exceptions: group 2 > 13, 15 > 16) | Decreases |
| Electron gain enthalpy (more negative) | Increases | Decreases (but Cl > F, S > O) |
| Electronegativity | Increases | Decreases |
| Metallic character | Decreases | Increases |
| Reducing power | Decreases | Increases |
| Basic nature of oxides | Decreases | Increases |

## Traps and exceptions

- **Be > B and N > O** in first IE; **Mg > Al, P > S** likewise.
- **Second IE: O > F > N > C** — look at the configuration of the cation.
- **Cl, not F, has the most negative electron gain enthalpy**; F is the most electronegative. Do not confuse the two properties.
- **Ga is smaller than Al.**
- **Mo ≈ W, Zr ≈ Hf, Nb ≈ Ta** (lanthanide contraction); Mn and Re are not similar.
- Noble gases have **positive** electron gain enthalpy and the highest IE in each period.
- Cation smaller, anion larger than parent atom.
- Mendeleev arranged by **atomic mass**; Moseley by **atomic number**.
- Second electron gain enthalpy is always endothermic.
- Al₂O₃, ZnO, BeO, SnO₂, PbO are amphoteric; CO, NO, N₂O are neutral, not acidic.

## Practice with answers
