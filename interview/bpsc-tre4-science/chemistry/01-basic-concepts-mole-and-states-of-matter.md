This chapter covers the language of chemistry: what matter is, how we count atoms (the mole), how we express concentration, and how gases and liquids behave. It runs from NCERT class 9 ("Matter in Our Surroundings", "Atoms and Molecules") through class 11 ("Some Basic Concepts", "States of Matter") to B.Sc. points such as van der Waals constants and molecular speeds. TRE asks these as one-line facts ("which is an intensive property?") and as 30-second numericals.

> **In simple words:** A mole is just a counting unit, like a dozen, but much bigger: 6.022 × 10²³ particles. Once you can turn grams into moles and moles into particles or litres, almost every basic numerical becomes two lines of arithmetic.

## 1. Matter and its classification

**Matter** is anything that has mass and occupies space. It exists in three common states (solid, liquid, gas); plasma and Bose–Einstein condensate are called the 4th and 5th states.

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Fixed | Takes shape of container | Takes shape of container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Fills the container |
| Intermolecular force | Strongest | Medium | Weakest |
| Compressibility | Negligible | Very small | High |
| Diffusion | Very slow | Moderate | Fastest |

**Classification by composition**

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Element | One kind of atom; cannot be broken down chemically | Fe, O₂, S₈, diamond |
| Compound | Two or more elements in a **fixed ratio by mass**; properties differ from its elements | H₂O, NaCl, CO₂ |
| Homogeneous mixture (solution) | Uniform composition throughout | Air, brass, sugar solution |
| Heterogeneous mixture | Non-uniform; visible boundaries | Sand + iron filings, oil + water |

**Physical change** — no new substance, usually reversible (melting of ice, dissolving sugar, magnetising iron). **Chemical change** — new substance forms, usually irreversible (rusting, burning, curdling of milk, digestion).

**Changes of state:** melting (s → l), boiling/evaporation (l → g), condensation (g → l), freezing (l → s), **sublimation** (s → g directly: camphor, naphthalene, iodine, NH₄Cl, dry ice), **deposition** (g → s).

- **Latent heat of fusion** of ice = 334 J g⁻¹ (≈ 80 cal g⁻¹). **Latent heat of vaporisation** of water = 2260 J g⁻¹ (≈ 540 cal g⁻¹). This is why steam at 100 °C burns more than water at 100 °C.
- **Evaporation** is a surface phenomenon at any temperature; it causes cooling. It increases with surface area, temperature and wind speed, and decreases with humidity.

### Intensive and extensive properties

An **intensive property** does not depend on the amount of substance; an **extensive property** does.

| Intensive (independent of amount) | Extensive (depends on amount) |
|---|---|
| Temperature, pressure, density | Mass, volume, number of moles |
| Refractive index, viscosity, surface tension | Enthalpy (H), internal energy (U) |
| Boiling point, melting point | Entropy (S), Gibbs energy (G) |
| Molarity, mole fraction, specific heat | Heat capacity, total charge |
| emf of a cell, pH, molar quantities (Hₘ, Sₘ) | Length, area |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Which of the following is an intensive property? (A) Refractive index (B) Volume (C) Mass." Official answer: **(A) Refractive index**.

> **Memory trick:** Divide one extensive property by another and you get an intensive one: mass/volume = density; heat capacity/mass = specific heat.

### Separating mixtures (class 9)

| Method | Separates | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Evaporation | Dissolved solid from liquid | Salt from sea water |
| Sublimation | Sublimable solid from non-sublimable | NH₄Cl from NaCl, camphor from salt |
| Centrifugation | Fine suspended particles by spinning | Cream from milk, blood tests |
| Separating funnel | Immiscible liquids | Oil and water, kerosene and water |
| Simple distillation | Liquid from dissolved solid, or liquids differing a lot in b.p. | Acetone + water |
| Fractional distillation | Miscible liquids with b.p. difference < 25 K | Petroleum fractions, gases of air |
| Chromatography | Components of a mixture by different adsorption/solubility | Dyes in ink, plant pigments |
| Crystallisation | Pure solid from impure solution | Pure CuSO₄, alum, sugar |
| Magnetic separation | Magnetic from non-magnetic | Iron filings from sulphur |

Steam distillation, vacuum distillation and chromatography types are taught in chapter 10 ([organic basics](/interview/bpsc-tre4-science/chemistry/organic-basics-iupac-isomerism-and-mechanisms)).

## 2. Laws of chemical combination

| Law | Statement in one line | Given by (year) |
|---|---|---|
| Conservation of mass | Mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction | Lavoisier (1789) |
| Definite (constant) proportions | A compound always contains the same elements in the same ratio by mass | Proust (1799) |
| Multiple proportions | When two elements form more than one compound, masses of one that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in small whole-number ratio | Dalton (1803) |
| Reciprocal proportions | Ratio of masses in which two elements combine with a third is related to how they combine with each other | Richter (1792) |
| Gaseous volumes | Gases combine in simple whole-number ratios by volume (same T, P) | Gay-Lussac (1808) |
| Avogadro's law | Equal volumes of all gases at same T and P contain equal numbers of molecules | Avogadro (1811) |

**Example of multiple proportions:** CO and CO₂ — 12 g carbon combines with 16 g and 32 g oxygen, ratio **1 : 2**. Similarly H₂O and H₂O₂ (16 : 32 = 1 : 2).

**Dalton's atomic theory (1808):** matter is made of indivisible atoms; atoms of one element are identical; atoms combine in small whole-number ratios; atoms are neither created nor destroyed. Later corrections: atoms are divisible (electron, proton, neutron) and isotopes mean atoms of one element can differ in mass.

## 3. Atomic mass, molecular mass and the mole

- **1 atomic mass unit (amu, now written u or Da)** = 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom = **1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg** (1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g).
- **Average atomic mass** accounts for isotopes. Chlorine: 75% ³⁵Cl + 25% ³⁷Cl → 0.75 × 35 + 0.25 × 37 = **35.5 u**.
- **Molecular mass** = sum of atomic masses. H₂SO₄ = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = **98 u**. **Formula mass** is used for ionic solids (NaCl = 58.5 u).
- **Mole:** amount of substance containing exactly **6.022 140 76 × 10²³** elementary entities (the Avogadro number, Nₐ). Since the 2019 SI redefinition, Nₐ is an exact fixed number.
- **Molar mass** = mass of 1 mole in grams (numerically equal to the molecular mass): H₂O = 18 g mol⁻¹.
- **Molar volume of a gas:** 22.4 L at STP (273 K, 1 atm); **22.7 L** at IUPAC STP (273.15 K, 1 bar); 24.8 L at SATP (298 K, 1 bar).

<div class="formula">

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Number of moles | n = mass / molar mass = N / Nₐ = V(gas at STP) / 22.4 L |
| Number of particles | N = n × 6.022 × 10²³ |
| Mass of one molecule | molar mass / Nₐ |

</div>

**Worked numerical 1.** How many molecules are in 36 g of water?
n = 36 / 18 = 2 mol. N = 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules**. (Atoms = 3 × that = 3.61 × 10²⁴.)

**Worked numerical 2.** Mass of one molecule of CO₂?
44 g / 6.022 × 10²³ = **7.31 × 10⁻²³ g**.

**Worked numerical 3.** Volume of 8 g of O₂ at STP?
n = 8 / 32 = 0.25 mol → 0.25 × 22.4 = **5.6 L**.

**Worked numerical 4.** Number of electrons in 1.6 g of CH₄?
n = 1.6/16 = 0.1 mol; each CH₄ has 6 + 4 = 10 electrons → 0.1 × 10 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **6.022 × 10²³ electrons** (1 mole of electrons).

> **Common mistake:** "One mole of oxygen" is ambiguous. One mole of O atoms weighs 16 g; one mole of O₂ molecules weighs 32 g. Read the question for "atoms" vs "molecules".

### Percentage composition, empirical and molecular formula

- **Percentage of element** = (mass of element in 1 mol / molar mass) × 100. Hydrogen in water = 2/18 × 100 = **11.1%**; oxygen = 88.9%.
- **Empirical formula** = simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. **Molecular formula** = n × empirical formula, where n = molar mass / empirical formula mass.

**Worked numerical 5.** A compound has C 40.0%, H 6.67%, O 53.3%; molar mass 180 g mol⁻¹. Find the formula.

| Element | % | ÷ atomic mass | ÷ smallest |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | 40.0 | 40.0/12 = 3.33 | 1 |
| H | 6.67 | 6.67/1 = 6.67 | 2 |
| O | 53.3 | 53.3/16 = 3.33 | 1 |

Empirical formula CH₂O (mass 30). n = 180/30 = 6 → **C₆H₁₂O₆** (glucose).

## 4. Stoichiometry and limiting reagent

A balanced equation gives the mole ratio. The **limiting reagent** is the reactant that is used up first and decides how much product forms; the other is **in excess**.

**Worked numerical 6.** 4 g H₂ reacts with 48 g O₂: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Which is limiting and how much water forms?
H₂ = 4/2 = 2 mol; O₂ = 48/32 = 1.5 mol. 2 mol H₂ needs only 1 mol O₂, so **H₂ is limiting**, 0.5 mol O₂ is left. Water = 2 mol = **36 g**.

**Worked numerical 7.** Mass of CaO from 50 g CaCO₃ (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂)?
Molar masses 100 and 56 → 50 × 56/100 = **28 g CaO**; CO₂ = 22 g (11.2 L at STP).

### Equivalent weight (still asked in old-style MCQs)

Equivalent weight = molar mass / n-factor. n-factor: acid = basicity (H₂SO₄: 2, so E = 49); base = acidity (Ca(OH)₂: 2, E = 37); ion = charge; oxidant/reductant = electrons gained/lost per formula unit (KMnO₄ in acid: 5, E = 158/5 = 31.6; in neutral: 3, E = 52.7; in strongly alkaline: 1).

## 5. Concentration terms

<div class="formula">

| Term | Definition | Unit | Depends on temperature? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass % (w/w) | mass of solute / mass of solution × 100 | % | No |
| Molarity (M) | moles of solute / volume of solution in L | mol L⁻¹ | **Yes** (volume changes) |
| Molality (m) | moles of solute / mass of solvent in kg | mol kg⁻¹ | No |
| Normality (N) | gram equivalents / volume in L | eq L⁻¹ | Yes |
| Mole fraction (x) | moles of component / total moles | none | No |
| ppm | mass of solute / mass of solution × 10⁶ | ppm | No |

</div>

Useful relations: N = M × n-factor; dilution M₁V₁ = M₂V₂; mixing M = (M₁V₁ + M₂V₂)/(V₁ + V₂); sum of mole fractions = 1.

**Worked numerical 8.** 4 g NaOH dissolved to make 250 mL solution. Molarity?
n = 4/40 = 0.1 mol; V = 0.25 L → M = 0.1/0.25 = **0.4 M**.

**Worked numerical 9.** 9.8 g H₂SO₄ in 500 g water. Molality?
n = 9.8/98 = 0.1 mol; m = 0.1/0.5 = **0.2 mol kg⁻¹**. Normality of the same acid if the solution were 500 mL: N = 2 × 0.2 = 0.4 N.

**Worked numerical 10.** 46 g ethanol mixed with 54 g water. Mole fraction of ethanol?
Ethanol 46/46 = 1 mol; water 54/18 = 3 mol → x = 1/4 = **0.25**.

**Worked numerical 11.** How much water must be added to 100 mL of 1 M HCl to make it 0.25 M?
M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ → V₂ = 1 × 100 / 0.25 = 400 mL → add **300 mL** water.

> **Exam angle:** "Which concentration term does not change with temperature?" → molality, mole fraction, mass %. Molarity and normality change because volume expands on heating.

## 6. The gas laws

**Pressure units:** 1 atm = 76 cm Hg = 760 mm Hg = 760 torr = 1.013 25 × 10⁵ Pa = 1.013 bar. 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa. **Absolute zero** = 0 K = −273.15 °C.

| Law | Statement | Formula | Constant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boyle (1662) | V ∝ 1/P | P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ | T, n |
| Charles (1787) | V ∝ T (kelvin) | V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ | P, n |
| Gay-Lussac (pressure law) | P ∝ T | P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ | V, n |
| Avogadro | V ∝ n | V₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂ | T, P |
| Ideal gas equation | combines all | PV = nRT | — |
| Dalton (partial pressures, 1801) | P(total) = p₁ + p₂ + … ; pᵢ = xᵢ P | — | T, V |
| Graham (diffusion, 1829) | rate ∝ 1/√(density) ∝ 1/√M | r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁) | T, P |

**Values of R:** 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 1.987 ≈ 2 cal K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 0.083 14 L bar K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

Other common plots: P vs 1/V is a straight line through origin (Boyle); PV vs P is a horizontal line for an ideal gas; V vs t (°C) is a straight line cutting the temperature axis at −273.15 °C.

**Worked numerical 12 (Boyle).** 3 L of gas at 2 atm is compressed to 6 atm at constant T. New volume = 2 × 3 / 6 = **1 L**.

**Worked numerical 13 (Charles).** 2 L at 27 °C heated to 177 °C at constant P. T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 450 K → V₂ = 2 × 450/300 = **3 L**.

**Worked numerical 14 (ideal gas).** Moles in 24.6 L at 1 atm and 300 K? n = PV/RT = 1 × 24.6 / (0.0821 × 300) = 24.6/24.63 ≈ **1 mol**.

**Worked numerical 15 (Graham).** Compare rates of diffusion of H₂ and O₂. r(H₂)/r(O₂) = √(32/2) = √16 = **4**. H₂ diffuses 4 times faster.

**Worked numerical 16 (Dalton).** A vessel has 2 mol N₂ and 3 mol O₂ at total pressure 10 atm. p(O₂) = (3/5) × 10 = **6 atm**.

**Gas density:** d = PM/RT. At STP, d = M/22.4 g L⁻¹ (O₂: 32/22.4 = 1.43 g L⁻¹). **Vapour density** = M/2, so M = 2 × VD.

## 7. Kinetic theory of gases

Postulates: gas is made of tiny particles in random motion; their own volume is negligible; no attractive forces; collisions are perfectly elastic; average kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature.

<div class="formula">

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Kinetic gas equation | PV = ⅓ m N u²(rms) |
| Average KE per mole | (3/2) RT |
| Average KE per molecule | (3/2) k T, k = R/Nₐ = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ |
| Root mean square speed | u(rms) = √(3RT/M) |
| Average speed | u(av) = √(8RT/πM) |
| Most probable speed | u(mp) = √(2RT/M) |
| Ratio u(mp) : u(av) : u(rms) | 1 : 1.128 : 1.224 (√2 : √(8/π) : √3) |

</div>

**Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution:** on heating, the curve flattens and its peak moves to higher speed; the area under the curve (total molecules) stays the same.

**Worked numerical 17.** Ratio of rms speeds of H₂ at 50 K and O₂ at 800 K? u ∝ √(T/M): √(50/2) : √(800/32) = √25 : √25 = **1 : 1**.

> **Remember:** Average kinetic energy depends only on temperature, not on the nature of the gas. At the same temperature, H₂ and CO₂ have the same average KE, but H₂ moves much faster.

## 8. Real gases

Real gases deviate from PV = nRT at **high pressure and low temperature**, because molecules have volume and attract each other.

- **Compressibility factor** Z = PV/nRT. Ideal gas: Z = 1. Z < 1 → attraction dominates (gas more compressible; e.g. CO₂, NH₃ at moderate P). Z > 1 → repulsion/volume dominates (H₂ and He at almost all pressures at room T).
- **van der Waals equation:** (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. **a** measures intermolecular attraction (units atm L² mol⁻²); **b** is the excluded volume (L mol⁻¹), b = 4 × actual volume of molecules in 1 mol.
- Easily liquefiable gases have **large a** (NH₃, SO₂, Cl₂). H₂ and He have very small a.
- **Boyle temperature** T_B = a/Rb: gas behaves ideally over a range of pressure.
- **Critical temperature** T_c = 8a/27Rb: above it a gas cannot be liquefied however high the pressure. T_c of CO₂ = 31.1 °C (304.2 K); P_c = 73 atm; V_c = 3b. Relations: T_c : T_B : T_i(inversion) = 1 : 3.375 : 6.75 (T_i = 2a/Rb).
- **Joule–Thomson effect:** expansion of a real gas through a porous plug causes cooling if the gas is below its inversion temperature. H₂ and He warm up on expansion at room temperature (inversion temperatures are low: H₂ about 200 K, He about 40 K).

## 9. Liquid state

| Property | Meaning | Effect of temperature | SI unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vapour pressure | Pressure of vapour in equilibrium with liquid | Increases | Pa |
| Boiling point | T at which vapour pressure = external pressure | — | K |
| Surface tension | Force per unit length on the surface | **Decreases** | N m⁻¹ |
| Viscosity | Resistance to flow (internal friction) | **Decreases** for liquids (increases for gases) | Pa s (= 10 poise) |

- At hills (low pressure) water boils below 100 °C; in a **pressure cooker** it boils above 100 °C (about 120 °C).
- **Normal boiling point** is at 1 atm (water 100 °C); **standard boiling point** at 1 bar (water 99.6 °C).
- Spherical drops, rise of liquid in capillary, cleaning action of soaps — due to surface tension. Soap/detergent lowers surface tension.
- Glycerol and honey are viscous due to extensive hydrogen bonding.

## Traps and exceptions

- **Refractive index, density, temperature, molarity, emf and boiling point are intensive.** Enthalpy, entropy, heat capacity and Gibbs energy are extensive (but molar enthalpy and specific heat are intensive).
- Molarity changes with temperature; **molality does not**.
- STP molar volume: 22.4 L (1 atm, old) versus 22.7 L (1 bar, IUPAC). Read which STP the question uses.
- Charles's law needs **kelvin**. Doubling the temperature from 10 °C to 20 °C does **not** double the volume.
- Dry ice (solid CO₂) sublimes at −78.5 °C at 1 atm; liquid CO₂ does not exist at 1 atm.
- **H₂ and He have Z > 1** at ordinary temperature; they warm on Joule–Thomson expansion at room temperature.
- Viscosity of a gas increases with temperature, opposite to liquids.
- Law of multiple proportions is Dalton's; law of definite proportions is Proust's — do not swap.
- Average KE depends only on T; rms speed depends on T and M.

## Practice with answers

1. Number of moles in 88 g of CO₂? — **2 mol** (88/44).
2. Volume of 2 g H₂ at STP? — **22.4 L** (1 mol).
3. Molarity of 5.85 g NaCl in 500 mL solution? — **0.2 M** (0.1 mol / 0.5 L).
4. Which is intensive: density or mass? — **Density** (independent of amount).
5. Which concentration term is unaffected by temperature: molarity or molality? — **Molality** (uses mass of solvent).
6. Who gave the law of definite proportions? — **Proust** (1799).
7. Ratio of masses of O combining with fixed C in CO and CO₂? — **1 : 2** (multiple proportions).
8. Gas that diffuses fastest: H₂, He or CH₄? — **H₂** (lowest molar mass).
9. Ratio of diffusion rates of CH₄ and SO₂? — **2 : 1** (√(64/16)).
10. Absolute zero in °C? — **−273.15 °C** (volume of ideal gas would become zero).
11. What happens to the Maxwell curve peak on heating? — **Shifts to higher speed and becomes lower** (area constant).
12. Compressibility factor of an ideal gas? — **1** (PV = nRT exactly).
13. Which constant of van der Waals equation shows attraction? — **a** (b shows molecular volume).
14. Gas with highest critical temperature among H₂, N₂, NH₃? — **NH₃** (strongest attraction, largest a).
15. Empirical formula of benzene (C₆H₆)? — **CH** (simplest ratio 1 : 1).
16. Mass of 0.5 mol of H₂SO₄? — **49 g** (0.5 × 98).
17. Equivalent weight of KMnO₄ in acidic medium? — **31.6** (158/5).
18. Partial pressure of O₂ in air at 1 atm if mole fraction is 0.21? — **0.21 atm** (pᵢ = xᵢP).
19. Why does water boil below 100 °C on a mountain? — **Lower atmospheric pressure** (vapour pressure equals it at a lower T).
20. Unit of viscosity coefficient in SI? — **Pa s (N s m⁻²)** (1 Pa s = 10 poise).
