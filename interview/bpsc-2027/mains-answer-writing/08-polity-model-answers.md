GS Paper II section 1 is Indian Polity "with special reference to Bihar". Questions test whether you know the **constitutional provision**, the **working** of the institution, its **problems**, and **reforms** — and whether you can show how it works in Bihar. This chapter gives a polity toolkit and nine full model answers. Content comes from the [Indian Polity](/interview/bpsc-2027/polity) book and the [Bihar Special](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special) chapter on Bihar's administration.

> **In simple words:** In a polity answer, quote the **Article**, name a **judgment or committee**, explain how it **works in practice**, and give **one Bihar example**. That combination scores.

## Polity answer toolkit

| Question type | Body framework |
|---|---|
| Constitutional institution (Governor, ECI) | Provision (Articles) → Composition/appointment → Powers → Issues → Committee/court views → Reforms |
| Rights (FR, RTI) | Provision → Scope → Judicial interpretation → Limits → Bihar example |
| Local government | Amendment → Structure → Functions, funds, functionaries → Bihar Act features → Issues → Way forward |
| Federalism | Constitutional scheme → Tensions → Cooperative mechanisms → Bihar's view → Way forward |

> **Memory trick:** **A-J-C-B** — **A**rticle, **J**udgment, **C**ommittee, **B**ihar. Try to include all four in every long polity answer.

All questions below are **practice questions in BPSC style**, not claimed previous-year questions. Current office holders and recent court cases change — mark them "check latest" in your notes.

## Model answer 1 — Panchayati Raj in Bihar

**Practice question:** *Discuss the main features of the Bihar Panchayat Raj Act, 2006. How far has it strengthened grassroots democracy?* (about 600 words)

**Introduction**

The **73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** (in force from **24 April 1993**, now National Panchayati Raj Day) gave panchayats constitutional status through **Part IX (Articles 243–243O)** and the **Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects)**. Bihar enacted the **Bihar Panchayat Raj Act, 2006**, which went beyond the national minimum in several ways.

**Structure**

**Main features of the 2006 Act**

1. **Three tiers:** Gram Panchayat, Panchayat Samiti and Zila Parishad, elected every **five years**.
2. **50% reservation for women** in seats and in posts of heads at all levels — Bihar was the **first state** to do this.
3. **Reservation for SCs, STs** in proportion to population, and for **Extremely Backward Classes (EBCs)**.
4. **Gram Kachahari:** an elected village court headed by a **Sarpanch**, which settles minor civil and criminal disputes locally, with legal support from a **Nyaya Mitra**.
5. **Gram Sabha** of all voters of the panchayat, which approves plans and selects beneficiaries.
6. **Standing committees** at each level for planning, health, education, social justice and works.
7. Elections conducted by the **State Election Commission** (Art 243K); finances reviewed by the **State Finance Commission** (Art 243I).

**How far has it strengthened grassroots democracy?**

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Large number of women, SC, ST and EBC representatives — social democracy at the base | Proxy rule by husbands of women heads ("mukhiya-pati") in some places |
| Gram Kachahari reduces small cases reaching courts | Limited devolution of the **3 Fs** — funds, functions, functionaries |
| Panchayats implement schemes like rural roads, drains and water supply under Saat Nischay | Dependence on state and central grants; little own revenue |
| Gram Sabha gives citizens a forum | Low attendance in Gram Sabha meetings |
| Women's leadership linked with Jeevika SHGs | Capacity and training gaps; record-keeping problems |

**Way forward**

- Transfer real control over the 29 Eleventh Schedule subjects with matching funds.
- Train elected representatives, especially first-time women heads.
- Strengthen own-source revenue (property tax, fees).
- Social audits and e-governance for transparency.
- Strict action against proxy representation.

**Conclusion**

The Bihar Panchayat Raj Act, 2006 made Bihar a pioneer of inclusive local democracy, especially for women. The next step is to turn representation into real power by devolving funds, functions and functionaries.

> **Why this scores:** constitutional base (Part IX, Art 243I, 243K, 11th Schedule) plus Bihar Act features; a structure diagram; "how far" answered with a two-sided table; specific Bihar terms (Gram Kachahari, Nyaya Mitra, mukhiya-pati); way forward uses the "3 Fs" — a well-known framework.

## Model answer 2 — The Governor

**Practice question:** *"The office of the Governor has often been a point of friction in Centre–state relations." Examine.* (about 600 words)

**Introduction**

The **Governor** is the constitutional head of a state (**Article 153**), appointed by the **President (Art 155)** and holding office **during the pleasure of the President (Art 156)**. The Governor is meant to be a link between the Centre and the state, but in practice the office has often caused controversy.

**Powers of the Governor**

| Power | Article |
|---|---|
| Executive head; appoints CM and ministers | 154, 164 |
| Acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, except in discretion | 163 |
| Summons, prorogues, dissolves the assembly | 174 |
| Assent to bills; may withhold, return, or reserve for the President | 200, 201 |
| Ordinances when the legislature is not in session | 213 |
| Report on breakdown of constitutional machinery | 356 |
| Pardon for state offences | 161 |

**Areas of friction**

1. **Appointment of the Chief Minister** in a hung assembly — choice of the party invited first.
2. **Reports for President's Rule (Art 356)** — alleged misuse to dismiss state governments.
3. **Delay in assent to bills** — keeping bills pending for long periods.
4. **Dissolution of the assembly** without allowing a floor test.
5. **Role as university chancellor** — clashes over vice-chancellor appointments.
6. **Political background** of appointees and removal when the central government changes.

**Bihar example**

In **2005**, the Bihar assembly was dissolved on the Governor's report before it could meet. In **Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)**, the Supreme Court held the dissolution **unconstitutional**, saying the Governor's report was based on assumptions. It is a leading case on the limits of the Governor's power.

**Judicial and committee views**

- **S.R. Bommai (1994):** majority must be tested on the floor of the House; President's Rule is subject to judicial review.
- **B.P. Singhal (2010):** the President's "pleasure" cannot be used arbitrarily to remove Governors.
- **Nabam Rebia (2016):** Governor's discretion is limited; cannot act on his own in summoning the House against the advice of ministers in normal circumstances.
- On delays in assent, the Supreme Court has given important rulings and an advisory opinion in 2025 (check the latest position).
- **Sarkaria Commission (1983–88):** Governor should be an eminent person from outside the state and not active in politics; consult the CM before appointment.
- **Punchhi Commission (2007–10):** fixed tenure of five years; removal only through a process; time limit for assent decisions.

**Way forward**

- Follow Sarkaria and Punchhi recommendations on appointment and tenure.
- Clear conventions on inviting the largest party/coalition and on floor tests.
- Time-bound decisions on bills.
- Use Art 356 only as a "last resort", as Dr B.R. Ambedkar hoped it would remain a "dead letter".

**Conclusion**

The Governor is meant to be a constitutional guardian, not a political agent. Clear conventions and the courts' guidance can restore the office's neutrality and strengthen cooperative federalism.

> **Why this scores:** articles in a table; six friction points; a strong Bihar case (Rameshwar Prasad, 2006); three judgments and two commissions — A-J-C-B fully covered; a process diagram; changing case law marked "check latest".

## Model answer 3 — Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

**Practice question:** *Explain the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy. Give examples from Bihar.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

**Fundamental Rights (Part III, Arts 12–35)** are justiciable rights of individuals; **Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP, Part IV, Arts 36–51)** are non-justiciable guidelines for the state to build a welfare state (Art 37). Their relationship has changed through court judgments.

**Evolution of the relationship**

| Phase | Case / amendment | Position |
|---|---|---|
| 1951 | **Champakam Dorairajan** | FRs prevail over DPSP |
| 1951 | **First Amendment** | Added Arts 31A, 31B and the **Ninth Schedule** to protect land reform laws |
| 1967 | **Golaknath** | Parliament cannot amend FRs |
| 1973 | **Kesavananda Bharati** | Parliament can amend but not the **basic structure** |
| 1976 | **42nd Amendment** | Gave primacy to all DPSPs over Arts 14 and 19 (Art 31C) |
| 1980 | **Minerva Mills** | Harmony and balance between FR and DPSP is part of the basic structure |

**Present view:** FRs and DPSPs are **complementary** — DPSPs give direction, FRs set limits; courts read DPSPs into FRs (e.g. right to education, health and livelihood under Art 21).

**Bihar examples**

1. **Land reforms:** Bihar's zamindari abolition law was challenged in the **Kameshwar Singh** case (Patna High Court, 1951). This led directly to the **First Amendment (1951)** and the Ninth Schedule.
2. **Art 47 (prohibition):** Bihar enforced total prohibition under the **Bihar Prohibition and Excise Act, 2016**.
3. **Art 40 (village panchayats):** Bihar Panchayat Raj Act, 2006.
4. **Art 39A (free legal aid):** the **Bhagalpur blinding case** (Khatri v. State of Bihar, 1981) — free legal aid recognised as part of Art 21.
5. **Art 21A (education)** and Art 45 — Bihar's school enrolment schemes.

**Conclusion**

FRs and DPSPs are two wheels of the same chariot. Bihar's history — from land reforms to prohibition — shows how DPSPs shape state policy within the limits of rights.

> **Why this scores:** a table of the case-law evolution; five Bihar examples linked to specific articles — one of them (Kameshwar Singh) explains a national amendment; neat conclusion.

## Model answer 4 — Right to public services and grievance redressal

**Practice question:** *How have the Bihar Right to Public Services Act, 2011 and the Bihar Right to Public Grievance Redressal Act, 2015 improved governance?* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

Good governance means services delivered on time, transparently and with accountability. Bihar passed the **Bihar Right to Public Services (RTPS) Act, 2011** and the **Bihar Lok Shikayat Nivaran Adhikar Adhiniyam, 2015** (Right to Public Grievance Redressal) to give citizens legal rights to services and to complaint resolution.

**RTPS Act, 2011**

- Notified services (caste, income and residence certificates, licences, etc.) must be delivered within a **fixed time**.
- **Designated public servants** are responsible; **appeals** lie to higher officers.
- **Penalties** on officials for delay without reason.
- Applications through RTPS counters and online (check latest portal).

**Grievance Redressal Act, 2015 (in force from 2016)**

- Right to have a grievance **heard and decided** within a fixed time (generally 60 working days).
- Public Grievance Redressal Officers at sub-division and district levels.
- **Hearing** in person or by video; appeal and penalty provisions.

**Improvements**

1. Shift from "favour" to "right" — citizens can demand services.
2. Fewer middlemen; reduced petty corruption.
3. Time-bound delivery measured by data.
4. Complaints about schemes, land and pensions resolved closer to home.

**Limitations**

Digital divide; staff shortage; quality not only speed; awareness among rural poor; penalties rarely imposed.

**National link**

These laws build on the **RTI Act 2005**, **citizen charters**, and the **Sevottam** model recommended by the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission**.

**Conclusion**

Bihar's service and grievance laws are a model of rights-based governance. Better awareness, staffing and digital access can make them reach every citizen.

> **Why this scores:** two Bihar laws with correct names and years; features, improvements and limits clearly separated; national link with RTI and 2nd ARC.

## Model answer 5 — Public Interest Litigation

**Practice question:** *Discuss the role of Public Interest Litigation (PIL) in protecting the rights of the poor. Refer to cases from Bihar.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

**Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** allows any public-spirited person to approach the Supreme Court (**Art 32**) or a High Court (**Art 226**) on behalf of those who cannot do so themselves. It relaxed the rule of **locus standi** (only the affected person can sue). Justices **P.N. Bhagwati** and **V.R. Krishna Iyer** shaped it in the late 1970s and 1980s.

**Bihar's role in the birth of PIL**

| Case | Year | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar** | 1979 | Thousands of undertrials in Bihar jails, many held longer than the maximum sentence, were released; **speedy trial** declared part of Art 21 |
| **Khatri v. State of Bihar (Bhagalpur blindings)** | 1981 | Free legal aid at the state's cost recognised as part of Art 21 |

**Contribution of PIL**

1. Rights of undertrials, bonded labourers and prisoners.
2. Environment — Ganga pollution cases (M.C. Mehta).
3. Workplace sexual harassment guidelines (**Vishaka, 1997**).
4. Right to food — mid-day meals and PDS orders (PUCL case).
5. Accountability of governments on health and welfare.

**Concerns**

- **Misuse** for publicity or private interest.
- **Judicial overreach** into executive policy.
- Burden on court time.

The Supreme Court has laid down guidelines to filter frivolous PILs (e.g. in **State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal, 2010**).

**Conclusion**

PIL, born partly out of Bihar's jail and police abuses, gave the poor access to justice. Used with restraint, it remains a powerful tool of social justice.

> **Why this scores:** definition with articles; two landmark Bihar cases with years and outcomes in a table; contributions and concerns balanced; a guideline case shows depth.

## Model answer 6 — Anti-defection law

**Practice question:** *Critically examine the working of the anti-defection law in India.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

The **Tenth Schedule**, added by the **52nd Amendment Act, 1985**, disqualifies legislators who defect from their party. It was meant to stop "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" politics of frequent party-switching.

**Main provisions**

- Disqualification if a member **voluntarily gives up** party membership or **votes against the party whip**.
- Independent members are disqualified if they join a party; nominated members if they join a party after six months.
- **Merger exception:** no disqualification if **two-thirds** of the legislature party agree to merge with another party.
- The **91st Amendment (2003)** removed the "split" (one-third) exception and limited the size of the Council of Ministers to **15%** of the House.
- The **Speaker/Chairman** decides disqualification.

**Positives**

1. Greater stability of governments.
2. Party discipline and accountability to party manifesto.
3. Reduced horse-trading.

**Problems**

1. **Speaker's delay or bias** in deciding petitions.
2. **Mass "mergers"** used to escape the law.
3. Limits a legislator's freedom to vote by conscience on every issue.
4. **Resignations** used to topple governments and re-contest.

**Court and committee views**

- **Kihoto Hollohan (1992):** upheld the law; Speaker's decision is subject to judicial review.
- **Keisham Meghachandra Singh (2020):** Speakers should decide petitions within about three months.
- **Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)** and the Law Commission suggested decisions by the President/Governor on the Election Commission's advice.

**Way forward:** time limit for decisions; an independent tribunal or the Election Commission to decide; whip limited to confidence and money bills.

**Conclusion**

The anti-defection law brought stability but created new loopholes. Reforms that make decisions fast and neutral will restore its purpose.

> **Why this scores:** "critically examine" → provisions, positives, problems, views, reforms; amendments and judgments correctly dated; practical way forward.

## Model answer 7 — Election Commission and electoral reforms

**Practice question:** *Discuss the role of the Election Commission of India in strengthening democracy and suggest electoral reforms.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

The **Election Commission of India (ECI)** under **Article 324** superintends, directs and controls elections to Parliament, state legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President. Elections to panchayats and municipalities are conducted by **State Election Commissions** (Art 243K, 243ZA).

**Role in strengthening democracy**

1. **Free and fair elections** across the world's largest electorate.
2. **Model Code of Conduct** during elections.
3. **Technology:** EVMs, **VVPAT** (paper trail), online voter services.
4. **Voter awareness:** SVEEP programme; **NOTA** option (after the PUCL judgment, 2013).
5. **Expenditure monitoring** and checks on money and muscle power.
6. **Electoral rolls:** regular and special revisions. Bihar saw a **Special Intensive Revision** of electoral rolls in 2025 before the assembly elections, which was examined by the Supreme Court (check the latest outcome).

**Issues**

- Appointment process of Election Commissioners — governed by the **Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners Act, 2023**.
- Criminalisation of politics.
- High election spending; opaque political funding — the **electoral bond scheme** was struck down by the Supreme Court in **February 2024**.
- Fake news and social media misuse.

**Reforms suggested**

- Debar candidates facing serious charges (after charges are framed).
- State funding of elections (Indrajit Gupta Committee, 1998).
- Transparency in party funding.
- Common electoral roll; "One Nation, One Election" debate (check latest).
- More power to the ECI to deregister parties.

**Conclusion**

The ECI has made Indian elections a global model. Reforms on money, crime and transparency will deepen the quality of democracy.

> **Why this scores:** article basis; six roles including a Bihar example; issues with correct recent laws and judgments; reforms with a committee name; balanced.

## Model answer 8 — Bicameral legislature in Bihar

**Practice question:** *Examine the composition and role of the Bihar Legislative Council. Is a second chamber useful at the state level?* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

Bihar is one of the few states with a **bicameral legislature**: the **Bihar Vidhan Sabha** (Legislative Assembly, **243** members) and the **Bihar Vidhan Parishad** (Legislative Council, **75** members). Under **Article 169**, Parliament can create or abolish a Council if the state assembly passes a resolution by a special majority.

**Composition of a Legislative Council (Art 171)**

| Share of members | Elected / chosen by |
|---|---|
| One-third | Members of the Legislative Assembly |
| One-third | Local bodies (municipalities, district boards, etc.) |
| One-twelfth | Graduates of three years' standing |
| One-twelfth | Teachers of secondary schools and above |
| One-sixth (about) | **Nominated by the Governor** — persons with knowledge of literature, science, art, cooperative movement, social service |

Size: not more than **one-third** of the assembly's strength and not less than **40**. Members serve **six years**; one-third retire every two years — it is a **permanent house**.

**Role**

1. Second look at bills; may delay ordinary bills (up to **three months** first time, one month second time) but cannot block them.
2. **Money bills** — only 14 days to recommend; assembly may ignore.
3. Represents graduates, teachers and local bodies.
4. Brings experienced and expert persons into law-making.

**Debate**

| For a second chamber | Against |
|---|---|
| Revising chamber prevents hasty laws | Can only delay; assembly prevails |
| Represents educated and expert groups | Cost to the exchequer |
| Accommodates leaders who do not contest direct elections | Used to accommodate defeated politicians |
| Continuity — permanent house | Graduates/teachers no longer a special group |

**Conclusion**

The Council's value depends on how it is used. If it focuses on careful review and expert debate, as intended, it adds quality to Bihar's law-making.

> **Why this scores:** exact constitutional details (Arts 169, 171, fractions, six-year term); Bihar seat numbers; both sides of the debate in a table; balanced conclusion.

## Model answer 9 — Fiscal federalism and Bihar

**Practice question:** *Discuss the issues in Centre–state financial relations with special reference to Bihar.* (about 600 words)

**Introduction**

India's Constitution gives the Centre most of the buoyant taxes but gives states most of the spending responsibilities (health, education, police, agriculture). This **vertical imbalance** is corrected by transfers recommended by the **Finance Commission (Art 280)**. For a low-income state like **Bihar**, central transfers are a large part of its revenue.

- **Tax devolution:** the **14th Finance Commission** raised states' share to **42%**; the **15th** recommended **41%** (after J&K became a UT). The **16th Finance Commission** covers 2026–31 (check its final recommendations and Bihar's share).
- **Grants-in-aid (Art 275)**, revenue deficit grants, local body grants.
- **Centrally sponsored schemes**, where states share costs.
- **GST compensation** to states (2017–22) under the **101st Amendment (2016)**; the **GST Council (Art 279A)** decides rates jointly.

**Issues**

1. **Vertical imbalance:** states spend most but raise less.
2. **Cesses and surcharges** are not shared with states, reducing the divisible pool.
3. **Loss of fiscal autonomy** after GST — states cannot change rates alone.
4. **Conditional schemes** reduce states' choice in spending.
5. **Borrowing limits** under FRBM rules set by the Centre.
6. **Horizontal criteria** — weights for population, area, income distance, demographic performance, forest cover — affect each state's share.

**Bihar's concerns**

- Bihar's per-capita income is the **lowest among major states** (check the latest Bihar Economic Survey), so it relies on the **income distance** criterion, which gives poorer states a higher share.
- **Special Category Status** demand: Bihar argues that loss of mineral-rich areas to Jharkhand (2000), floods and poverty justify special help. The Centre has said the 14th Finance Commission removed the distinction between general and special category states for devolution; special assistance and packages have been given instead (check latest).
- Bihar's **own tax revenue** fell after prohibition (2016) removed excise income from liquor.
- Need for funds for **flood control** (Kosi, Gandak) — an inter-state and international (Nepal) issue.

**Cooperative mechanisms**

**NITI Aayog** (2015, replaced the Planning Commission), the **Inter-State Council (Art 263)**, zonal councils (Bihar is in the **Eastern Zonal Council**), and the GST Council.

**Way forward**

- Include cesses in the divisible pool or cap them.
- More untied funds; flexibility in centrally sponsored schemes.
- Special support for **backward regions and disaster-prone states**.
- States must raise own revenue — property tax, mineral and non-tax revenue — and improve spending quality.

**Conclusion**

Fiscal federalism works when transfers reduce inequality between states without reducing their autonomy. For Bihar, fair devolution together with better own-revenue efforts is the path to catch up with richer states.

> **Why this scores:** clear concept (vertical imbalance); diagram of transfer channels; correct Finance Commission figures, articles and amendment; a full Bihar section with special category status explained neutrally; changing data marked "check latest".

### Mains questions

**1. Practice question: "Discuss the role of the Bihar Public Service Commission in ensuring a merit-based civil service."** (about 300 words)

Outline:
- *Intro:* State PSCs under Arts 315–323; BPSC conducts recruitment for state services.
- *Body:* functions (Art 320) — exams, advice on promotion, disciplinary matters; independence (removal only by President, Art 317); challenges — delays in exam cycles, litigation, paper leak concerns, need for technology; reforms — fixed calendar, computer-based tests, Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act 2024 at the national level (check latest state law).
- *Conclusion:* a credible PSC is the base of good administration.

**2. Practice question: "Urban local bodies in Bihar are weak in finances and capacity." Discuss.** (about 300 words)

Outline:
- *Intro:* 74th Amendment 1992, Part IXA, 12th Schedule (18 subjects).
- *Body:* Bihar's low urbanisation (~11.3%, Census 2011); municipal corporations, councils, nagar panchayats; 50% women's reservation in ULBs; weak property tax, staff shortage, waterlogging in Patna, solid waste; reforms — municipal cadre, digitised property tax, AMRUT and Smart City (Patna, Muzaffarpur, Bhagalpur, Biharsharif selected).
- *Conclusion:* strong cities are needed for jobs and to reduce migration.

**3. Practice question: "Examine the significance of the Right to Information Act, 2005."** (about 300 words)

Outline:
- *Intro:* RTI under Art 19(1)(a); Act of 2005.
- *Body:* 30-day reply, 48 hours for life and liberty; Information Commissions; exemptions (Sec 8); impact on transparency; concerns — pendency, attacks on activists, 2019 amendment on tenure/salary, DPDP Act 2023 change to personal information exemption; Bihar — "Jaankari" call-centre facility for RTI (check latest).
- *Conclusion:* RTI is the citizen's tool of accountability.
