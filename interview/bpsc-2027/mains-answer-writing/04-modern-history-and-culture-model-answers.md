GS Paper I section 1 covers modern Indian history from the mid-19th century, Bihar's role in the freedom struggle, and Indian art, culture and literature. This chapter gives you a toolkit for history answers and then ten full model answers. Read each one, then close the book and write your own version in the time shown. For the content behind these answers, study the [History](/interview/bpsc-2027/history) and [Bihar Special](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special) books.

> **In simple words:** History answers need three things: the right **order** (background → event → impact), the right **names and dates**, and a clear **"so what"** (significance). Always add what happened **in Bihar**.

## How to use the model answers

1. Read the practice question and **plan your own answer for one minute** before reading the model.
2. Read the model answer. Mark the facts you did not know.
3. Read the "why this scores" note.
4. Next day, write the answer yourself from memory in the time limit. Compare.

All questions below are **practice questions written in BPSC style**. They are not claimed to be previous-year questions.

## History answer toolkit

| Question type | Body framework |
|---|---|
| A revolt or movement | Background/causes → Course (key events, places, leaders) → Bihar's role → Why it ended/failed → Significance |
| A personality | Early life (1 line) → Ideas → Work/actions → Contribution → Legacy |
| An organisation | Formation → Aims → Methods → Achievements → Limits |
| Compare two phases | Table: aims, methods, leaders, social base, achievements, limits |
| Art or culture form | Origin → Features (themes, colours, technique) → Styles/schools → Famous artists → Present status, GI, challenges |
| Literature | Period/context → Key writers with works → Themes → Social impact |

> **Mains angle:** History questions often say "Discuss the role of Bihar in…". Give Bihar its **own sub-heading** with 3–5 points: places, leaders, events, numbers (if sure).

> **Memory trick:** **B-C-B-S** — **B**ackground, **C**ourse, **B**ihar, **S**ignificance. It fits almost every movement question.

## Model answer 1 — The Revolt of 1857 in Bihar

**Practice question:** *Discuss the course of the Revolt of 1857 in Bihar and assess the role of Kunwar Singh.* (about 600 words)

**Introduction**

The Revolt of 1857 began at Meerut on 10 May 1857 and spread across north India. In Bihar, it took the form of both a soldiers' mutiny and a popular rebellion led by local landlords. Its most famous leader was **Babu Kunwar Singh** of **Jagdishpur** (in present Bhojpur district), who fought at around 80 years of age.

**Background in Bihar**

- **Economic distress:** the Permanent Settlement (1793) and high revenue demands ruined many peasants and small zamindars.
- **Loss of local power:** old landed families such as Kunwar Singh's estate were in debt and lost the support of the Company administration.
- **Religious fears:** activities of missionaries and rumours about greased cartridges spread among soldiers.
- **Wahabi network in Patna** created anti-British feeling among some sections; the Patna Commissioner **William Tayler** acted harshly against suspected rebels.

**Course of the revolt in Bihar**

1. **Patna (July 1857):** **Peer Ali**, a bookseller of Patna, organised a rising; he was captured and hanged on Tayler's orders. **Waris Ali** in Tirhut was also executed.
2. **Danapur mutiny (25 July 1857):** three native infantry regiments at Danapur rose in revolt and marched towards Arrah, where Kunwar Singh took command.
3. **Siege of Arrah:** the rebels besieged a small fortified house where Europeans and Sikh soldiers took shelter. A relief force under **Major Vincent Eyre** ended the siege in early August and took Jagdishpur.
4. **Wider campaign:** Kunwar Singh moved across central India and UP — Rewa, Banda, Kalpi and Kanpur — and captured **Azamgarh** in 1858.
5. **Return to Jagdishpur:** while crossing the Ganga he was wounded in the arm and is said to have cut off the injured arm himself. On **23 April 1858** he defeated the British force near Jagdishpur (celebrated as **Vijayotsav** in Bihar). He died on **26 April 1858**.
6. **After him:** his brother **Amar Singh** continued guerrilla fighting for some months.
7. **Other centres:** risings also took place at Gaya, Rajgir and in the Chotanagpur region (then part of the wider Bihar region), where leaders like Thakur Vishwanath Shahdeo and Pandey Ganpat Rai fought.

**Assessment of Kunwar Singh's role**

| Strength | Detail |
|---|---|
| Leadership at old age | Gave the revolt a respected leader in western Bihar |
| Guerrilla tactics | Mobile warfare, surprise attacks; fought over a wide area |
| Unity | Soldiers, zamindars and peasants fought together |
| Symbol | Remains a symbol of courage in Bihar's folk songs and memory |

| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| Local base | Could not build a wide organisation across Bihar |
| Weapons and resources | Rebels lacked modern arms and money |
| No common plan | Like the revolt as a whole, no national command |

**Bihar's significance in 1857**

Bihar was one of the main areas of sustained resistance outside Delhi, Awadh and central India. Kunwar Singh's campaign tied down British troops for almost a year.

**Conclusion**

The revolt in Bihar was crushed, but it showed that ordinary people and local leaders could challenge Company rule. Kunwar Singh became a lasting symbol of resistance, and his memory inspired later generations of nationalists in Bihar.

> **Why this scores:** covers both parts — "course" and "assess the role"; a timeline diagram; precise names (Peer Ali, Danapur, Arrah, Vincent Eyre, Azamgarh), dates (25 July 1857, 23 and 26 April 1858); assessment table with strengths and limits; balanced conclusion.

## Model answer 2 — Socio-religious reform movements

**Practice question:** *Examine the contribution of socio-religious reform movements of the 19th century to the making of modern India.* (about 600 words)

**Introduction**

In the 19th century, educated Indians exposed to Western ideas and proud of their own traditions began to question social evils such as sati, child marriage, caste discrimination and the denial of education to women. Their reform movements prepared the social ground for nationalism.

**Major movements**

| Movement | Founder, year | Main ideas and work |
|---|---|---|
| Brahmo Samaj | Raja Rammohan Roy, 1828 | One God, against idol worship and sati; supported the abolition of sati (1829) |
| Prarthana Samaj | Atmaram Pandurang, 1867 (Bombay) | Theism, inter-caste dining, women's education (M.G. Ranade) |
| Satyashodhak Samaj | Jyotiba Phule, 1873 | Against caste oppression; book *Gulamgiri* |
| Arya Samaj | Dayananda Saraswati, 1875 (Bombay) | "Back to the Vedas", *Satyarth Prakash*, against idol worship and untouchability; DAV schools |
| Theosophical Society | Blavatsky and Olcott, 1875 (New York); Adyar 1882 | Revival of ancient religions; Annie Besant later |
| Ramakrishna Mission | Swami Vivekananda, 1897 | Service to humanity; Vivekananda at Chicago Parliament of Religions, 1893 |
| Aligarh Movement | Sir Syed Ahmad Khan | Modern education for Muslims; M.A.O. College, Aligarh, 1875 |

**Contributions to modern India**

1. **Social reform laws:** abolition of sati (1829), **Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act 1856** (Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar's campaign), later the Age of Consent Act (1891).
2. **Women's education:** schools for girls; Phule and Savitribai Phule's work in Pune.
3. **Attack on caste and untouchability:** Phule, Arya Samaj, later Narayana Guru in Kerala.
4. **Rational thinking:** reason and conscience over blind ritual; respect for science.
5. **Modern education:** DAV institutions, Aligarh college, missionary and private schools.
6. **Self-confidence and national pride:** Vivekananda and Dayananda gave Indians pride in their culture — this fed later nationalism.
7. **Press and public opinion:** reformers used newspapers and public meetings, building a public sphere.

**Bihar dimension**

- **Patna College (1863)** became a centre of modern education in Bihar.
- **Bihar Bandhu** (from 1872) is regarded as Bihar's first Hindi newspaper; it spread reform and political ideas.
- Arya Samaj and Brahmo ideas spread among Bihar's educated middle class, who later led the movement for a separate Bihar province.

**Limitations**

- Mostly **urban and upper-caste/middle-class**; little reach among peasants.
- Some movements were **religion-specific**, and later revivalist strands sharpened community identities.
- Reforms in law moved faster than change in social practice.

**Conclusion**

The reform movements gave India modern ideas of equality, reason and dignity. By creating educated, confident Indians, they laid the social foundation on which the national movement was built.

> **Why this scores:** "examine contribution" is met with a table plus seven contribution points; Acts with years; a Bihar dimension even though the question is India-level; limitations show balance.

## Model answer 3 — Moderates and Extremists

**Practice question:** *Distinguish between the Moderates and the Extremists in the Indian National Congress.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

The Indian National Congress (INC), founded in **1885** at Bombay with **W.C. Bonnerjee** as its first president, was led by the **Moderates** till about 1905. The **Extremists** (or assertive nationalists) rose during the Swadeshi movement against the **Partition of Bengal (1905)**. The two groups split at the **Surat session (1907)**.

**Comparison**

| Basis | Moderates (about 1885–1905) | Extremists (about 1905–1920) |
|---|---|---|
| Leaders | Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjea, G.K. Gokhale | Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh |
| Goal | Reforms within British rule; more Indians in councils and services | Swaraj — "Swaraj is my birthright" (Tilak) |
| Methods | Petitions, speeches, resolutions ("prayer, petition, protest") | Boycott, Swadeshi, national education, mass mobilisation |
| Faith in British justice | Yes | No |
| Social base | Educated urban middle class | Wider — students, lower middle class, some masses |
| Main achievement | **Economic critique** — Naoroji's drain theory (*Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*); Indian Councils Act 1892 | Made Swaraj the goal; mass participation; self-reliance |
| Limitation | Little mass base | Lack of organisation after leaders were jailed; religious symbols sometimes alienated some groups |

**Bihar link**

Bihar's leaders such as **Sachchidananda Sinha** and **Mazharul Haque** worked within the Congress mainstream; the INC held its **1912 session at Bankipore (Patna)**.

**Conclusion**

The two groups were complementary: Moderates built the intellectual case against colonial rule; Extremists gave it mass energy. They reunited at Lucknow in **1916**, and Gandhi later combined both strengths.

> **Why this scores:** "Distinguish" is best answered with a table — seven criteria in little space; dates (1885, 1905, 1907, 1916); a one-line Bihar link; balanced conclusion.

## Model answer 4 — The movement for a separate Bihar

**Practice question:** *Trace the movement for the creation of a separate province of Bihar.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

Until 1912, Bihar was part of the **Bengal Presidency**. Educated Biharis felt that Bengal-based administration ignored Bihar in jobs, education and development. Their movement led to the creation of the **Bihar and Orissa province** in **1912**.

**Causes**

1. **Under-representation** of Biharis in government jobs, which were mostly held by people from Bengal.
2. **Neglect** of Bihar's education and development by the Calcutta administration.
3. **Rise of an educated middle class** in Patna and other towns.
4. **Language and identity:** demand for Hindi/Urdu in courts and offices and a sense of Bihari identity.

**Stages of the movement**

- **Press:** the **Bihar Times** (1894), started by **Sachchidananda Sinha** and **Mahesh Narayan**, raised the slogan "**Bihar for Biharis**".
- **Organisations and meetings:** student conferences and the Bihar Provincial Conference (from 1908) built public opinion.
- **Leaders:** Sachchidananda Sinha, Mahesh Narayan, **Ali Imam**, **Hasan Imam**, **Mazharul Haque**, Deep Narayan Singh and others; Hindus and Muslims worked together.
- **Partition of Bengal (1905)** and its annulment opened the question of reorganising Bengal.
- **Delhi Durbar (12 December 1911):** the annulment of the partition and the creation of a new province were announced.
- **22 March 1912:** **Bihar and Orissa** became a separate province — now celebrated as **Bihar Diwas**.

**Later changes:** Orissa became a separate province in **1936**; Jharkhand was carved out in **2000**.

The movement showed the power of an educated middle class using the press and constitutional methods. It gave Bihar its own administration, university (Patna University, 1917) and high court (Patna High Court, 1916), and a stronger regional identity.

> **Why this scores:** "Trace" is met with stages in order and a timeline diagram; key leaders and the newspaper named; date of Bihar Diwas; a "later changes" line shows completeness.

## Model answer 5 — Non-Cooperation and Khilafat in Bihar

**Practice question:** *Describe the course of the Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Movement in Bihar.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

The **Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22)**, launched by Gandhi and joined with the **Khilafat Movement**, was the first nationwide mass movement. Bihar, where Gandhi had worked in Champaran in 1917, responded strongly.

**Background**

The **Rowlatt Act (1919)**, the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre (13 April 1919)** and the Khilafat issue created anger. The Congress approved non-cooperation at the Calcutta special session (September 1920) and Nagpur session (December 1920).

**Course in Bihar**

1. **Boycott of courts and colleges:** **Rajendra Prasad** gave up his legal practice; many students left government colleges.
2. **National education:** **Bihar Vidyapith** was inaugurated by Gandhi in **February 1921** at Patna, with Mazharul Haque as its chancellor and Rajendra Prasad closely associated; national schools opened in districts.
3. **Sadaqat Ashram:** founded by **Mazharul Haque** at Patna; it became the headquarters of the Bihar Congress. His paper *The Motherland* supported the movement.
4. **Hindu–Muslim unity:** Khilafat leaders and Congress workers held joint meetings across Bihar.
5. **Swadeshi and charkha:** burning of foreign cloth, khadi production, picketing of liquor shops.
6. **Tilak Swaraj Fund** and Congress membership drives were successful in Bihar.

**End of the movement**

After the violence at **Chauri Chaura (February 1922)**, Gandhi withdrew the movement. Many Bihar workers were disappointed but turned to constructive work.

**Conclusion**

The movement made the Congress a mass organisation in Bihar, built institutions like Bihar Vidyapith and Sadaqat Ashram (both still present in Patna), and trained the leaders who led Bihar in the 1930s and 1940s.

> **Why this scores:** background in two lines; six numbered points with names, institutions and a date; clear end; the conclusion links institutions to today, which examiners appreciate.

## Model answer 6 — Quit India Movement in Bihar

**Practice question:** *"Bihar was one of the main centres of the Quit India Movement." Discuss.* (about 600 words)

**Introduction**

On **8 August 1942** the Congress passed the Quit India resolution at Bombay, and Gandhi gave the call "**Do or Die**". All top leaders were arrested the next morning. Without leaders, the people took the movement into their own hands. Bihar, along with eastern UP, saw some of the most intense resistance in India.

**Causes**

- Failure of the **Cripps Mission (March–April 1942)**.
- Fear of a Japanese invasion and anger at wartime price rise and shortages.
- Desire for immediate freedom.

1. **Patna Secretariat martyrs (11 August 1942):** students tried to hoist the national flag on the Secretariat building. Police firing killed **seven students** — among them Umakant Prasad Sinha, Ramanand Singh, Satish Prasad Jha, Jagatpati Kumar, Devipada Choudhry, Rajendra Singh and Ramgovind Singh. The **Shaheed Smarak (Martyrs' Memorial)** at Patna, sculpted by **Devi Prasad Roy Chowdhury**, honours them.
2. **Attack on symbols of authority:** railway stations, post offices, police stations and telegraph lines were attacked across north and central Bihar. Rail and telegraph links between Bihar and other provinces were cut for days in many areas.
3. **Rural uprisings:** peasants joined in many districts — Saran, Champaran, Muzaffarpur, Darbhanga, Bhagalpur, Monghyr, Patna and Shahabad. In **Siwan**, **Phulena Prasad Srivastava** was killed while leading a crowd to hoist the flag at Maharajganj police station; his wife **Tara Rani Srivastava** carried on.
4. **Underground movement:** **Jayaprakash Narayan** and companions (including Yogendra Shukla and Suraj Narayan Singh) escaped from **Hazaribagh Central Jail** on the night of Diwali, **9 November 1942**. JP organised the **Azad Dasta** (guerrilla squads) from the Nepal border area.
5. **Repression:** the government used the army, aerial firing at some places, collective fines and mass arrests.

**Why Bihar was a major centre**

- Strong Congress and Kisan Sabha networks built since Champaran and the 1930s.
- Active students and socialists (Congress Socialist Party, JP).
- Agrarian distress and wartime hardship.

**Significance**

- Showed that the **mass of Indians**, not only leaders, wanted freedom immediately.
- The British realised they could not rule India against such public anger after the war.
- Produced leaders like JP who shaped post-independence politics.

**Limitations**

- Movement was crushed within a few months; lack of central coordination.
- Some violence went against Gandhian non-violence.

**Conclusion**

The Quit India Movement in Bihar combined student sacrifice, peasant uprisings and an underground struggle. It made Bihar one of the strongest centres of the "last mass movement" before freedom, and its memory lives on in the Shaheed Smarak and the story of JP's escape.

> **Why this scores:** "Discuss" with a quoted statement needs evidence and a verdict; four strands in a hub diagram; precise date (11 August 1942), names of martyrs, the sculptor, JP's escape date; a "why Bihar" block directly answers the statement; limitations show balance.

## Model answer 7 — The Kisan Sabha movement

**Practice question:** *Evaluate the role of Swami Sahajanand Saraswati in the peasant movement of Bihar.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

**Swami Sahajanand Saraswati** (1889–1950) was the foremost peasant leader of Bihar. He turned scattered peasant anger against zamindars into an organised movement that demanded the **abolition of zamindari**.

**Role and work**

1. **Organisation:** he founded the **Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha** at **Sonepur in 1929**.
2. **All India Kisan Sabha:** formed at **Lucknow in April 1936** with Sahajanand as its first president (N.G. Ranga was a leading founder).
3. **Demands:** abolition of zamindari, end of illegal cesses (abwabs), security of tenure, reduction of rent, restoration of bakasht land.
4. **Bakasht struggle (1937–39):** tenants fought to recover *bakasht* lands (land taken back by zamindars for their "own cultivation"). Leaders like **Karyanand Sharma** (Barahiya Tal) and **Yadunandan Sharma** (Reora, Gaya) led local struggles.
5. **Writings:** his autobiography *Mera Jivan Sangharsh* and works on peasants' condition spread awareness.
6. **Pressure on Congress:** he pushed the Congress to include agrarian demands; differences with Congress ministries (1937–39) showed the class question within nationalism.

**Assessment**

| Achievements | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Built a mass peasant organisation | Divided at times between Congress and left groups |
| Made zamindari abolition a national demand | Landless labourers' issues got less attention |
| Brought peasants into the freedom struggle | Gains during British rule were limited |

**Outcome:** after independence, Bihar became one of the first states to pass a zamindari abolition law — the **Bihar Land Reforms Act, 1950**.

**Conclusion**

Sahajanand gave Bihar's peasants a voice and an organisation. The abolition of zamindari after 1947 was, in large part, the fruit of the movement he led.

> **Why this scores:** "Evaluate" needs achievements and limits — the table does this; dates (1929, 1936), places (Sonepur, Reora, Barahiya), terms (bakasht) explained; the outcome links the movement to post-1947 land reform.

## Model answer 8 — Women in the national movement

**Practice question:** *Discuss the role of women in India's freedom struggle with examples from Bihar.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

Women joined the freedom struggle in growing numbers from the Swadeshi movement onward. Gandhi's non-violent methods made it easier for women to take part in large numbers.

**Phases and roles**

1. **Swadeshi (1905–08):** boycott of foreign goods at home, support to processions.
2. **Non-Cooperation (1920–22):** spinning, khadi, picketing of liquor and foreign cloth shops.
3. **Civil Disobedience (1930–34):** salt-making, picketing, going to jail — **Sarojini Naidu** led the Dharasana salt raid after Gandhi's arrest.
4. **Quit India (1942):** **Aruna Asaf Ali** hoisted the flag at Gowalia Tank, Bombay; **Usha Mehta** ran the underground Congress Radio; **Matangini Hazra** was killed in Bengal.
5. **Revolutionary and INA roles:** Pritilata Waddedar, Bina Das; the **Rani of Jhansi Regiment** of the INA under **Lakshmi Sahgal (Swaminathan)**.

**Examples from Bihar**

- **Kasturba Gandhi** ran a school and worked among women in Champaran (Bhitiharwa) during 1917.
- **Prabhavati Devi**, wife of Jayaprakash Narayan, was a committed Gandhian worker and was jailed.
- **Tara Rani Srivastava** of Siwan continued the march after her husband Phulena Prasad was shot in 1942.
- Women in Bihar's towns and villages picketed shops and joined prabhat pheris.

**Significance**

- Freedom struggle became a social movement; women's public role grew.
- Prepared the ground for equal rights for women in the Constitution (Articles 14, 15, universal adult franchise).

**Conclusion**

Women were not only supporters but leaders and martyrs of the struggle. Their participation, including in Bihar, broke social barriers and laid the base for women's rights in independent India.

> **Why this scores:** organised by phase; national names plus four Bihar examples; significance links to the Constitution; complete in 300 words.

## Model answer 9 — Madhubani (Mithila) painting

**Practice question:** *Describe the main features of Madhubani painting and discuss the challenges it faces today.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

**Madhubani** or **Mithila painting** is a folk art of the Mithila region of north Bihar (Madhubani, Darbhanga and nearby districts). Traditionally drawn by women on walls and floors of homes for rituals and weddings, it is now famous worldwide and has a **GI (Geographical Indication) tag**.

**Origin and spread**

- Linked in legend to the wedding of Sita, daughter of King Janaka of Mithila.
- Came to wider notice after the **1934 earthquake**, when British officer **W.G. Archer** saw paintings on damaged walls.
- During the drought of the late 1960s, the All India Handicrafts Board encouraged women to paint on **paper**, which turned it into a source of income.

**Features**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Themes | Gods and goddesses (Krishna, Durga, Shiva-Parvati), nature, fish, birds, sun and moon, wedding scenes (Kohbar) |
| Style | Bold outlines, no empty space — gaps filled with flowers, lines and patterns; double outlines |
| Colours | Traditionally natural: turmeric, lamp black, indigo, flowers, cow dung |
| Tools | Fingers, twigs, matchsticks, nib pens |
| Styles | **Bharni** (filled colour), **Kachni** (fine line work), **Tantrik**, **Godna** (tattoo-like), **Kohbar** (marriage chamber) |

**Recognition**

Many Madhubani artists have received the **Padma Shri**, beginning with **Jagdamba Devi** (1975); others include Sita Devi, Baua Devi and Dulari Devi. Madhubani railway station walls painted by local artists drew national attention.

**Challenges**

1. **Middlemen** take most of the profit; artists earn little.
2. **Copying and machine prints** reduce the value of original work.
3. **Chemical colours** replacing natural ones affect authenticity.
4. **Young people leaving** the art for other jobs.
5. Weak **marketing and branding**.

**Way forward:** direct online sales and cooperatives, enforcement of the GI tag, training and design support, art tourism in Mithila villages.

**Conclusion**

Madhubani painting is a living record of Mithila's culture and women's creativity. Protecting artists' incomes is the best way to protect the art.

> **Why this scores:** both parts — features and challenges — get equal space; table of features with named styles; recognition with a safe, verifiable fact; practical way forward.

## Model answer 10 — Bihar's contribution to modern literature

**Practice question:** *Discuss the contribution of writers from Bihar to modern Hindi and regional literature.* (about 300 words)

**Introduction**

Bihar has a long literary tradition from **Vidyapati** (Maithili, 14th–15th century) onward. In the modern period, writers from Bihar shaped Hindi poetry, the regional ("aanchalik") novel and folk theatre in Bhojpuri and Maithili.

**Major writers and works**

| Writer | Language | Key works / contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Ramdhari Singh "Dinkar"** | Hindi | *Rashmirathi*, *Kurukshetra*, *Urvashi* (Jnanpith Award, 1972), *Sanskriti ke Char Adhyay*; called "Rashtrakavi" for his nationalist poetry |
| **Phanishwar Nath "Renu"** | Hindi | *Maila Anchal* (1954) — pioneer of the aanchalik (regional) novel; *Parti Parikatha*; story *Mare Gaye Gulfam* (film *Teesri Kasam*) |
| **Nagarjuna** (Vaidyanath Mishra, "Yatri" in Maithili) | Hindi, Maithili | People's poet; Sahitya Akademi Award for Maithili collection *Patrahin Nagna Gachh* |
| **Bhikhari Thakur** | Bhojpuri | Plays *Bidesiya*, *Beti Bechwa*, *Gabarghichor* on migration, girl-selling, women's suffering; called the "Shakespeare of Bhojpuri" |
| **Ramvriksh Benipuri** | Hindi | Sketches and essays; freedom fighter |
| **Shivpujan Sahay** | Hindi | Novel *Dehati Duniya*; editor and prose writer |

**Themes**

1. **Nationalism and courage** — Dinkar's poetry inspired the freedom movement and after.
2. **Rural life and social reality** — Renu and Nagarjuna wrote about poverty, caste and politics in villages.
3. **Migration and women's pain** — Bhikhari Thakur's *Bidesiya* on men leaving for Calcutta and Assam.
4. **Social reform** — plays against dowry and selling of daughters.

**Significance**

- Brought regional life into mainstream Hindi literature.
- Gave Bhojpuri and Maithili literary dignity; **Maithili** was added to the **Eighth Schedule** in **2003** (92nd Amendment).

**Conclusion**

Writers from Bihar gave modern Indian literature its nationalist voice and its village realism. Their works remain a guide to Bihar's society and culture.

> **Why this scores:** a table delivers many names and works quickly; themes show analysis, not just a list; the Eighth Schedule fact links literature to polity.

### Mains questions

**1. Practice question: "Champaran Satyagraha was a turning point for both Gandhi and Bihar." Comment.** (about 300 words)

Outline:
- *Intro:* 1917, first satyagraha in India, indigo peasants.
- *Body:* tinkathia, tawan, sharahbeshi; Rajkumar Shukla; Gandhi's defiance of the order; evidence of peasants recorded; Champaran Agrarian Committee and Act 1918; rise of Rajendra Prasad, Anugrah Narayan Sinha, Brajkishore Prasad; constructive work (Bhitiharwa school).
- *Conclusion:* Gandhi became a mass leader; Bihar became a centre of Gandhian politics.

**2. Practice question: "Discuss the role of Jayaprakash Narayan in the national movement."** (about 300 words)

Outline:
- *Intro:* socialist leader from Bihar (born Sitab Diara, 1902).
- *Body:* Congress Socialist Party (1934) founder member; Quit India — Hazaribagh escape, Azad Dasta; ideas of socialism and democracy; later Bhoodan and the 1974 movement (one line, beyond scope).
- *Conclusion:* "Lok Nayak" — symbol of youth and courage.

**3. Practice question: "Explain the features of Indian temple architecture styles (Nagara, Dravida, Vesara)."** (about 300 words)

Outline:
- *Intro:* three main styles named in texts.
- *Body:* table — region, shikhara/vimana, gopuram, examples (Nagara: Khajuraho, Konark; Dravida: Brihadeshwara, Thanjavur; Vesara: Hoysala temples, Belur, Halebidu); Bihar link: Mahabodhi Temple, Bodh Gaya (UNESCO site, 2002) — brick temple of the Gupta-era tradition.
- *Conclusion:* temples as centres of art, economy, and culture.
