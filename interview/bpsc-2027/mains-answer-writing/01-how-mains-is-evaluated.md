This chapter explains what happens to your answer booklet after the exam and what the person marking it is looking for. Once you know how marks are given, you can write to earn them. Everything in the later chapters (structure, Bihar angle, model answers) is built on these rules.

> **In simple words:** An examiner reads hundreds of booklets. Each answer gets about one or two minutes. Marks go to answers that are relevant, well organised, full of correct facts, linked to Bihar, and complete. A long, unorganised answer scores less than a short, well-built one.

## Who checks your answer and how

Your booklet is coded so the examiner does not know your name. Examiners are usually subject teachers (college and university faculty). They mark against the question, often with guidance from a head examiner so that marking stays similar across examiners.

Think about the examiner's day:

1. They have a large pile of booklets and limited time.
2. They read the **question**, then look at your **first few lines** to see whether you understood it.
3. They **scan** the body: headings, underlined key words, a diagram, numbers.
4. They read your **conclusion** to see whether you finished the thought.
5. They give a mark and move on.

So the examiner forms an opinion quickly. Your job is to make the good parts **visible**: a clear opening line, headings, short points, key words underlined, one diagram or table, and a proper ending.

> **In simple words:** Write so that a tired reader can see in 30 seconds that you answered the question fully.

## What earns marks: the six things examiners look for

| # | Quality | What it means | How to show it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Relevance** | You answered what was asked, all parts of it | Underline the directive word ("discuss", "evaluate") and every part of the question before writing |
| 2 | **Structure** | Intro → body with sub-headings → conclusion | Headings, numbered points, logical order |
| 3 | **Content** | Correct facts, names, dates, articles, data | Specific facts beat general statements |
| 4 | **Depth / analysis** | Causes, effects, both sides, your judgement | "Because…", "As a result…", "However…" |
| 5 | **Bihar angle** | Link to Bihar where the syllabus asks for it | One Bihar paragraph or 2–3 Bihar points in every answer where it fits |
| 6 | **Presentation** | Neat handwriting, spacing, diagrams | Leave a line between points; one simple diagram |

Content and relevance carry the most weight. Presentation cannot save a wrong answer, but it can lift a correct answer from average to good.

> **Mains angle:** BPSC GS papers have a syllabus that explicitly says "with special reference to Bihar" for many lines. An answer on Panchayati Raj that never mentions the Bihar Panchayat Raj Act 2006 or 50% reservation for women in Bihar's panchayats is missing marks the examiner expects to give.

### Understanding the directive word

The directive word tells you what kind of answer the examiner wants. Read it twice.

| Directive | What to do | Typical structure |
|---|---|---|
| **Describe** | Give features, what happened | Points in order (time or theme) |
| **Discuss** | Explain the topic from several sides | Features + causes + effects + views |
| **Examine** | Look closely at facts and test a claim | Points for, points against, finding |
| **Analyse** | Break into parts, explain how parts link | Causes → mechanism → effects |
| **Evaluate / Assess** | Judge value or success, with evidence | Achievements, limits, overall verdict |
| **Critically examine** | Strengths and weaknesses, then judgement | Positives, negatives, way forward |
| **Explain** | Make clear why/how | Cause-effect chain, maybe a flowchart |
| **Comment** | Give your view with reasons | Short background, view, reasons |
| **Elucidate** | Explain in detail with examples | Explanation + examples |
| **Compare / Distinguish** | Similarities and differences | Table is ideal |
| **Trace** | Show development over time | Timeline |
| **Illustrate** | Explain using examples, diagrams | Examples, diagram |

> **Common mistake:** Writing only "features" when the question says "critically evaluate". You get some content marks but lose the analysis marks. A "critically evaluate" answer must have a **limitations** part and a **judgement**.

## How marks are split inside one answer

No official breakdown is published. Treat the following as a planning guide, not a rule.

| Part | Share of your words | What it does for marks |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | about 10% | Shows understanding; sets direction |
| Body | about 75–80% | Most content marks; facts, analysis, Bihar angle |
| Conclusion | about 10% | Shows completeness and balance; way forward |

An answer without a conclusion looks unfinished. An answer that is all introduction (long background, little substance) looks empty.

## Word limits and how long that really is

Questions carry a word limit (the paper states it). This book practises two lengths:

| Answer length | Approx. pages (average handwriting) | Body points | Time to write |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short, about 250–300 words | about 1.5–2 pages | 5–7 | about 12–15 minutes |
| Long, about 500–600 words | about 3–4 pages | 8–12 with sub-headings | about 22–28 minutes |
| Statistics problem | depends on working | full steps + answer + graph if asked | as allotted |

Count your own words once: write 100 words in your normal handwriting and see how many lines that takes. Most people write 9–12 words per line. Then you know a 300-word answer is about 25–33 lines.

> **Remember:** Going 10–15% over the word limit is not punished in practice if the content is relevant, but going far over steals time from other questions. An unattempted question scores zero. **Completing the paper matters more than perfecting one answer.**

## Planning the 3-hour paper

The most common Mains failure is not lack of knowledge. It is **not finishing the paper**. Make a time plan before the exam and practise it in every full-length test.

### Step-by-step time plan

1. **First 10 minutes:** read the cover instructions; read every question; tick the ones you will attempt in each section; jot 3–4 key words next to each chosen question (in the question paper margin, if allowed, or rough space).
2. **Divide time by marks.** Minutes per question ≈ (marks of the question ÷ total marks) × writing minutes. Example: if a 300-mark paper has 155 writing minutes, a question worth 38 marks gets about 38 ÷ 300 × 155 ≈ 20 minutes.
3. **Start with your strongest section** to build confidence, but attempt **every section**. Number each answer exactly as in the question paper.
4. **Watch the clock at each answer's end.** If you are 5 minutes behind, shorten the next answer to points.
5. **Statistics:** solve it when you are fresh (many toppers do it first or right after their first section) because calculation errors increase when you are tired. Statistics answers are exact, so they can be high-scoring.
6. **Last 5 minutes:** check question numbers, underline missed key words, finish any incomplete conclusion with one line.

> **Memory trick:** **R-P-W-C** — **R**ead, **P**lan, **W**rite, **C**heck. Never skip P; 30 seconds of planning saves 5 minutes of confused writing.

## What reduces marks

| Mistake | Why it costs marks | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Answering a different question | Relevance is the first test | Underline the directive and each part |
| Long background, little analysis | Intro eats word limit | Intro ≤ 3 lines |
| Wrong facts (dates, articles) | Examiner loses trust in whole answer | Write only facts you are sure of; skip the rest |
| No Bihar angle where syllabus asks | Missed expected marks | Keep a Bihar fact bank ([Chapter 03](/interview/bpsc-2027/mains-answer-writing/adding-the-bihar-angle-data-and-diagrams)) |
| No conclusion | Looks unfinished | Always 2–3 lines, even in a rush |
| Paragraph blocks of 15 lines | Hard to scan | Points, headings, spacing |
| Leaving questions unattempted | Zero marks | Time plan; switch to points when late |
| Overwriting and cutting | Messy, hard to read | Plan before writing; single neat strike-through |
| Personal opinions without reasons | Looks emotional | "Because…" with evidence |
| Political or one-sided language | Examiner expects balance | Neutral words; government and critics both |

> **Common mistake:** Inventing a statistic to "sound strong". If you are not sure of a number, write it qualitatively ("a large share", "more than half") or skip it. A wrong number damages the whole answer.

## Language: English medium tips

You will write in English. You do not need difficult words. You need **clear, correct, short** sentences.

- Use simple sentence frames: "The main causes were…", "As a result…", "However…", "Therefore…".
- One idea per sentence. One theme per point.
- Use the standard term the first time ("Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP)"), then the short form.
- Keep spelling of key names correct: **Champaran, Sachchidananda Sinha, Swami Sahajanand Saraswati, Panchayati Raj, Kosi**.
- Avoid slang, idioms and emotional words ("very very important", "shameful").

> **Bihar connection:** Many of the strongest Bihar facts are names and places (Jagdishpur, Motihari, Sadaqat Ashram, Sonepur, Hazaribagh jail). Practise spelling them. A misspelt key name can look like you are guessing.

### Mains questions

**1. "Time management decides Mains success more than knowledge." Discuss with reference to a 3-hour GS paper.** (about 300 words)

Outline:
- *Intro:* Mains is a test of knowledge expressed within limits; unattempted questions score zero.
- *Body:* (1) marks per minute logic — divide time by marks; (2) choice of questions in 10 minutes; (3) fixed time per answer and switching to points when late; (4) attempting every section; (5) statistics early, when fresh; (6) buffer and checking; (7) practice through weekly full-length tests.
- *Conclusion:* Knowledge earns marks only if written; a planned paper converts preparation into marks.

**2. What qualities make an answer score well in a descriptive examination? Explain.** (about 300 words)

Outline:
- *Intro:* The examiner reads quickly; marks follow visible quality.
- *Body:* relevance to directive; structure; specific facts; analysis (cause-effect, both sides); Bihar angle; diagram/table; neat presentation; balanced conclusion.
- *Conclusion:* Good answers are planned, not just written.
