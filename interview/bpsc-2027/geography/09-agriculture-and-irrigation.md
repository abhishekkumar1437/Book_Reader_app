Agriculture is where geography meets the economy. BPSC Prelims asks about cropping seasons, crop–climate–soil conditions, leading states, shifting cultivation names, revolutions and irrigation. Mains GS II asks about problems of Indian agriculture, the Green Revolution, irrigation and groundwater, and Bihar's farm sector. This chapter covers the **geography** of farming — where and why crops grow. The **economics** — MSP, PDS, food security, land reforms — is in [Agriculture and Food Security](/interview/bpsc-2027/economy/agriculture-and-food-security), and Bihar's crops (makhana, litchi, maize) in [Agriculture, Minerals and Natural Resources of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/agriculture-minerals-and-natural-resources-of-bihar).

> **In simple words:** Indian farming follows the monsoon. Crops sown with the rains in June–July are **kharif**; crops sown in winter are **rabi**; a short summer crop in between is **zaid**. Each crop needs its own temperature, rain and soil — rice needs heat and lots of water, wheat needs a cool winter, cotton needs black soil, tea needs sloping, rainy hills. Only about half of India's farmland is irrigated, so the monsoon still decides many harvests.

## Why agriculture matters

- Agriculture and allied sectors employ about **45% of India's workforce** but produce only about **16–18% of Gross Value Added** (check the latest Economic Survey). This gap shows low productivity per worker and disguised unemployment.
- It supplies food, raw materials (cotton, jute, sugarcane) and exports (rice, spices, tea, marine products).
- In **Bihar**, the share of people depending on farming is much higher than the national average, and most holdings are **marginal** (below 1 hectare).

### Land use (nine-fold classification)

Land records divide the reporting area into: forests; land not available for cultivation (barren and non-agricultural uses); permanent pastures; land under tree crops and groves; culturable waste; current fallows; other fallows; and **net sown area**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Net sown area (NSA)** | Area sown at least once in a year — about **140 million hectares**, roughly **46%** of the reporting area |
| **Gross cropped area (GCA)** | Total area sown, counting land sown twice as two |
| **Cropping intensity** | (GCA ÷ NSA) × 100 — higher means more crops per year on the same land |
| Current fallow | Land left uncultivated for one year or less |

States with very high NSA share include **Punjab, Haryana** and **Bihar** (Bihar's plains are almost fully farmed) — check the latest land-use statistics.

## Types of farming

| Type | Features | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Subsistence farming** | Small plots, family labour, simple tools, grown mainly for the family | Most of India, including Bihar |
| **Intensive subsistence** | High inputs of labour on small land to get more output | Densely populated plains (Bihar, West Bengal, Kerala) |
| **Commercial farming** | Grown for sale; high inputs of seed, fertiliser, machines | Punjab, Haryana (wheat, rice) |
| **Plantation farming** | Single crop on a large estate, needs capital, labour and processing nearby | **Tea** (Assam, WB), **coffee** (Karnataka), **rubber** (Kerala), coconut, spices |
| **Mixed farming** | Crops plus livestock | Many parts of India |
| **Dry-land farming** | In areas with less than about 75 cm rain; drought-resistant crops | Rajasthan, Deccan interior, south Bihar fringe |
| **Shifting (slash-and-burn) cultivation** | Clear forest by burning, farm for a few years, move on when fertility falls | Hills of the North-East, Odisha, MP, Andhra Pradesh |

## Cropping seasons

| Season | Sown | Harvested | Main crops |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kharif** (monsoon) | **June–July** (with the rains) | **September–October** | **Rice**, maize, jowar, bajra, ragi, **cotton**, **jute**, groundnut, soybean, arhar (tur), moong, urad, sugarcane (planting in some areas) |
| **Rabi** (winter) | **October–December** | **March–April** | **Wheat**, barley, **gram**, peas, masoor, **mustard/rapeseed**, linseed |
| **Zaid** (summer) | March–April (between rabi and kharif) | June | **Watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber**, vegetables, fodder crops, moong |

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar's farmers traditionally name **four** seasons: **Bhadai** (early kharif, harvested around Bhado — August–September: early paddy, maize, jute), **Aghani** (main winter paddy harvested in Aghan — November–December), **Rabi** (wheat, maize, pulses, oilseeds harvested in spring) and **Garma** (summer moong, vegetables). Bihar's **rabi maize** has grown fast and gives very high yields.

## Major crops: climate, soil and leading states

Leading states change from year to year. The states below are the ones standard books and recent data commonly show (check the latest Agricultural Statistics at a Glance or the Economic Survey before quoting rank).

### Horticulture and allied sectors

- India is the **2nd largest producer of fruits and vegetables** (after China).
- **Mango** — Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh; **banana** — AP, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu; **onion** — Maharashtra (Lasalgaon, Nashik — Asia's biggest onion market); **potato** — **UP, West Bengal, Bihar**.
- **Litchi** — **Bihar** is the leading state; **Shahi litchi of Muzaffarpur** has a GI tag.
- **Makhana (fox nut)** — **Bihar** produces the great majority of India's makhana (Mithila region: Darbhanga, Madhubani, Purnia, Katihar); **Mithila Makhana** has a GI tag; the Union Budget 2025–26 announced a **Makhana Board** for Bihar (check the latest).
- **Milk** — India is the **largest producer** in the world (Operation Flood); **Uttar Pradesh** leads among states. Bihar's dairy cooperative brand is **Sudha** (COMFED).
- **Fish** — India is the **2nd largest fish producer**; **Andhra Pradesh** leads; **Pradhan Mantri Matsya Sampada Yojana (2020)**. Bihar has become self-sufficient or nearly so in fish in recent years (check the latest).

### The Green Revolution

- Started in **1965–66** (first results 1967–68) with **High-Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds** of wheat (dwarf Mexican varieties from Borlaug's centre) and rice (IR-8 from IRRI, Philippines), with **assured irrigation**, **chemical fertilisers**, pesticides, credit and **Minimum Support Prices**.
- Led by **M.S. Swaminathan** (scientist) with Agriculture Minister **C. Subramaniam**.
- Concentrated in **Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh**; wheat benefited much more than rice at first.

| Achievements | Problems |
|---|---|
| Food self-sufficiency; end of "ship-to-mouth" dependence on imports (PL-480) | Regional inequality — eastern India (including **Bihar**) was left behind |
| Large buffer stocks | Crop inequality — pulses, oilseeds, coarse grains neglected |
| Rise in farm incomes in irrigated areas | **Groundwater depletion** (Punjab, Haryana) |
| Growth of agro-industries | Soil degradation, salinity, chemical residues, health problems |
| — | Rich–poor gap among farmers; mono-cropping (wheat–rice) |

**Bringing Green Revolution to Eastern India (BGREI)** — launched **2010–11** for Assam, **Bihar**, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, eastern UP and West Bengal, to raise rice and wheat productivity in this water-rich but low-yield region.

## Irrigation

India needs irrigation because rainfall is **seasonal** (about 75% in four months), **uneven** across space, **unreliable** year to year, and because HYV crops and multiple cropping need assured water.

About **half** of India's net sown area is irrigated (net irrigated area roughly 75 million hectares — check the latest land-use statistics); the rest is rain-fed.

### Sources of irrigation

| Source | Share of net irrigated area (approx.) | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tube wells and other wells** (groundwater) | **Over 60%** — the largest source | UP, Punjab, Rajasthan, MP, Gujarat, **Bihar** | Cheap electricity and diesel pumps → **overuse** |
| **Canals** | About **a quarter** | Plains and deltas — UP (largest canal-irrigated area), Punjab, Haryana, AP, Bihar | Need perennial rivers or dams |
| **Tanks** | Small share (under 5%) | **Tamil Nadu**, Telangana, AP, Karnataka (hard rock peninsula) | Traditional rain-water storage |
| Others | Rest | Springs, ahars, *kuls* (Himalaya), *zings* (Ladakh) | — |

**Punjab** has the **highest share of its area irrigated** (nearly all).

### Methods of saving water

- **Drip irrigation** (water drop by drop at the root) and **sprinkler irrigation** — together called **micro-irrigation**; can save 30–50% water.
- **Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY), 2015** — "**Har Khet Ko Pani**" and "**More Crop Per Drop**"; includes the **Accelerated Irrigation Benefit Programme (AIBP, 1996)** for completing big projects and watershed development.
- **Command Area Development** (1974) — to make full use of canal water in project areas.

### Groundwater crisis

- India is the **world's largest user of groundwater**.
- The **Central Ground Water Board (CGWB)** assesses blocks as safe, semi-critical, critical or **over-exploited**; over-exploitation is worst in **Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Delhi** and parts of Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and western UP.
- Problems: falling water tables, costlier pumping, arsenic and fluoride contamination (**arsenic in the Ganga plains of Bihar** — Buxar, Bhojpur, Patna, Vaishali, Bhagalpur and other districts), and power subsidy burdens.
- **Atal Bhujal Yojana (2019)** — community-led groundwater management in seven states (Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, MP, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, UP).
- **Jal Shakti Abhiyan: "Catch the Rain"** campaign; **Jal Jeevan Mission (2019)** for tap water to rural homes.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar has three canal systems — the **Sone canal** (built in the **1870s** from Dehri; Shahabad–Magadh plains: Rohtas, Bhojpur, Buxar, Aurangabad), the **Kosi canals** (Eastern and Western Kosi Canals) and the **Gandak canals** (Tirhut, Saran). Most irrigation, however, comes from **shallow tube wells**. South Bihar's traditional **ahar–pyne** system (an *ahar* is a reservoir bounded on three sides; *pynes* are channels that bring river water to it) in Gaya, Jehanabad, Nalanda and Aurangabad is being revived under **Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali**. Bihar has run **Agriculture Road Maps** since 2008; the **fourth Road Map (2023–28)** is current (check the latest).

## Agro-climatic regions

- The **Planning Commission (1988)** divided India into **15 agro-climatic regions** based on soil, climate, physiography and water — e.g. Western Himalayan, Eastern Himalayan, Lower Gangetic Plains, **Middle Gangetic Plains** (Bihar and eastern UP), Upper Gangetic Plains, Trans-Gangetic Plains (Punjab–Haryana), Eastern Plateau and Hills (Chota Nagpur), Central Plateau, Western Plateau, Southern Plateau, East Coast Plains, West Coast Plains, Gujarat Plains, Western Dry Region (Rajasthan), Island Region.
- **Bihar** has **four agro-climatic zones**: **Zone I** (north-west alluvial plain), **Zone II** (north-east alluvial plain), **Zone III-A** (south-east alluvial plain) and **Zone III-B** (south-west alluvial plain). Details: [Agriculture, Minerals and Natural Resources of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/agriculture-minerals-and-natural-resources-of-bihar).

## Problems of Indian agriculture (Mains points)

1. **Small and fragmented holdings** — average holding about 1.1 hectares nationally (Agriculture Census 2015–16) and smaller in Bihar.
2. **Dependence on the monsoon** — half the area rain-fed.
3. **Low productivity** compared with world averages.
4. **Soil degradation** and declining organic carbon.
5. **Groundwater overuse** and poor canal efficiency.
6. **Poor storage, market access and post-harvest losses** (perishables).
7. **Credit and debt**; dependence on moneylenders.
8. **Climate change** — heat stress, erratic rain, floods and droughts.
9. **Disguised unemployment** and low non-farm opportunities in villages.
10. **Price risk** — MSP benefits concentrated in a few crops and states.

### Mains questions

**Q1. Assess the impact of the Green Revolution in India. Why did it bypass eastern India, including Bihar, and what is being done to bring it there? (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** HYV-based strategy of 1965–66; Swaminathan and Borlaug; aim of food self-sufficiency.
- **Positive impact:** big rise in wheat and rice output; buffer stocks; end of PL-480 dependence; higher farm incomes; agro-industry and rural non-farm growth.
- **Negative impact:** regional and crop imbalance; groundwater depletion; soil and water pollution; mono-cropping; inequality among farmers.
- **Why Bihar was bypassed:** poor assured irrigation and power at the time; floods and waterlogging in the north; small, fragmented holdings and tenancy; weak land reforms and credit; poor extension, markets and roads; institutional weaknesses.
- **Now:** BGREI (2010–11); Bihar's Agriculture Road Maps (2008 onward; fourth 2023–28); rabi maize boom; seed replacement; zero-tillage wheat; farm mechanisation subsidies; makhana and litchi value chains; Dr. Rajendra Prasad Central Agricultural University and Bihar Agricultural University research.
- **Conclusion:** eastern India has water, soil and labour — the "second Green Revolution" can come from here if it is sustainable and climate-smart.

**Q2. "Groundwater is India's invisible crisis." Discuss the causes and suggest measures, with a reference to Bihar's arsenic problem.**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** India is the world's largest groundwater user; wells and tube wells give over 60% of irrigation and most drinking water.
- **Causes:** free or cheap power for pumps; water-intensive crops (paddy, sugarcane) in dry regions; MSP and procurement favouring them; urbanisation and paved surfaces cutting recharge; lack of regulation (groundwater treated as private property of the landowner).
- **Effects:** falling water tables; dry wells; costlier pumping; sea-water intrusion on coasts; arsenic, fluoride and nitrate contamination.
- **Bihar:** arsenic in shallow aquifers of Ganga-belt districts (Buxar, Bhojpur, Patna, Vaishali, Bhagalpur and others); health effects (skin lesions, cancer); supply of treated surface water and deep-aquifer wells; Har Ghar Nal Ka Jal under Saat Nischay.
- **Measures:** Atal Bhujal Yojana; micro-irrigation; crop diversification; metering and separate feeders for farm power; rainwater harvesting, check dams and pond revival (Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali, Amrit Sarovar); aquifer mapping; community water budgets.
- **Conclusion:** manage groundwater as a common resource — "measure, regulate, recharge".
