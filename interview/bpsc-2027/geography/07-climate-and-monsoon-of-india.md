India's whole economy is often described as "a gamble on the monsoon". This chapter explains why the monsoon comes, how it spreads, why it fails in some years, and how rain is spread across India. BPSC Prelims asks about pre-monsoon showers (mango showers, Kal Baisakhi), western disturbances, the two branches of the monsoon, the wettest places, the north-east monsoon of Tamil Nadu, and El Niño. Mains GS II asks you to explain the monsoon mechanism and its effect on agriculture, floods and drought — especially in Bihar. Read chapter 3 first if you have not.

> **In simple words:** In summer the land of north-west India and Tibet becomes very hot, so air rises and pressure falls. The sea stays cooler, so pressure there is higher. Moist winds from the Indian Ocean rush in towards the land from the south-west — this is the south-west monsoon, which gives about three-quarters of India's yearly rain from June to September. In winter the land is colder than the sea, so dry winds blow from the land to the sea from the north-east. The real mechanism also involves the shifting rain belt (ITCZ), jet streams high in the sky and ocean conditions far away in the Pacific and Indian Oceans.

## What controls India's climate

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Latitude** | The **Tropic of Cancer** passes through the middle of India — the south is tropical, the north subtropical |
| **Himalaya** | Block cold Central Asian winds in winter; trap monsoon winds and make them rain over the plains |
| **Distance from the sea** | Coastal areas have mild (equable) climates; interiors like Delhi and Patna have extreme heat and cold (continental climate) |
| **Altitude** | Hill stations are cooler (temperature falls about 6.5 °C per km) |
| **Relief** | Western Ghats and Meghalaya hills cause heavy orographic rain; rain-shadow areas behind them are dry |
| **Pressure and winds** | Seasonal reversal of winds — the monsoon |
| **Jet streams** | Subtropical westerly jet in winter (western disturbances); tropical easterly jet in summer (monsoon) |
| **Western disturbances** | Winter rain in the north-west |
| **Tropical cyclones** | Heavy rain on the east coast, especially October–November |
| **El Niño, La Niña, IOD** | Year-to-year strength of the monsoon |

India's climate is called **tropical monsoon type**.

## The four seasons (IMD)

| Season | Months | Main features |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold weather (winter)** | **December – February** | Clear skies, low temperature in the north, cold waves, fog; dry north-east winds; rain from **western disturbances** in the north-west; rain in **Tamil Nadu** |
| **Hot weather (summer, pre-monsoon)** | **March – May** | Rising temperature, Loo, dust storms, thunderstorms; pre-monsoon showers |
| **South-west monsoon (rainy season)** | **June – September** | About **75%** of the year's rain for the country as a whole |
| **Post-monsoon (retreating monsoon)** | **October – November** | Clear skies, "**October heat**"; cyclones from the Bay of Bengal; heavy rain on the Coromandel coast |

### Cold weather season

- The sun is overhead in the Southern Hemisphere; a **high-pressure area** forms over north-west India.
- Cold, dry winds blow out from the land towards the sea in a north-east direction — the **north-east trade winds / winter monsoon**. They are mostly dry because they come from the land.
- **Western disturbances** — shallow low-pressure systems from the **Mediterranean** region, brought east by the **subtropical westerly jet stream** which flows south of the Himalaya in winter. They give **light winter rain in Punjab, Haryana, west UP and Delhi**, and **snow in the Himalaya**. This small rain (*mahawat*) is very useful for **rabi crops, especially wheat**.
- The north-east winds pick up moisture over the **Bay of Bengal** and give **winter rain to the Tamil Nadu coast** — the **north-east monsoon** (October–December).
- **Fog, cold waves and temperature inversions** in the north Indian plains, including Bihar; trains and flights are delayed.

### Hot weather season

- The sun moves north; temperatures reach **45 °C or more** in north-west India in May.
- A **thermal low** develops over north-west India and Pakistan.
- **Loo** — hot, dry, gusty afternoon winds of the north plains (Punjab to Bihar).
- Dust storms in Rajasthan and Punjab.

**Pre-monsoon showers (local names — frequently asked):**

| Name | Where | Why useful |
|---|---|---|
| **Mango showers** | Kerala and coastal Karnataka | Help mangoes ripen early |
| **Blossom (cherry) showers** | Kerala and Karnataka (coffee areas) | Make **coffee** flowers blossom |
| **Kal Baisakhi (Nor'westers)** | **West Bengal**, Odisha, Bangladesh; also **Bihar** and Jharkhand | Violent evening thunderstorms with hail ("calamity of the month of Baisakh"); useful for **tea, jute, rice** |
| **Bardoli Chheerha** | Assam | Same storms — helpful for **tea** |
| **Loo** | North-west and north plains | Hot, dry wind (not a shower) |

### 1. Differential heating (classical thermal theory)

First suggested by **Edmund Halley (1686)**. Land heats and cools faster than water. In summer the land becomes a low-pressure area and the sea a high-pressure area; winds blow from sea to land. In winter it is the opposite. This explains the **reversal** but not the timing, the breaks or the year-to-year changes.

### 2. Shift of the ITCZ (dynamic concept)

- The **Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** is the low-pressure belt where trade winds of both hemispheres meet. It follows the overhead sun.
- In July it shifts north to about **20°–25°N** over the Gangetic plain, where it is called the **monsoon trough**.
- The **south-east trade winds** of the Southern Hemisphere are pulled across the equator. The **Coriolis force** turns them to the right, so they become **south-west winds** — the south-west monsoon.
- In winter the ITCZ moves south of the equator and north-east winds prevail.

### 3. Tibetan Plateau heating

- The **Tibetan Plateau** (average about 4,500 m) is heated strongly in summer. It heats the air at a great height, producing **rising air and low pressure near the surface** and a **high-pressure area (anticyclone) in the upper air**.
- From this upper high, winds flow out southward as the **Tropical Easterly Jet (TEJ)** at about 15°N over peninsular India. A strong TEJ is linked with a strong monsoon.

### 4. Jet streams

- In winter the **subtropical westerly jet** blows **south of the Himalaya**. In early summer it weakens and **jumps north of the Tibetan Plateau** (around early June). This sudden shift is linked with the **"burst" (abrupt onset)** of the monsoon over India.
- The **Somali jet (Findlater jet)** — a low-level, strong south-westerly current off East Africa — carries moisture to the west coast.

### 5. Mascarene High

A high-pressure area near **Madagascar (Mascarene Islands)** in the southern Indian Ocean. Its strength helps push winds towards India.

### 6. Ocean–atmosphere links

| Factor | Effect on the monsoon |
|---|---|
| **El Niño** (warm central–eastern Pacific) | Usually **weakens** the monsoon (droughts such as 2002, 2009, 2015) |
| **La Niña** | Usually **strengthens** it |
| **Positive Indian Ocean Dipole** (warmer western Indian Ocean) | Usually **helps**; can offset El Niño |
| **Negative IOD** | Usually hurts |
| **Madden–Julian Oscillation (MJO)** | An eastward-moving pulse of clouds that can cause active and break spells within a season |

> **Mains angle:** A strong monsoon answer names at least four mechanisms — differential heating, ITCZ shift, Tibetan heating with TEJ, westerly jet shift — and two ocean links — ENSO and IOD — and shows them in one labelled diagram. That alone lifts an average answer to a good one.

- **Burst of the monsoon** — the sudden start of heavy rain with thunder and lightning when the monsoon arrives on the Kerala coast.
- **Break in the monsoon** — dry spells of one to several weeks within the season, when the **monsoon trough** moves north to the Himalayan foothills. During breaks, plains get little rain but the **Himalayan foothills and north Bihar get heavy rain** → floods in north Bihar even while the rest of India is dry.
- **Monsoon depressions** — low-pressure systems that form over the **Bay of Bengal** and move west-north-west along the monsoon trough, giving much of the rain to central and north India.

**Arabian Sea branch** (stronger — about three times the Bay of Bengal branch, per NCERT):
1. Strikes the **Western Ghats** → very heavy orographic rain on the windward side (Konkan, Malabar). The leeward Deccan (east of the Ghats) is a **rain-shadow** area (e.g. Pune, parts of Marathwada, north Karnataka).
2. Enters along the **Narmada–Tapi** valleys into central India and the Chota Nagpur plateau.
3. Enters **Saurashtra and Kutch**, then passes over **west Rajasthan parallel to the Aravalli** — so it gives **very little rain** to Rajasthan; joins the Bay of Bengal branch over Punjab and Haryana.

**Bay of Bengal branch:**
1. Strikes **Myanmar** and **Bangladesh**; a large part is deflected into the North-East — the funnel-shaped **Khasi hills** produce extreme rain at **Mawsynram** and **Cherrapunji (Sohra)**.
2. The other part is deflected **westward by the Himalaya** along the Ganga plain. Rain **decreases from east to west**: West Bengal → **Bihar** → eastern UP → Delhi → Punjab.
3. The **Tamil Nadu coast** stays relatively **dry in June–September** because it lies in the rain-shadow of the Western Ghats for the Arabian Sea branch and parallel to the Bay of Bengal branch.

### Retreating (post-monsoon) season

- From October the monsoon trough weakens and moves south; winds weaken; skies clear.
- **October heat** — high temperature plus high humidity makes the weather oppressive.
- **Tropical cyclones** from the Bay of Bengal hit the east coast (Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu).
- The **Coromandel coast (Tamil Nadu)** gets most of its rain in **October–December** from the north-east monsoon and cyclones.

## Rainfall distribution

| Annual rainfall | Regions |
|---|---|
| **Above 200 cm** (very high) | **Western coast and Western Ghats**, **sub-Himalayan North-East**, Meghalaya hills, Andaman & Nicobar |
| **100–200 cm** (high) | **Eastern India — West Bengal, Odisha, Bihar, Jharkhand**, eastern MP, Chhattisgarh, the Western Ghats' eastern slopes, Tamil Nadu coast |
| **50–100 cm** (moderate) | Western UP, Delhi, Haryana, Punjab, eastern Rajasthan, Gujarat, the interior Deccan |
| **Below 50 cm** (low) | **Western Rajasthan, Kutch**, **Ladakh**, rain-shadow parts of the Deccan |

- **Mawsynram** (Meghalaya) records the **highest average annual rainfall in the world** (about 11,000–12,000 mm); **Cherrapunji (Sohra)** is close behind.
- **Lowest rainfall:** western Rajasthan (around Jaisalmer) and **Leh** (Ladakh).
- **Variability:** where rainfall is low (Rajasthan, Gujarat, interior Deccan), it is also **most variable** (coefficient of variation often above 50%) — these are drought-prone. Where rainfall is high (west coast, North-East), variability is lower.

### How IMD describes the monsoon

The **Long Period Average (LPA)** of June–September rainfall for India as a whole is about **87 cm** (based on 1971–2020).

| Category | Rainfall as % of LPA |
|---|---|
| Deficient | Below 90% |
| Below normal | 90–95% |
| **Normal** | **96–104%** |
| Above normal | 105–110% |
| Excess | Above 110% |

## Monsoon as a unifying bond — and a risk

**Unifying role:** the same rhythm of seasons runs across the country; festivals (Onam, Pongal, Baisakhi, Chhath, Bihu), the farm calendar (kharif–rabi) and even songs follow it. The Himalaya protect the whole subcontinent from cold winds.

**Economic role:**
- Over half of India's net sown area is still **rain-fed**; kharif output (rice, pulses, oilseeds, cotton) depends on the monsoon.
- It recharges **groundwater** and fills **reservoirs** for hydropower and the rabi season.
- A weak monsoon raises food prices (inflation), reduces rural demand and affects GDP.

**Risks:** late onset, long breaks, early withdrawal, uneven spread, **floods** (Assam, Bihar, Kerala 2018) and **droughts** (Rajasthan, Marathwada, Bundelkhand, south Bihar).

**Drought** (IMD meteorological definition): rainfall deficiency of **more than 25%** of normal; **26–50%** = moderate, **above 50%** = severe.

### Heat waves and cold waves

- IMD declares a **heat wave** in the plains when the maximum temperature is at least **40 °C** and is **4.5–6.4 °C above normal** (severe if more than 6.4 °C above normal), or when the actual maximum reaches **45 °C** or more (check IMD's latest criteria).
- Heat waves are becoming more frequent and longer due to climate change. **Heat Action Plans** (first in **Ahmedabad, 2013**) are now made by many states and cities, including Bihar (check the latest).
- **Cold waves** and dense fog hit the north plains in December–January; in Bihar schools often close for cold spells.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar gets most of its rain from the **Bay of Bengal branch** between mid-June and September. Average annual rainfall is roughly **1,000–1,200 mm**, **highest in the north-east** (Kishanganj, Purnia, Araria, near the Himalayan foothills) and **lowest in the south-west and west** (Kaimur–Rohtas, Buxar, Bhojpur) — the same east-to-west decline seen across the Ganga plain. North Bihar floods come partly from heavy rain in the **Nepal catchments**, especially during **monsoon breaks**, when the trough sits along the foothills. South Bihar faces drought in years of weak or delayed rain. Bihar also faces **Kal Baisakhi** storms, **Loo**, **heat waves**, **cold waves**, and high **lightning** risk. Read [Rivers, Floods and Climate of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/rivers-floods-and-climate-of-bihar) for agro-climatic zones and figures.

## Quick revision

- India: tropical monsoon climate; Tropic of Cancer divides it roughly into tropical and subtropical halves.
- Four seasons: Dec–Feb winter; Mar–May summer; Jun–Sep SW monsoon (~75% of rain); Oct–Nov retreating.
- Western disturbances: from the Mediterranean, steered by the westerly jet → winter rain for wheat in the north-west.
- North-east monsoon: Tamil Nadu coast, October–December.
- Mango showers — Kerala/Karnataka (mangoes); blossom showers — coffee; Kal Baisakhi — West Bengal/Bihar (tea, jute, rice); Bardoli Chheerha — Assam.
- Monsoon mechanism: differential heating (Halley), ITCZ shift to ~20–25°N (monsoon trough), Tibetan heating + Tropical Easterly Jet, westerly jet jumps north of Tibet (burst), Mascarene High, Somali jet, ENSO, IOD, MJO.
- Normal onset Kerala 1 June; whole country ~8 July; withdrawal starts ~17 Sept; ends ~15 Oct.
- Arabian Sea branch stronger; Western Ghats windward heavy rain, Deccan rain-shadow.
- Bay of Bengal branch: Meghalaya extreme rain; deflected west along the Ganga plain — rain falls from Kolkata to Patna to Delhi.
- Rajasthan dry because winds run parallel to the Aravalli.
- Mawsynram: highest average rainfall in the world.
- LPA ~87 cm; normal = 96–104% of LPA.
- Coromandel = As climate; Ganga plain = Cwg.
- Drought: deficiency over 25%; severe over 50%.
- Heat Action Plan first in Ahmedabad (2013).

### Mains questions

**Q1. Explain the mechanism of the Indian south-west monsoon. Why does it vary from year to year? (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** monsoon = seasonal wind reversal; June–September gives ~75% of India's rain; IMD normal onset 1 June over Kerala.
- **Mechanism:** (1) differential heating of land and sea; (2) northward shift of the ITCZ to ~20–25°N and Coriolis turning of SE trades into SW winds; (3) Tibetan Plateau heating — upper-air high and the Tropical Easterly Jet; (4) subtropical westerly jet moving north of the Himalaya in early June — burst of monsoon; (5) Mascarene High and Somali jet; (6) role of the Himalaya and Western Ghats in causing rain. Add a labelled diagram.
- **Variability:** El Niño and La Niña; Indian Ocean Dipole; MJO; snow cover over Eurasia/Himalaya; breaks; date of onset and withdrawal; climate change increasing extreme rain events.
- **Impacts:** agriculture, food inflation, hydropower, groundwater; floods in Bihar and Assam, droughts in Rajasthan and south Bihar.
- **Conclusion:** better forecasting (IMD's dynamical models), crop contingency plans and water storage reduce India's "gamble on the monsoon".

**Q2. "Bihar suffers from floods and droughts in the same year." Explain this paradox in the light of the monsoon and the physiography of the state.**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Bihar receives ~1,000–1,200 mm of mostly Bay of Bengal monsoon rain, but very unevenly.
- **Floods in the north:** rivers from the Nepal Himalaya (Kosi, Gandak, Bagmati, Kamla) carry rain from catchments outside Bihar; monsoon breaks shift heavy rain to the foothills; flat gradient, silt, embankment breaches; Ganga backwater during high flows.
- **Drought in the south:** plateau-fed seasonal rivers (Son, Punpun, Phalgu); lower and more variable rain in the south-west; hard rocks with low groundwater recharge; decline of traditional ahar–pyne systems.
- **Same-year paradox:** e.g. a year with late or weak rain in the south and heavy catchment rain in Nepal — district lists of both flood and drought are declared.
- **Responses:** flood forecasting with Nepal; embankment and drainage management; drought-tolerant varieties; ahar–pyne and pond revival under Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali; crop insurance; contingency plans; diversion schemes (Kosi–Mechi).
- **Conclusion:** Bihar needs a "two-handed" water policy — drain and protect in the north, store and recharge in the south.
