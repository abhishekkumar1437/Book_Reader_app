This chapter explains what lies under our feet and how the surface gets its shape. BPSC asks about the layers and discontinuities of the earth, seismic waves, rock types (especially metamorphic pairs like limestone → marble), plate boundaries with examples, and landforms such as oxbow lakes, deltas and sand dunes. For Mains, earthquakes and the formation of the Himalaya are useful, and Bihar gives you a strong angle through the **1934 earthquake** and the **Kosi megafan**.

> **In simple words:** The earth is like an egg — a thin shell (crust), a thick hot middle (mantle) and a metal centre (core). The outer shell is broken into large moving pieces called plates. Where plates push, pull or slide, we get mountains, earthquakes and volcanoes. On the surface, rivers, glaciers, wind and waves slowly cut and build the land. Rocks keep changing from one type into another in a never-ending cycle.

## How we know about the interior

Nobody has travelled to the centre of the earth. The deepest borehole (Kola Superdeep Borehole, Russia) reached only about **12 km** — tiny compared with the **6,371 km** radius. So we learn from:

| Direct sources | Indirect sources |
|---|---|
| Mining and deep drilling | **Seismic (earthquake) waves** — the most important source |
| Material thrown out by volcanoes (magma) | Meteorites (similar material to the earth's interior) |
| — | Gravity differences, magnetic field, temperature and pressure rising with depth |

Temperature rises by roughly **1 °C for every 32 m** in the upper crust; the rate slows down deeper inside.

### Seismic waves

| Wave | Type | Travels through | Speed | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P-waves** (primary) | Longitudinal (push–pull, like sound) | Solids, liquids and gases | **Fastest**, arrive first | Similar to sound waves |
| **S-waves** (secondary) | Transverse (side-to-side) | **Solids only** | Slower | Cannot pass the liquid outer core |
| Surface waves (L-waves — Love and Rayleigh) | Move along the surface | Surface layers | Slowest | **Most destructive** |

**Shadow zone:** Seismographs between about **105° and 145°** from the epicentre receive no direct P- or S-waves. Beyond 145° only P-waves arrive; S-waves are absent beyond 105° on the far side. Because S-waves cannot cross a liquid, this proves that the **outer core is liquid**.

| Layer | Depth | State and material | Key points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Crust** | Oceanic ~5–7 km; continental ~30–35 km on average, up to ~70 km under big mountains | Solid rock | Thinnest layer (<1% of earth's volume). Continental crust = **SIAL** (silica + alumina, lighter, granite-like); oceanic crust = **SIMA** (silica + magnesium, heavier, basalt) |
| **Mantle** | Moho to ~2,900 km | Solid but hot; upper part partly molten | About **83–84% of the earth's volume**. The **asthenosphere** (upper mantle, up to about 400 km) is weak and semi-molten — the main source of **magma** and the layer on which plates move |
| **Outer core** | ~2,900–5,150 km | **Liquid** iron and nickel | Movement of this liquid metal creates the earth's **magnetic field** |
| **Inner core** | ~5,150–6,371 km | **Solid** iron and nickel (**NIFE**) under huge pressure | Temperature about 5,000–6,000 °C |

- **Lithosphere** = crust + the uppermost solid part of the mantle (about 100 km thick). It is broken into plates.

### Discontinuities (boundaries)

| Name | Between |
|---|---|
| **Conrad** | Upper crust (SIAL) and lower crust (SIMA) |
| **Mohorovičić (Moho)** | **Crust and mantle** |
| **Repetti** | Upper mantle and lower mantle |
| **Gutenberg** | **Mantle and outer core** (~2,900 km) |
| **Lehmann** | **Outer core and inner core** (~5,150 km) |

> **Memory trick:** From outside to inside — "**C**an **M**y **R**ed **G**oat **L**eap?" — Conrad, Moho, Repetti, Gutenberg, Lehmann.

### What the earth is made of

| Earth's crust (by weight) | Whole earth (by weight) |
|---|---|
| **Oxygen ~46.6%** | **Iron** ~32% (most abundant) |
| Silicon ~27.7% | Oxygen ~30% |
| Aluminium ~8.1% (most abundant metal in the crust) | Silicon ~15% |
| Iron ~5% | Magnesium ~14% |
| Then calcium, sodium, potassium, magnesium | — |

> **Common mistake:** "Most abundant element of the earth's crust" = **oxygen**; "most abundant metal of the crust" = **aluminium**; "most abundant element of the whole earth" = **iron**.

## Earthquakes

An **earthquake** is a sudden shaking of the ground when energy stored in rocks is released, usually when rocks break along a **fault**.

- **Focus (hypocentre):** the point inside the earth where the rupture starts.
- **Epicentre:** the point on the surface directly above the focus; shaking is usually strongest here.
- **Seismograph:** the instrument that records waves; the study of earthquakes is **seismology**.

| Scale | Measures | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Richter scale** (and the modern **moment magnitude** scale) | **Magnitude** — energy released | Logarithmic: each whole number = **10 times** more ground movement and roughly **32 times** more energy |
| **Modified Mercalli scale** | **Intensity** — damage felt at a place | I to XII, based on what people see and feel |

**Causes:** plate movements (most), volcanic activity, collapse of mines and caves, and **reservoir-induced** quakes from the weight of water in big dams (example: **Koyna, Maharashtra, 1967**).

### Earthquakes in India

India has seismic zones under the BIS code. The older map had **Zones II–V** (Zone V = highest risk). Zone V included the North-East, parts of J&K and Ladakh, Himachal, Uttarakhand, the Rann of Kutch, **north Bihar** and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands. In 2025 the BIS issued a revised seismic code which reports say places the Himalayan belt in a new highest zone (**Zone VI**) — as of 2026, check the latest map before quoting zone numbers.

| Earthquake | Year | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Bihar–Nepal earthquake** | **15 January 1934** | Magnitude about 8; heavy damage in **Munger, Darbhanga, Motihari, Sitamarhi**; Gandhiji toured the affected areas |
| Assam (Assam–Tibet) | 1950 | About 8.6, one of the strongest recorded on land |
| Koyna | 1967 | Reservoir-induced |
| Latur (Killari), Maharashtra | 1993 | Peninsular, thought to be a "stable" area |
| **Bhuj, Gujarat** | **26 January 2001** | About 7.7 |
| Indian Ocean tsunami | 26 December 2004 | Sumatra quake (~9.1); hit Tamil Nadu, A&N Islands |
| Gorkha, Nepal | 25 April 2015 | About 7.8; also caused deaths and damage in Bihar |

> **Bihar connection:** North Bihar lies close to the Himalayan collision zone and on soft alluvium, which shakes more and can liquefy. Districts along the Nepal border are among the most earthquake-prone in India. Read [Physical Geography of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/physical-geography-of-bihar) for the regions.

## Volcanoes

A **volcano** is an opening in the crust through which **magma** (molten rock inside the earth) comes out. Once on the surface it is called **lava**. Volcanoes also throw out gases, ash and rock pieces (*pyroclasts*).

| By activity | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Active | Erupts often or recently | Etna, Stromboli ("**Lighthouse of the Mediterranean**"), Kilauea; **Barren Island** (India's only active volcano, Andaman Sea) |
| Dormant | Sleeping; may erupt again | Fujiyama (Japan), Vesuvius; **Narcondam** (Andaman Sea) |
| Extinct | No eruption in historical time, not expected | Kilimanjaro is often given as dormant; Mt Popa (Myanmar) as extinct |

| By shape | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Shield volcano | Runny basalt lava, gentle slopes, huge size | Mauna Loa (Hawaii) |
| Composite (strato) volcano | Layers of lava and ash, steep cone, explosive | Fuji, Vesuvius, Mt St Helens |
| Cinder cone | Small, steep, loose ash | Parícutin (Mexico) |
| Caldera | Large crater after a volcano collapses | Krakatoa area, Yellowstone |
| Flood basalt | Lava pours out of cracks over huge areas | **Deccan Traps** (India, about 66 million years ago) |

- The **Pacific Ring of Fire** has most of the world's active volcanoes and earthquakes (convergent plate boundaries around the Pacific).
- Related features: **hot springs** and **geysers** (Old Faithful, Yellowstone). In India: Manikaran (HP); in Bihar, the hot springs of **Rajgir**, **Sita Kund** (Munger) and **Bhimbandh** (Munger) are famous.

**Intrusive forms** (magma that cools inside the crust): **batholith** (huge dome-shaped mass of granite, base of mountains), **laccolith** (dome with flat base), **lopolith** (saucer-shaped), **phacolith** (wavy, in folds), **sill** (horizontal sheet), **dyke** (vertical wall-like sheet).

## Rocks and the rock cycle

A **rock** is a natural mass of one or more **minerals**. A **mineral** is a natural substance with a fixed chemical make-up and crystal form (quartz, feldspar, mica). **Feldspar** is the most common mineral group in the crust. Hardness is measured on the **Mohs scale** (1 talc … 10 diamond).

### Three rock types

| Type | How formed | Examples | Key points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Igneous** ("fire-formed") | Cooling of magma or lava | **Intrusive / plutonic** (cool slowly inside, big crystals): **granite**, gabbro. **Extrusive / volcanic** (cool fast on surface, small crystals): **basalt**, pumice, obsidian | Called **primary rocks** (all others come from them); no fossils; Deccan plateau is basalt |
| **Sedimentary** | Layers (*strata*) of sediment pressed and cemented | Sandstone, shale, **limestone**, conglomerate, **coal**, chalk | Only type with **fossils**; cover about 75% of the land surface but only ~5% of the crust's volume; hold coal, oil and gas |
| **Metamorphic** ("changed form") | Existing rocks changed by heat and pressure | See table below | Often hard and banded (foliated) |

### Metamorphic pairs (very commonly asked)

| Original rock | Becomes |
|---|---|
| **Limestone** | **Marble** (Taj Mahal — Makrana marble, Rajasthan) |
| **Sandstone** | **Quartzite** |
| **Shale / clay** | **Slate** (then schist with more heat) |
| **Granite** | **Gneiss** |
| Coal | Graphite (under high metamorphism) |

## Continental drift to plate tectonics

### Continental drift (Alfred Wegener, 1912)

Wegener said all continents were once joined in one supercontinent, **Pangaea**, surrounded by one ocean, **Panthalassa**. Pangaea split into **Laurasia** (north) and **Gondwanaland** (south — India, Africa, South America, Australia, Antarctica). The sea between them was the **Tethys Sea**.

Evidence:
- **Jig-saw fit** of South America and Africa coastlines.
- Same **fossils** on now-separated continents — the plant **Glossopteris** and the reptile **Mesosaurus**.
- Same rocks of the same age across oceans (e.g. Brazil and West Africa).
- **Tillite** (glacial deposits) in India, Africa, Australia — showing they were once near the South Pole together.
- Placer gold in Ghana with no local source — the source rocks are in Brazil.

Weakness: Wegener could not explain the **force** that moved the continents. **Arthur Holmes** (1930s) suggested **convection currents** in the mantle.

### Sea-floor spreading (Harry Hess, early 1960s)

- New ocean crust forms at **mid-ocean ridges** where magma rises, and spreads outward.
- Rocks are **youngest near the ridge** and older away from it.
- Magnetic stripes on the ocean floor are symmetrical on both sides of the ridge (**palaeomagnetism**).
- Old crust sinks back at **trenches** (subduction). The ocean floor is nowhere older than about 200 million years.

### Plate tectonics (late 1960s)

The lithosphere is broken into rigid **plates** that float and move on the asthenosphere.

**Seven major plates:** Pacific (largest), North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian (often split into Indian and Australian), Antarctic. Minor plates include Nazca, Cocos, Philippine, Arabian, Caribbean, Juan de Fuca, Scotia.

| Boundary | Also called | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Divergent** | Constructive | Plates move apart; magma rises; new crust | Mid-Atlantic Ridge (Iceland sits on it), East African Rift Valley |

| **Convergent** — continent–continent | Collision | Neither sinks easily; crust crumples into high fold mountains | **Himalaya** (Indian plate hits Eurasian plate), Alps |
| **Transform** | Conservative | Plates slide side by side | **San Andreas Fault** |

### How the Himalaya formed

1. About 200 million years ago India was part of Gondwanaland, near Africa and Antarctica.
2. The Indian plate broke away and moved **north** across the Tethys Sea. On the way it passed over a hotspot that produced the **Deccan Traps** (about 66 million years ago).
3. About **40–50 million years ago** India collided with the Eurasian plate.
4. Sediments of the Tethys Sea were squeezed and folded upward into the **Himalaya**. That is why **marine fossils** are found high in the Himalaya.
5. India is still pushing north by about **4–5 cm a year**, so the Himalaya are still rising and the region has many earthquakes.

> **Mains angle:** Use this sequence to explain (a) why the Himalaya are young fold mountains, (b) why the Himalayan belt and north Bihar are earthquake-prone, and (c) why the Ganga plain formed in the depression (*foredeep*) in front of the Himalaya and was filled with river sediment.

## Forces that shape the land

| Endogenic (inside the earth) | Exogenic (on the surface) |
|---|---|
| Driven by the earth's internal heat | Driven by the Sun's energy and gravity |
| **Diastrophism**: **orogeny** (mountain building by folding/faulting) and **epeirogeny** (uplift or sinking of large areas) | **Weathering** — breaking rocks where they lie |
| Volcanism, earthquakes | **Mass wasting** — landslides, mudflows, soil creep, avalanches |
| Build relief (make the land uneven) | **Erosion** and **deposition** by water, ice, wind, waves — wear relief down (*gradation*) |

### Weathering

| Type | Process | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Physical (mechanical) | Rock breaks without change in chemistry | **Exfoliation** (onion-peel weathering in hot deserts, day–night temperature change); **frost wedging** (water freezes in cracks and expands) |
| Chemical | Minerals change | **Oxidation** (rusting of iron in rocks → red colour), **carbonation** (rainwater with CO₂ dissolves limestone — caves), hydration, solution |
| Biological | Plants and animals | Roots splitting rocks; burrowing animals; lichens |

## Landforms by agent

### Running water (rivers) — the most important agent

| Stage | Erosional landforms | Depositional landforms |
|---|---|---|
| Youth (mountains) | **V-shaped valleys**, **gorges** and **canyons** (Grand Canyon, Colorado River), **waterfalls**, rapids, **potholes** | — |
| Maturity (plains) | Wider valleys, **meanders** (loops) | **Alluvial fans** (where a river leaves the hills), **floodplains**, **natural levees** (raised banks), point bars |
| Old age (near the sea) | **Oxbow lakes** — a meander loop cut off | **Delta** (river splits into *distributaries* at the mouth), braided channels |

- **Delta types:** arcuate/fan-shaped (Nile, **Ganga–Brahmaputra**), bird's-foot (Mississippi), cuspate (Tiber).
- An **estuary** is a funnel-shaped mouth where sea tides wash sediment away, so no delta forms. **Narmada and Tapi** form estuaries; most east-flowing Indian rivers form deltas.
- The **Ganga–Brahmaputra delta** (Sundarbans) is the **largest delta in the world**.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar is a textbook of river landforms. The **Kosi** has built one of the world's largest **alluvial fans (megafan)** in north Bihar by swinging its course westward over centuries. **Kanwar Lake (Kabar Tal)** in Begusarai is described as **Asia's largest freshwater oxbow lake** and is a Ramsar site (2020). North Bihar's many *chaurs* and *mauns* (wetlands, oxbows) come from shifting rivers. See [Rivers, Floods and Climate of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/rivers-floods-and-climate-of-bihar).

### Groundwater (karst landforms in limestone)

Rainwater with dissolved CO₂ slowly dissolves limestone. Such regions are called **karst** (after a region in Slovenia).

- **Sinkholes**, **caves** and **lapies** (rough grooved surface).
- **Stalactites** hang from the cave ceiling; **stalagmites** rise from the floor; when they join they form **pillars**.
- Indian examples: Borra Caves (Andhra Pradesh), Belum Caves (AP), Mawsmai Caves (Meghalaya).

> **Memory trick:** stala**c**tite — **c**eiling; stala**g**mite — **g**round.

### Glaciers

| Erosional | Depositional |
|---|---|
| **Cirque** (armchair-shaped hollow; a lake in it is a *tarn*), **arête** (sharp ridge), **horn** (pyramid peak — Matterhorn), **U-shaped valley**, **hanging valley** (often with a waterfall), **fjord** (drowned glacial valley — Norway) | **Moraines** (unsorted debris: lateral, medial, terminal), **drumlins** (egg-shaped hills), **eskers** (winding ridges), **outwash plains** |

### Wind (mostly in deserts)

| Erosional | Depositional |
|---|---|
| **Deflation hollows**, **mushroom (pedestal) rocks**, **yardangs**, **inselbergs** (isolated rocky hills), zeugen | **Sand dunes** — **barchan** (crescent-shaped, horns point downwind — common in the Thar), **seif** (long ridges parallel to wind); **loess** (fine wind-blown silt, very fertile — **Loess Plateau, China**) |

### Sea waves

| Erosional | Depositional |
|---|---|
| **Sea cliffs**, wave-cut platforms, **sea caves**, **sea arches**, **sea stacks** (an arch collapses, leaving a pillar) | **Beaches**, **spits** (sand ridge joined to land at one end), **bars**, **lagoons** (water cut off by a bar — **Chilika**, Pulicat), **tombolo** (bar joining an island to land) |

## Mountains, plateaus and plains

| Type | How formed | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Fold mountains** | Compression folds rock layers (plate collision) | Young: **Himalaya, Alps, Andes, Rockies, Atlas**. Old: **Aravalli** (among the oldest in the world), Appalachians, Urals |
| **Block mountains** | Land between faults is raised (*horst*) or dropped (*graben* = rift valley) | Horst: Vosges (France), Black Forest (Germany), Sierra Nevada (USA). Graben: **East African Rift**, Rhine valley, **Narmada and Tapi valleys** (India) |
| **Volcanic mountains** | Built by lava and ash | **Kilimanjaro**, **Fujiyama**, Mt St Helens, Cotopaxi |
| **Residual mountains** | Remains of old mountains worn down | Aravalli, Nilgiri, Parasnath, Rajmahal hills |

**Plateaus** (high, mostly flat land): intermontane (enclosed by mountains — **Tibetan Plateau**, the highest and largest, the "**roof of the world**"), piedmont (at the foot of mountains), continental/lava (**Deccan Plateau**, Columbia Plateau).

**Plains** (low, flat land): mostly depositional — the **Indo-Gangetic plain** is one of the largest alluvial plains in the world.

## Quick revision

- Earth's mean radius 6,371 km; deepest borehole only ~12 km (Kola).
- P-waves: fastest, through solids + liquids; S-waves: solids only; surface waves most destructive.
- Shadow zone 105°–145°; liquid outer core proven by absence of S-waves.
- Moho = crust–mantle; Gutenberg = mantle–core (~2,900 km); Lehmann = outer–inner core (~5,150 km); Conrad = within crust.
- SIAL = continental crust; SIMA = oceanic crust; NIFE = core.
- Asthenosphere = semi-molten upper mantle; magma source; plates slide on it.
- Crust: O > Si > Al > Fe. Whole earth: Fe most abundant.
- Richter = magnitude (logarithmic, ×10); Mercalli = intensity (I–XII).
- Koyna 1967 reservoir-induced quake; Bihar–Nepal quake 15 Jan 1934; Bhuj 26 Jan 2001.
- Barren Island = India's only active volcano; Narcondam = dormant.
- Deccan Traps = flood basalt, ~66 million years ago.
- Limestone → marble; sandstone → quartzite; shale → slate; granite → gneiss.
- Fossils only in sedimentary rocks; granite intrusive, basalt extrusive.
- Wegener 1912 — Pangaea, Panthalassa, Laurasia, Gondwanaland, Tethys; Glossopteris, Mesosaurus.
- Holmes — convection currents; Hess — sea-floor spreading.
- Divergent: Mid-Atlantic Ridge; convergent: Himalaya, Andes, Mariana Trench; transform: San Andreas.
- Stalactite from ceiling, stalagmite from ground.
- Barchan = crescent dune; loess = wind-blown silt (China).
- Narmada, Tapi: rift valleys and estuaries.
- Kanwar Lake (Begusarai) = Asia's largest freshwater oxbow lake; Kosi megafan.

### Mains questions

**Q1. Explain the theory of plate tectonics. How does it explain the origin of the Himalaya and the earthquake risk in north Bihar? (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** lithosphere broken into rigid plates moving over the asthenosphere; theory grew from Wegener's drift (1912) and Hess's sea-floor spreading (1960s).
- **Body:** (1) plates and boundary types with one example each; (2) driving force — mantle convection, ridge push, slab pull; (3) India's northward journey from Gondwanaland across the Tethys; (4) collision with Eurasia ~40–50 million years ago, folding of Tethys sediments — marine fossils at height; (5) three parallel ranges (Himadri, Himachal, Shiwalik) formed in phases; (6) the depression in front filled by rivers → Ganga plain; (7) India still moving ~4–5 cm/year → stress builds → Himalayan earthquakes; (8) north Bihar: closeness to the Main Frontal Thrust, soft alluvium, high population density; 1934 and 2015 examples.
- **Conclusion:** plate tectonics explains both the gift (fertile plains, rivers) and the hazard (earthquakes) of north India; preparedness needs building codes, awareness and disaster-response capacity.

**Q2. "Bihar is highly vulnerable to earthquakes." Examine the causes and suggest measures for earthquake preparedness in the state.**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Bihar's location near the Himalayan collision belt; 1934 earthquake (magnitude ~8) and 2015 Nepal earthquake impacts.
- **Causes of vulnerability:** seismic zoning of north Bihar in the highest category; soft alluvial soil that amplifies shaking and can liquefy; high population density (Census 2011: 1,106 persons/km²); unplanned construction, few engineered buildings; flood-damaged structures weaker; low awareness.
- **Measures:** enforce BIS building codes (IS 1893 seismic code) in towns; retrofit schools, hospitals, government buildings; train masons; mock drills in schools; Bihar State Disaster Management Authority plans and SDRF capacity; early warning and communication; insurance; include earthquake safety in the school curriculum.
- **Conclusion:** earthquakes cannot be prevented, but deaths can — "earthquakes don't kill, buildings do".
