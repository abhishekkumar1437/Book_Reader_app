Soils decide what can be grown; vegetation and wildlife decide what we must protect. BPSC Prelims asks "which soil is found where and suits which crop", "which national park is in which state and famous for which animal", biosphere reserves, tiger reserves, Ramsar sites and wildlife projects. Mains GS II asks about soil degradation, deforestation and conservation, and Bihar's low forest cover. Many numbers here (tiger counts, Ramsar sites, protected areas) change every year, so they are marked "check the latest". Bihar's own forests and sanctuaries are covered in [Agriculture, Minerals and Natural Resources of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/agriculture-minerals-and-natural-resources-of-bihar).

> **In simple words:** Soil is the thin top layer of the land where plants grow. It forms very slowly from broken rock mixed with dead plants and animals. Different rocks and climates make different soils — river mud in the plains, black soil on lava rocks, red soil on old granite. Natural vegetation depends mainly on rainfall: thick evergreen forests where rain is heavy, deciduous forests in moderate rain, thorny bushes in dry areas. India protects its plants and animals in national parks, sanctuaries and biosphere reserves.

## How soil forms

**Pedology** is the science of soils. Soil-forming factors:

| Factor | Role |
|---|---|
| **Parent rock** | Gives minerals and colour (basalt → black soil; granite → red soil) |
| **Climate** | **Most important** in the long run — temperature and rainfall control weathering and leaching |
| Relief | Steep slopes have thin soil; valleys have deep soil |
| Living things | Plants, microbes, earthworms add **humus** (decayed organic matter) |
| Time | Hundreds to thousands of years to form a few centimetres |

## The eight soil types of India (ICAR classification)

The **Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR)** groups India's soils into eight major types.

| Soil | Where found | Properties | Suitable crops |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alluvial** | **Northern Plains** (Punjab to Assam, including **all of Bihar's plains**), river valleys and **deltas** of the east coast | **Most widespread** (about 40% of India's area); deposited by rivers; **rich in potash**, **poor in nitrogen and phosphorus**; **khadar** (new, fertile) and **bhangar** (old, with kankar) | Wheat, rice, sugarcane, maize, pulses, oilseeds, jute — the most productive soil |
| **Black (regur)** | **Deccan Trap** region — Maharashtra, Gujarat, western MP, north Karnataka, Telangana, parts of AP and Tamil Nadu | Formed from **basalt lava**; clayey, **holds moisture**; **swells when wet and cracks when dry** ("self-ploughing"); **rich in lime, iron, magnesia, potash**; **poor in nitrogen, phosphorus and humus** | **Cotton** ("**black cotton soil**"), sugarcane, soybean, jowar, tobacco, citrus |
| **Red and yellow** | On old crystalline rocks — **Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, AP, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand**, eastern MP, parts of **south Bihar** (plateau fringe) | **Red** from iron oxide; **yellow** when the iron is hydrated; sandy to loamy; **poor in nitrogen, phosphorus, humus** | Millets, pulses, groundnut, potato, rice with irrigation and fertiliser |
| **Laterite** | Areas of high temperature and heavy rain — **Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu hills, Odisha, Meghalaya, Assam hills**, parts of MP | Formed by **intense leaching** (rain washes away silica and lime); rich in **iron and aluminium**, poor in humus, nitrogen, lime; hardens on exposure — used as **bricks** (Latin *later* = brick) | **Cashew**, **tea, coffee, rubber**, coconut (with manure) |
| **Arid (desert)** | **Western Rajasthan**, Kutch | Sandy, saline, low humus and nitrogen; **kankar** layer lower down blocks water | Bajra, pulses, guar; with irrigation (Indira Gandhi Canal) wheat, cotton |
| **Saline and alkaline** (*usar, reh, kallar, thur*) | Western Gujarat, **Sundarbans**, deltas of the east coast; **Punjab–Haryana–west UP** where over-irrigation raised salts | Excess sodium, potassium, magnesium salts; infertile | Reclaimed with **gypsum**, drainage, salt-tolerant crops (barley, berseem) |
| **Peaty and marshy** | Heavy-rain and waterlogged areas — **Kerala (Kuttanad "kari" soil)**, coastal West Bengal, Odisha, Tamil Nadu, **northern Bihar** and southern Uttarakhand | Very high **organic matter**, black, acidic, heavy | Rice, jute (where drained) |
| **Forest and mountain** | Himalaya, Western and Eastern Ghats | Thin, acidic in high areas, rich humus in valleys | Tea, coffee, spices, fruits |

> **Prelims angle:** "Black soil is rich in ___" → **lime, iron, magnesia, potash**. "Alluvial soil is deficient in ___" → **nitrogen and phosphorus**. "Laterite soil results from ___" → **leaching under heavy rain and high temperature**. "Regur" is the local name for **black soil**; it got its colour mainly from **titaniferous magnetite** and iron.

> **Bihar connection:** The Bihar plains are almost all **alluvial**. North Bihar has newer, sandy-loam **khadar** with frequent fresh silt, and low-lying waterlogged **chaur** lands with **peaty, marshy** soil. South Bihar has older alluvium (**bhangar**), and in the south (Gaya, Nawada, Jamui, Banka, Kaimur, Rohtas plateau) **red and yellow** soils appear. The *tal* lands (Mokama, Barh) stay under water for months and grow rabi pulses later. See [Agriculture, Minerals and Natural Resources of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/agriculture-minerals-and-natural-resources-of-bihar).

## Soil erosion, degradation and conservation

**Soil erosion** is the removal of the top layer of soil by water or wind.

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Sheet erosion** | A thin layer removed evenly over a large area by flowing water |
| **Rill erosion** | Small finger-like channels |
| **Gully erosion** | Deep channels; land becomes useless — **badlands / ravines** of the **Chambal** (MP, Rajasthan, UP) |
| **Wind erosion** | In dry areas — Rajasthan, Kutch |
| River bank erosion | Big problem along the **Ganga and Kosi in Bihar** and the Brahmaputra in Assam |

**Causes:** deforestation, overgrazing, faulty farming on slopes, shifting cultivation (*jhum*), over-irrigation (salinity, waterlogging), mining, construction.

**Conservation methods:**
- **Contour ploughing** (ploughing along the contour lines of a slope)
- **Terrace (step) farming** on hills
- **Strip cropping** — alternating strips of crops and grass
- **Shelter belts** — rows of trees to cut wind speed (Rajasthan)
- **Check dams, gully plugging**, watershed development
- Afforestation, controlled grazing, crop rotation, mulching
- **Gypsum** for alkaline soils; drainage for waterlogged land

**Schemes and facts:**
- **Soil Health Card Scheme** launched in **2015** (at Suratgarh, Rajasthan) — gives farmers a card on nutrient status and fertiliser advice.
- **World Soil Day: 5 December.**
- ISRO's **Desertification and Land Degradation Atlas** shows nearly **a third** of India's land is degraded (check the latest edition). India has pledged to **restore 26 million hectares** of degraded land by **2030**.
- **National Bureau of Soil Survey and Land Use Planning** — **Nagpur**.

| **Tropical moist deciduous** | 100–200 cm | **North-East foothills, Western Ghats' east slopes, Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, north Bihar (Valmiki)** | Shed leaves in the dry season; **sal, teak, sheesham, bamboo, sandalwood, khair, kusum, arjun, mulberry** |
| **Tropical dry deciduous** | 70–100 cm | Rainier parts of the peninsula, UP and **Bihar plains** | **Teak, sal, peepal, neem, tendu** (leaves for bidis), palas, amaltas |
| **Monsoon deciduous (moist + dry)** together | — | — | **Most widespread forest type in India** and most important commercially |
| **Tropical thorn** | Below 70 cm | Rajasthan, Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana, rain-shadow Deccan | **Acacia (babool), khejri, kikar, cactus, euphorbias**; long roots, thick leaves |
| **Montane** | Varies with height | Himalaya, Nilgiri | **1,000–2,000 m**: wet temperate — oak, chestnut; **1,500–3,000 m**: **coniferous** — **pine, deodar, silver fir, spruce, cedar**; **above 3,600 m**: alpine meadows (*bugyals*), then mosses and lichens. In the south, **sholas** (temperate forests in valleys) of the Nilgiri and Anaimalai |
| **Littoral and swamp (mangrove)** | Coasts and deltas | **Sundarbans** (WB) — named after the **sundari tree**; **Bhitarkanika** (Odisha), Godavari–Krishna deltas, **Pichavaram** (Tamil Nadu), Andaman & Nicobar | Grow in salty, tidal water; **breathing roots (pneumatophores)**; protect coasts from cyclones and tsunamis |

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar has **one of the lowest forest covers** among states — under 10% of its area (check the latest ISFR) — because almost all of it is densely populated farmland. Most forests lie in **West Champaran (Valmiki)** and in the southern hills (**Kaimur, Rohtas, Gaya, Nawada, Jamui, Munger, Banka**). After Jharkhand separated in **2000**, Bihar lost most of its forests and minerals. Bihar's **Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali** mission (2019) includes large tree-planting drives. Mains answers on "environment of Bihar" should mention this gap.

## Biodiversity and its protection

India has about **2.4% of the world's land** but about **7–8% of recorded species**, and is one of the world's **17 megadiverse countries**.

**Biodiversity hotspots in India (4):** **Himalaya**, **Indo-Burma** (North-East), **Western Ghats and Sri Lanka**, **Sundaland** (Nicobar Islands).

### Types of protected areas

| Type | Law | Protection |
|---|---|---|
| **National Park** | Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 | Highest; **no human activity** like grazing or private rights allowed |
| **Wildlife Sanctuary** | Same Act | Some activities (grazing, collection of minor produce) may be allowed |
| Conservation Reserve | Added in 2002 | Between protected areas; government land |
| Community Reserve | Added in 2002 | Community or private land managed with local people |
| **Biosphere Reserve** | Not under the WPA; under UNESCO's **Man and the Biosphere (MAB)** programme (1971) | Large areas with three zones — **core** (strictly protected), **buffer** (research, education), **transition** (human settlements, sustainable use) |
| Tiger Reserve | Notified by the state on NTCA advice (Section 38V, WPA) | Core and buffer zones |

India has about **106 national parks** and over **570 wildlife sanctuaries** (numbers change — check the latest).

### Tiger reserves and Project Tiger

- **Project Tiger** started on **1 April 1973** with **9 reserves**; managed by the **National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA)** since 2006.
- India has about **58 tiger reserves** (as of 2025 — check the latest).
- **Largest:** **Nagarjunsagar–Srisailam** (Andhra Pradesh–Telangana).
- **All India Tiger Estimation 2022** (results 2023): minimum **3,167** tigers (average estimate about **3,682**) — about **75% of the world's wild tigers**. **Madhya Pradesh** had the most, then Karnataka and Uttarakhand. The next estimation (2026) results may be out by exam time — check.
- **Valmiki Tiger Reserve** (Bihar) shows a rising tiger count (about 54 in 2022 — check the latest).

### Wetlands and Ramsar sites

- The **Ramsar Convention** on wetlands was signed at **Ramsar, Iran, in 1971**; India joined in **1982**. **World Wetlands Day: 2 February.**
- India's first Ramsar sites: **Chilika** (Odisha) and **Keoladeo** (Rajasthan), 1981.
- India had **more than 90 Ramsar sites** by 2025 — the largest network in Asia; **Tamil Nadu** has the most; **Sundarbans** is the largest site (check the latest count).

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar's Ramsar sites (check the latest): **Kanwar (Kabar Tal) Lake**, Begusarai (2020 — the first); **Nagi** and **Nakti** bird sanctuaries, Jamui (2024); **Gokul Jalashay** (Buxar) and **Udaipur Jheel** (West Champaran) (2025). Bihar's main sanctuaries include **Valmiki**, **Kaimur**, **Bhimbandh** (Munger, hot springs), **Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary** (Bhagalpur), **Gautam Buddha** (Gaya), **Rajgir**, **Udaipur** (West Champaran) and **Kanwar Jheel** bird sanctuary.

| Law / policy | Year | Note |
|---|---|---|

**IUCN Red List** categories (from worst): Extinct → Extinct in the Wild → **Critically Endangered** → **Endangered** → **Vulnerable** → Near Threatened → Least Concern. Indian examples: **Critically Endangered** — Great Indian Bustard, gharial, Jerdon's courser; **Endangered** — tiger, Asian elephant, Gangetic dolphin, red panda, lion-tailed macaque; **Vulnerable** — one-horned rhino, snow leopard (check the latest Red List).

## Quick revision

- ICAR: 8 soil types; alluvial most widespread (~40%), rich in potash, poor in N and P.
- Black (regur): Deccan Trap basalt; cotton; rich in lime, iron, magnesia, potash; self-ploughing.
- Red: iron oxide; yellow when hydrated; poor in N, P, humus.
- Laterite: leaching; cashew, tea, coffee; bricks.
- Peaty: Kerala, coastal WB/Odisha, north Bihar.
- Saline: usar/reh/kallar; cure with gypsum.
- Chambal ravines = gully erosion.
- Soil Health Card 2015; World Soil Day 5 Dec; NBSS&LUP Nagpur.
- Monsoon deciduous = most widespread forest; sal and teak.
- Sundarbans named after the sundari tree; Bhitarkanika mangroves.
- ISFR 2023: forest 21.76%, forest + tree 25.17%; MP largest by area; Mizoram highest % among states; target 33%.

### Mains questions

**Q1. Classify the soils of India and explain the problems of soil degradation. Suggest measures for soil conservation with reference to Bihar. (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** soil as a basic resource; ICAR's eight types.
- **Classification:** a table of the eight types — region, properties, crops (alluvial, black, red & yellow, laterite, arid, saline, peaty, forest).
- **Degradation:** water and wind erosion, gullies (Chambal), salinity and waterlogging (Punjab–Haryana), nutrient mining by intensive cropping, loss of organic carbon, chemical overuse, desertification (nearly a third of land degraded per ISRO atlas).
- **Bihar:** riverbank erosion along the Ganga and Kosi; sand deposits after floods on farmland; waterlogging in chaurs and tal lands; low organic carbon; acidic patches in the north-east; plateau-fringe erosion in the south.
- **Measures:** Soil Health Cards and balanced fertiliser use; organic and natural farming (Bihar's organic corridor along the Ganga); contour bunds and check dams in south Bihar; drainage of waterlogged land; bank protection; agroforestry under Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali; crop rotation with pulses.
- **Conclusion:** "a nation that destroys its soil destroys itself" — conservation is an investment in food security.

**Q2. Discuss the measures taken in India for wildlife conservation. How successful have they been? Give examples, including from Bihar.**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** India's megadiversity and four hotspots; pressures from habitat loss, poaching, conflict.
- **Legal:** WPA 1972 (amended 2022), Forest Conservation Act, Biological Diversity Act 2002, Forest Rights Act 2006, 42nd Amendment.
- **Institutional:** national parks, sanctuaries, tiger and elephant reserves, biosphere reserves (18), Ramsar sites, NTCA, Wildlife Crime Control Bureau.
- **Projects:** Tiger (1973), Crocodile (1975), Elephant (1992), Rhino Vision, Cheetah (2022), Dolphin (2020).
- **Successes:** tiger numbers rose to ~3,167+ (2022); rhino recovery in Kaziranga; lions in Gir; olive ridley protection.
- **Gaps:** human–wildlife conflict, fragmented corridors, linear infrastructure, Great Indian Bustard decline, cheetah mortality, invasive species.
- **Bihar:** Valmiki tiger recovery and its link with Chitwan; Vikramshila dolphin sanctuary; Ramsar sites at Kanwar, Nagi–Nakti and others; gharials in the Gandak; low forest cover remains the main challenge.
- **Conclusion:** conservation succeeds where local communities benefit (eco-development, eco-tourism) — "people-centred conservation".
