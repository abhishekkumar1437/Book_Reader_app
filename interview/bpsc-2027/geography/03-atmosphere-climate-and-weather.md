This chapter is the science behind weather. It is also the foundation for the Indian monsoon (chapter 7), so do not skip it. BPSC Prelims regularly asks about layers of the atmosphere (ozone in the stratosphere, radio waves in the ionosphere), pressure belts and winds, local winds (Loo, Chinook, Sirocco), types of rainfall, cyclones and El Niño. Mains uses it for questions on climate change, heat waves, lightning and cyclones, all of which have a clear Bihar angle.

> **In simple words:** The atmosphere is a blanket of air around the earth. The Sun heats the ground, the ground heats the air, and hot air rises. Where air rises, pressure is low; where it sinks, pressure is high. Air flows from high to low pressure — that is wind. The earth's spin bends the wind. Rising air cools and its moisture forms clouds and rain. Weather is today's condition of the air; climate is the average over about 30 years.

## Weather and climate

| Weather | Climate |
|---|---|
| State of the atmosphere at a place and time (hours, days) | Average weather of a place over a long period — usually **30 years** or more |
| Changes quickly | Changes slowly |
| Example: "Patna is foggy today" | Example: "Bihar has a humid subtropical monsoon climate" |

Elements of weather and climate: **temperature, pressure, wind, humidity, clouds, precipitation**.

## Composition of the atmosphere

<div class="formula">

| Gas | Share by volume (dry air) | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrogen** | ~78.08% | Most abundant; needed for proteins (via nitrogen fixation) |
| **Oxygen** | ~20.95% | Breathing, burning |
| **Argon** | ~0.93% | Third most abundant; inert gas |
| **Carbon dioxide** | ~0.04% (about 420 ppm and rising — check latest) | Greenhouse gas; photosynthesis |
| Others | Neon, helium, methane, krypton, hydrogen, **ozone** (tiny) | Ozone absorbs ultraviolet rays |

</div>

- **Water vapour** varies from almost 0 to about 4% of the air; it is highest in warm, humid tropics. It absorbs heat and is a greenhouse gas.
- **Dust particles** act as nuclei on which water vapour condenses (needed for clouds and rain).
- About **half the mass** of the atmosphere lies below about 5.5 km; most of it lies within about 30 km.

| Layer | Height (approx.) | Main facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Troposphere** | Surface to **~8 km at the poles** and **~18 km at the equator** (thicker at the equator because strong heating pushes air up) | Almost all **weather**, clouds, water vapour and dust; temperature falls about **6.5 °C per km** (**normal lapse rate**); top boundary = **tropopause** |
| **Stratosphere** | Tropopause to ~50 km | Temperature **rises** with height because **ozone absorbs ultraviolet rays**; **ozone layer** about 15–35 km; calm, so jet aircraft fly in its lower part |
| **Mesosphere** | ~50 to ~80–85 km | **Coldest** layer (about −90 °C near the top); **meteors burn** here |
| **Thermosphere** | ~85 km to ~600 km | Temperature rises sharply; contains the **ionosphere** (charged particles) which **reflects radio waves** back to earth; **auroras** occur here; the International Space Station orbits at about 400 km |
| **Exosphere** | Above ~600 km | Extremely thin air; light gases (hydrogen, helium) escape to space |

> **Prelims angle:** Three favourite one-liners — weather phenomena: **troposphere**; ozone and jet planes: **stratosphere**; radio waves: **ionosphere** (in the thermosphere). The tropopause is higher over the equator than over the poles.

## Heat: insolation and the heat budget

**Insolation** (*incoming solar radiation*) is the energy received from the Sun, mostly as short waves. The earth then gives out heat as long waves (**terrestrial radiation**). The atmosphere is heated mainly **from below** — by the ground — not directly by the Sun.

**Heat budget** (NCERT model, taking insolation at the top of the atmosphere as 100 units):
- About **35 units** are reflected back to space without heating anything. This reflectivity is the earth's **albedo** (~0.3–0.35). Snow and ice have very high albedo; forests and oceans low.
- About **65 units** are absorbed — about 14 by the atmosphere and 51 by the earth's surface.
- The earth radiates the same 65 units back over time, so the planet does not keep getting hotter (until greenhouse gases rise).

**Ways heat moves:**

| Process | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Conduction | Heat passes by contact | Ground warms the air touching it |
| **Convection** | Vertical movement — warm air rises | Afternoon thunderstorms |
| **Advection** | **Horizontal** movement of heat by wind | The **Loo** of north India in May–June |
| Radiation | Heat travels as waves | Terrestrial radiation at night |

### Factors controlling temperature

Latitude, altitude (higher = colder), distance from the sea (*continentality* — coasts have mild, "maritime" climates; interiors have extreme climates), ocean currents, winds, relief and cloud cover.

- **Isotherms** are lines joining places of equal temperature.
- **Temperature inversion** — temperature **increases** with height instead of falling. It happens on **long, clear, calm winter nights** when the ground loses heat fast and cools the air above it. Results: **fog**, frost in valleys, and trapped smoke and dust (**smog** in Delhi and the north Indian plains, including Patna, in winter).

## Atmospheric pressure

**Pressure** is the weight of the column of air above a place. Average sea-level pressure is **1013.25 millibars (hPa)**. It is measured with a **barometer** (mercury or aneroid). Lines of equal pressure are **isobars**.

- Pressure falls with height.
- Hot air expands and rises → **low pressure**. Cold air contracts and sinks → **high pressure**.
- Wind blows from **high pressure to low pressure**. The difference in pressure over distance is the **pressure gradient**; the steeper it is (isobars close together), the stronger the wind.

| Belt | Latitude | Origin | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Equatorial low** | 0°–5° N and S | **Thermal** (intense heating, air rises) | Calm, light winds — "**doldrums**"; zone where trade winds meet = **Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)**; heavy convectional rain |
| **Subtropical high** | ~30°–35° N and S | **Dynamic** (air that rose at the equator sinks here) | Calm, dry, sinking air — "**horse latitudes**"; most hot deserts lie near these latitudes on the **western sides of continents** |
| **Subpolar low** | ~60°–65° N and S | Dynamic (earth's rotation spins air outward) | Stormy; meeting of westerlies and polar easterlies (polar front) |
| **Polar high** | 90° N and S | Thermal (extreme cold) | Cold, dense, sinking air |

These belts **shift north and south with the overhead Sun** — northward in the northern summer (June), southward in the northern winter. This seasonal shift is central to the monsoon.

### Coriolis force and Ferrel's law

Because the earth rotates, winds are deflected — to the **right in the Northern Hemisphere** and to the **left in the Southern Hemisphere** (**Ferrel's law**). The force is **zero at the equator** and maximum at the poles. That is why tropical cyclones **do not form within about 5° of the equator**.

- **Buys Ballot's law:** in the Northern Hemisphere, if you stand with your back to the wind, low pressure is on your left.

### Planetary (permanent) winds

| Wind | Blows from → to | Direction | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trade winds** | Subtropical high → equatorial low | **NE trades** (north), **SE trades** (south) | Steady; named from old sailing trade; bring rain to **eastern** sides of continents in the tropics |
| **Westerlies** | Subtropical high → subpolar low | SW (north), NW (south) | Stronger and steadier in the Southern Hemisphere (less land): **Roaring Forties** (40°S), **Furious Fifties** (50°S), **Shrieking (Screaming) Sixties** (60°S); bring rain to **western** sides of continents in temperate latitudes (Britain, Chile) |
| **Polar easterlies** | Polar high → subpolar low | NE (north), SE (south) | Cold, dry |

**Three-cell model:** **Hadley cell** (equator to 30°, thermal), **Ferrel cell** (30° to 60°), **Polar cell** (60° to pole).

### Periodic (seasonal) winds

- **Monsoons** — winds that reverse direction with seasons (from the Arabic *mausim*, season). Explained fully in [Climate and Monsoon of India](/interview/bpsc-2027/geography/climate-and-monsoon-of-india).
- **Land and sea breezes** — by day the land heats faster → low pressure over land → **sea breeze** blows onto land; at night the opposite → **land breeze**.
- **Mountain and valley breezes** — by day air rises up slopes (**valley breeze, anabatic**); at night cold air slides down (**mountain breeze, katabatic**), causing frost in valleys.

### Jet streams

**Jet streams** are narrow belts of very fast winds (often 150–300 km/h) in the upper troposphere, about **9–14 km** high.

| Jet | Where | Role for India |
|---|---|---|
| **Subtropical westerly jet** | Around 25°–35°N | In **winter it blows south of the Himalaya** and steers **western disturbances** into north India; in summer it shifts north of the Tibetan Plateau, which helps the monsoon to set in |
| **Tropical easterly jet** | Around 15°N over India and Africa, in **summer** | Linked to strong south-west monsoon |
| Polar front jet | 40°–60° | Steers temperate cyclones |

## Moisture: humidity, clouds and rain

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Humidity** | Water vapour in the air |
| Absolute humidity | Actual amount (grams per cubic metre) |
| **Relative humidity** | Water vapour present as a percentage of the most the air can hold at that temperature; 100% = **saturated** |
| **Dew point** | Temperature at which air becomes saturated and condensation starts |
| Condensation forms | Dew, frost (if below 0 °C), mist, **fog**, clouds |

### Clouds

| Cloud | Height | Look | Weather |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cirrus** | High (8–12 km) | Thin, feathery, made of ice crystals | Fair weather |
| **Cumulus** | Low–middle | Cotton-wool heaps with flat base | Fair; can grow into storm clouds |
| **Stratus** | Low | Flat grey layers | Dull, drizzle |
| **Nimbus** (nimbostratus) | Low–middle | Dark grey, thick | Continuous rain |
| **Cumulonimbus** | From low up to the tropopause | Tall, anvil-shaped tower | **Thunderstorms, lightning, hail, cloudbursts** |

## Air masses, fronts and cyclones

An **air mass** is a huge body of air with nearly the same temperature and humidity throughout (e.g. *maritime tropical*, *continental polar*). A **front** is the boundary between two air masses — cold front, warm front, stationary front, occluded front.

### Tropical cyclones

A **tropical cyclone** is an intense low-pressure system over warm tropical seas, with winds spiralling inward.

Conditions needed:
1. Large sea surface with temperature above about **27 °C**.
2. **Coriolis force** strong enough to spin the air — so not between about 5°N and 5°S.
3. Little change in wind speed with height (low vertical **wind shear**).
4. A pre-existing weak low-pressure area.
5. Upper-level divergence that removes rising air.

Structure: a calm, clear centre called the **eye**; around it the **eye wall**, with the strongest winds and heaviest rain. Winds spin **anticlockwise in the Northern Hemisphere** and clockwise in the Southern. When a cyclone hits land (**landfall**) it brings strong winds, heavy rain and a **storm surge** (a sea-water wall pushed onto the coast). Over land it weakens because it loses its moisture supply.

| Name | Region |
|---|---|
| **Cyclone** | Indian Ocean (Bay of Bengal, Arabian Sea) |
| **Hurricane** | Atlantic Ocean, Caribbean, eastern Pacific |
| **Typhoon** | Western Pacific (China, Japan, Philippines) |
| Willy-willy (old term) | Australia |

**IMD classification** of disturbances in the north Indian Ocean (by maximum sustained wind):

| Category | Wind speed (km/h) |
|---|---|
| Depression | 31–49 |
| Deep depression | 50–61 |
| Cyclonic storm | 62–88 |
| Severe cyclonic storm | 89–117 |
| Very severe cyclonic storm | 118–166 |
| Extremely severe cyclonic storm | 167–221 |
| Super cyclonic storm | 222 and above |

- Names for north Indian Ocean cyclones come from a list prepared by **13 member countries** of the WMO/ESCAP Panel (India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Pakistan, Maldives, Oman, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Iran, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Yemen); the current list (2020) has 169 names, used in order.
- The **Bay of Bengal** gets more cyclones than the Arabian Sea (warmer water, cyclones from the Pacific, funnel shape). The **Odisha super cyclone of 1999** is the strongest recorded in the region in recent decades.

### Temperate (extra-tropical) cyclones

| Tropical cyclone | Temperate cyclone |
|---|---|
| Forms over warm seas, 5°–30° | Forms in mid-latitudes, 35°–65°, over land and sea |
| No fronts | Formed along **fronts** |
| Smaller, very violent | Larger, less violent |
| Moves east → west (with trade winds) | Moves **west → east** (with westerlies) |
| Dies over land | Can travel over land |

**Western disturbances** that bring winter rain and snow to north India are temperate-type low-pressure systems that start over the **Mediterranean** region.

- **Anticyclone:** high-pressure system; winds blow outward, **clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere**; clear, dry, calm weather.
- **Tornado:** a small, extremely violent funnel of wind over land (common in the USA's "Tornado Alley"); over water it is a **waterspout**.

## El Niño, La Niña and the Indian Ocean Dipole

**Walker circulation** — normally, trade winds push warm surface water west across the Pacific towards Indonesia and Australia. Cold water rises (*upwelling*) off **Peru**, giving rich fishing.

| Phenomenon | What happens | Usual effect on India |
|---|---|---|
| **El Niño** ("the little boy / Christ child" — appears near Christmas) | Trade winds weaken; warm water moves **east** to the central and eastern Pacific off Peru | Often **weakens the south-west monsoon** — droughts in 2002, 2009, 2015 were El Niño years; heavy rain and floods in Peru, drought in Australia |
| **La Niña** ("the little girl") | Stronger trade winds; eastern Pacific becomes **colder** than normal | Usually **good or excess monsoon**; cold winters in north India |
| **Southern Oscillation** | See-saw of air pressure between **Tahiti** (east Pacific) and **Darwin** (Australia) | El Niño + Southern Oscillation together = **ENSO** |
| **Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)** | Difference in sea temperature between the western Indian Ocean (near Africa) and the eastern Indian Ocean (near Indonesia) | **Positive IOD** (west warmer) usually **helps** the monsoon and can offset El Niño (e.g. 2019); negative IOD hurts it |

> **Mains angle:** For any question on monsoon variability, drought in Bihar, or food security, use "ENSO + IOD" as one body point: India's monsoon is linked to ocean–atmosphere systems thousands of kilometres away, so forecasting (IMD's long-range forecast) and contingency crop plans matter.

## World climate types (Köppen and NCERT)

**Köppen** classified climates by temperature and rainfall with letters: **A** tropical humid, **B** dry, **C** warm temperate, **D** cold (snow forest), **E** polar, and **H** highland. Small letters add detail (e.g. *w* = dry winter, *s* = dry summer).

| Climate | Where | Features | Life and economy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Equatorial** (Af) | Amazon basin, Congo basin, Indonesia, Malaysia | Hot and wet all year; daily afternoon convectional rain; no real seasons | Dense evergreen rainforest ("selva" in Amazon); Pygmies of Congo |
| **Tropical monsoon** (Am) | India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Thailand, Vietnam | Wet summer, dry winter | Rice, jute, tea |
| **Savanna** (Aw) | Africa (Sudan, Kenya), Brazil (Campos), Venezuela (Llanos) | Hot; wet summer, long dry season | Tall grass with scattered trees; big game animals; Maasai herders |
| **Hot desert** (BWh) | **Western sides** of continents at 15°–30°: Sahara, Kalahari, Namib, Atacama, Arabian, Thar, Great Australian | Very low rain, huge day–night temperature range | Oases, nomadic herding (Bedouins, Tuaregs); **Atacama is among the driest places on earth** |
| **Mediterranean** (Cs) | **Western sides** at 30°–40°: Mediterranean basin, California, central Chile, Cape Town, south-west Australia | **Rain in winter**, dry sunny summer | Citrus fruits, grapes and wine, olives — "**orchard lands of the world**" |
| **China type / humid subtropical** (Cfa) | Eastern sides at 25°–35°: south-east China, south-east USA, south Brazil | Hot humid summer, rain throughout | Rice, cotton, tea |
| **British type / west European** (Cfb) | Western sides at 45°–60°: Britain, France, Norway, New Zealand, British Columbia | Mild, cloudy, rain all year from westerlies | Dairy farming, mixed farming |
| **Temperate grassland / steppe** | Continental interiors: Prairies, Steppes, Pampas, Veld, Downs | Hot summer, cold winter, low rain | "**Granaries of the world**" — wheat, ranching |
| **Taiga / boreal** (Dfc) | Siberia, Canada, Scandinavia (Northern Hemisphere only) | Long, very cold winter, short summer | **Coniferous softwood forests** — timber, paper pulp |
| **Tundra** (ET) | Arctic coasts of Canada, Alaska, Russia, Greenland | Very cold; frozen subsoil (*permafrost*) | Mosses, lichens; Inuit, Sami (Lapps); reindeer |

> **Bihar connection:** Most of Bihar has a **humid subtropical climate with a dry winter** (Köppen **Cwa**, written **Cwg** in NCERT), with the south-west monsoon giving most of the rain. Details of Bihar's seasons, rainfall and agro-climatic zones are in [Rivers, Floods and Climate of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/rivers-floods-and-climate-of-bihar).

## Greenhouse effect, climate change and the ozone layer

### Greenhouse effect

The atmosphere lets most short-wave sunlight in but **traps part of the long-wave heat** the earth sends back. This natural **greenhouse effect** keeps the earth's average temperature about 15 °C instead of about −18 °C.

**Greenhouse gases (GHGs):** water vapour, **carbon dioxide** (biggest human-caused contributor), **methane** (from paddy fields, cattle, landfills; much stronger than CO₂ per molecule), **nitrous oxide** (fertilisers), CFCs and HFCs, ozone near the ground.

Burning coal, oil and gas, deforestation and some farm practices have raised GHG levels, causing **global warming** and **climate change** — heat waves, erratic monsoons, melting Himalayan glaciers, sea-level rise, more extreme cyclones.

| Agreement | Year | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| UNFCCC | 1992 (Rio Earth Summit) | Framework convention; yearly COP meetings |
| Kyoto Protocol | 1997 | Binding cuts for developed countries |
| **Paris Agreement** | 2015 | Keep warming "well below 2 °C", try for **1.5 °C**; countries set **NDCs** |
| India's updated NDC | 2022 | Reduce emissions intensity of GDP by 45% by 2030 from the 2005 level; about 50% of installed power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030; **net zero by 2070** (announced at COP26, Glasgow) |

For the full list of conventions, see [Environment Conventions and Protected Areas](/interview/bpsc-2027/current-affairs/environment-conventions-and-protected-areas).

### Ozone layer

- **Ozone (O₃)** in the stratosphere absorbs harmful **ultraviolet (UV-B)** rays that cause skin cancer and cataracts and harm crops.
- It is measured in **Dobson Units**.
- It is destroyed by **CFCs** (chlorofluorocarbons, from old fridges and sprays), halons and similar chemicals. The worst thinning — the "**ozone hole**" — appears over **Antarctica** each spring (September–October).
- **Vienna Convention** 1985; **Montreal Protocol** **1987** — phases out ozone-depleting substances; considered the most successful environmental treaty. **Kigali Amendment** (2016) adds phasing down of **HFCs** (which are not ozone-depleting but are strong greenhouse gases).
- **World Ozone Day: 16 September.**

> **Bihar connection:** Climate change shows up in Bihar as erratic monsoons (floods in the north and drought in the south in the same year), more heat-wave days, and **lightning** — Bihar records some of the highest lightning deaths in India, mostly among farmers working in open fields during pre-monsoon and monsoon storms. The state runs lightning-warning systems for mobile phones (check the latest). Bihar's climate actions under **Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali** are covered in [Government Schemes and Policies of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/government-schemes-and-policies-of-bihar).

### Mains questions

**Q1. What is a tropical cyclone? Explain the conditions of its formation and suggest measures to reduce cyclone damage on India's east coast. (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** intense low-pressure system over warm tropical seas; called cyclone, hurricane or typhoon by region.
- **Conditions:** sea surface temperature above ~27 °C; Coriolis force (beyond 5° latitude); low vertical wind shear; pre-existing disturbance; upper divergence; continuous moisture supply.
- **Structure:** eye, eye wall, spiral rain bands; storm surge at landfall.
- **Why the east coast suffers more:** warmer Bay of Bengal, remnants of Pacific typhoons, shallow coast and funnel-shaped bay raise surges (Odisha 1999).
- **Measures:** IMD early warning and naming; cyclone shelters and evacuation (Odisha's near-zero-casualty model in later cyclones such as Phailin 2013, Fani 2019); mangrove belts (Bhitarkanika, Sundarbans) as natural barriers; National Cyclone Risk Mitigation Project; coastal zoning and resilient housing; insurance; community volunteers.
- **Conclusion:** cyclones will intensify with warmer seas; preparation saves lives — shift from relief to risk reduction (Sendai Framework).

**Q2. Discuss the likely impacts of climate change on the agriculture and people of Bihar, and suggest adaptation measures.**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Bihar's economy depends heavily on agriculture and on the monsoon; it is a flood-prone north and drought-prone south.
- **Impacts:** erratic monsoon onset and breaks; more intense rain → floods in north Bihar (Kosi, Gandak, Bagmati basins); rainfall deficit in south Bihar (Gaya, Nawada, Aurangabad); higher temperatures cut wheat yield at grain filling (terminal heat); heat waves and Loo; more lightning deaths; groundwater stress; health impacts (heat stroke, vector-borne disease); migration.
- **Adaptation:** climate-resilient seed varieties and early wheat sowing; zero tillage; crop diversification (maize, makhana, pulses); water harvesting (ahar–pyne revival, Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali); weather-based crop insurance and agro-advisories; lightning warnings and awareness; tree cover expansion; heat-action plans for cities.
- **Conclusion:** Bihar contributes little to global emissions but is highly vulnerable; adaptation must be built into every district plan.
