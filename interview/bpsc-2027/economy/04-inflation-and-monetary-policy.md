Inflation is one of the most asked economy topics in every state PSC. Prelims asks which body publishes CPI or WPI, the base years, the meaning of terms like stagflation and core inflation, and what happens when the RBI raises the repo rate. Mains asks about the effect of food inflation on the poor and the role of monetary policy.

> **In simple words:** Inflation means a general and continuous rise in prices, so the same ₹100 buys less. The RBI fights inflation by making loans costlier (raising the repo rate) and by taking extra money out of the system. When prices are falling and growth is weak, it does the opposite.

## What is inflation?

**Inflation** is a **sustained rise in the general price level** of goods and services over a period. It reduces the **purchasing power** of money.

**Inflation rate (%) = (Price index this year − Price index last year) ÷ Price index last year × 100**

### Worked example: purchasing power

If inflation is 6%, something that costs ₹100 today will cost ₹106 next year. Your ₹100 note will buy only 100 ÷ 1.06 ≈ **₹94.3** worth of today's goods next year.

**Rule of 70:** prices double in about **70 ÷ inflation rate** years. At 7% inflation, prices double in about **10 years**.

### Related terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Deflation** | Fall in the general price level (negative inflation) |
| **Disinflation** | Inflation rate falls, but prices still rise (e.g. from 7% to 4%) |
| **Reflation** | Deliberate policy to raise prices/demand after a deflation or slump |
| **Stagflation** | High inflation + low growth + high unemployment together (1970s oil shocks) |
| **Skewflation** | Rise in prices of a few items only (e.g. onion, tomato) while the rest stay stable |
| **Headline inflation** | Inflation of the full CPI basket |
| **Core inflation** | Headline inflation excluding volatile **food and fuel** items |
| **Base effect** | A low/high price level last year makes this year's percentage change look high/low |
| **Shrinkflation** | Same price, smaller packet size |
| **Hyperinflation** | Extremely fast inflation (often 50%+ per month) — Germany 1923, Zimbabwe 2008, Venezuela |

### Speed of inflation

| Type | Approx. rate per year |
|---|---|
| **Creeping** | Below 3% |
| **Walking / trotting** | 3–10% |
| **Running / galloping** | 10–20% and above (double digits) |
| **Hyperinflation** | Extremely high, out of control |

> **Common mistake:** **Disinflation** is not deflation. If inflation falls from 6% to 3%, prices are still rising — that is disinflation.

### Worked example: the base effect

| Month | Price index | Change over last year |
|---|---|---|
| Sept last year | 100 (prices had fallen) | — |
| Sept this year | 106 | **6%** |

If last September prices had been unusually **high** at 104, the same 106 would show only **1.9%** inflation. Nothing changed this year — only last year's base. Newspapers say "inflation fell due to a favourable base effect".

## Causes of inflation

### Demand-pull inflation

"Too much money chasing too few goods." Demand rises faster than supply.

Causes: rise in money supply, higher government spending, tax cuts, rise in incomes, easy credit, rising exports, black money.

### Cost-push inflation

Production costs rise, so sellers raise prices.

Causes: rise in **crude oil** prices, higher wages, costlier raw materials, **indirect taxes**, rupee depreciation (imports costlier), supply shocks — drought, floods, pest attack.

### Structural causes in India

- **Food inflation** — monsoon dependence, weak storage and cold chains, middlemen, small market size.
- **Supply bottlenecks** — roads, power, logistics costs.
- **Imported inflation** — India imports about **85%** of its crude oil.

<div class="formula">

| Demand-pull | Cost-push |
|---|---|
| Starts on the buyer side | Starts on the seller side |
| More money / income / credit | Higher input costs (oil, wages, taxes) |
| Cure: tight monetary & fiscal policy | Cure: supply-side measures (imports, storage, tax cuts on inputs) |
| Usually with rising output | Can reduce output → stagflation |

</div>

## Effects of inflation

| Group | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Fixed-income earners** (pensioners, salaried, daily wage workers) | **Lose** — real income falls |
| **Borrowers / debtors** | **Gain** — repay in cheaper money |
| **Lenders / creditors, savers** | **Lose** — real value of repayment and savings falls |
| **Business / producers** | Often gain in mild inflation (profits rise) |
| **Farmers** | Gain if crop prices rise faster than input costs; lose if inputs rise faster |
| **Government** | Gains on debt; tax collection rises; but subsidy bills may rise |
| **Exporters** | Lose competitiveness if Indian prices rise faster than abroad |
| **The poor** | Lose most — spend about **half** of income on food |

**Real interest rate ≈ Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate.** If your FD pays 7% and inflation is 6%, your real return is only about **1%**. If inflation is 8%, your real return is **−1%**.

> **Mains angle:** Inflation is called a "**tax on the poor**" because poor households have no inflation-protected assets and spend most of their income on food. Use this in Bihar answers: high share of food in Bihar's consumption basket means food inflation hurts Bihar's households more.

### The Phillips curve

The **Phillips curve** (A.W. Phillips, 1958) shows a short-run **trade-off between inflation and unemployment**: lower unemployment comes with higher inflation. In the long run, and during stagflation, the trade-off breaks down.

## Measuring inflation in India

### Consumer Price Index (CPI)

CPI measures change in the **retail prices** paid by consumers for a fixed **basket** of goods and services.

| Index | Published by | Base year |
|---|---|---|
| **CPI (Rural), CPI (Urban), CPI (Combined)** | **NSO, MoSPI** | **2024** (new series from February 2026; earlier 2012) — check the latest |
| **CPI-IW (Industrial Workers)** | **Labour Bureau** (Ministry of Labour, Shimla) | 2016 |
| **CPI-AL / CPI-RL (Agricultural / Rural Labourers)** | **Labour Bureau** | Older base 1986-87; revision under way — check the latest |

- **CPI (Combined)** is the **headline inflation** measure and the RBI's **target** (since **2014**, on the **Urjit Patel Committee** recommendation).
- CPI includes **services** (education, health, transport, housing in urban index).
- **Food and beverages** have the largest weight. In the old 2012 series this was about **46%**; in the new 2024 series, based on the **Household Consumption Expenditure Survey 2023-24**, the food weight is lower because households now spend a smaller share on food (check exact weights).
- CPI-IW is used to calculate **Dearness Allowance (DA)** of government employees; CPI-AL is used to revise minimum wages for farm labour.

### Wholesale Price Index (WPI)

| Point | WPI |
|---|---|
| Published by | **Office of the Economic Adviser, DPIIT (Ministry of Commerce and Industry)** |
| Base year | **2011-12** (revision to a newer base is in progress — check the latest) |
| Covers | **Goods only** — no services |
| Groups | Primary articles (~23%), fuel & power (~13%), **manufactured products (~64%)** |
| Frequency | Monthly |

> **Prelims angle:** Classic pair: **CPI → MoSPI (NSO); WPI → DPIIT (Commerce Ministry).** WPI does **not** include services. **Manufactured products** have the largest weight in WPI; **food** has the largest weight in CPI. The **GDP deflator** is the widest measure.

### Worked example: calculating CPI and inflation

Suppose a family's basket in the **base year** has three items.

| Item | Quantity | Base-year price (₹) | Cost in base year (₹) | This year's price (₹) | Cost this year (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rice | 10 kg | 40 | 400 | 44 | 440 |
| Cooking oil | 2 litre | 150 | 300 | 165 | 330 |
| Bus fare | 10 trips | 30 | 300 | 30 | 300 |
| **Total** | | | **1,000** | | **1,070** |

**CPI this year = (Cost of basket this year ÷ Cost in base year) × 100 = 1,070 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 107**

If CPI last year was **102**, inflation this year = (107 − 102) ÷ 102 × 100 ≈ **4.9%**.

Notice: rice and oil rose **10%**, bus fare did not rise at all. The **weight** of each item (its share in spending) decides how much it affects the index — this is a **Laspeyres-type** index (fixed base-year quantities).

### Other indices

- **Producer Price Index (PPI)** — planned to replace WPI in time (check the latest).
- **House Price Index** — RBI.
- **Index of Industrial Production (IIP)** — not a price index; measures industrial output (NSO; new base 2022-23 — check the latest).

## Monetary policy: meaning and goals

**Monetary policy** is the RBI's management of **money supply and interest rates** to achieve:

1. **Price stability** (main goal since 2016)
2. Support for **growth**
3. **Financial stability**, adequate credit, stable exchange rate

| Stance | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Expansionary / accommodative / dovish** | Cut rates, add liquidity → cheaper loans → more spending (used in slowdowns, e.g. COVID 2020) |
| **Contractionary / tight / hawkish** | Raise rates, absorb liquidity → costlier loans → lower demand and inflation |
| **Neutral** | Can move either way depending on data |

## Flexible Inflation Targeting and the MPC

- The **RBI Act was amended in 2016** to give a statutory basis to **Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT)**.
- The **Government**, in consultation with the RBI, sets the target **every five years**. The target is **4% CPI (Combined) inflation, with a band of 2% to 6%**. It was first set for **2016–21**, renewed for **2021–26**, and renewed again for **1 April 2026 – 31 March 2031** (notified **March 2026**).
- If inflation stays **above 6% or below 2% for three consecutive quarters**, it is a **failure**; the RBI must send a **report to the Government** explaining reasons and remedies.

### Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| Legal basis | Section **45ZB** of the RBI Act (2016 amendment) |
| Members | **6** — **3 from RBI** (Governor as chairperson, Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, one officer nominated by the RBI Board) + **3 external members** appointed by the Central Government |
| External members' term | **4 years**, not eligible for reappointment |
| Meetings | At least **4 a year** (in practice **6**, every two months) |
| Quorum | 4 members, including Governor (or Deputy Governor in his absence) |
| Voting | Each member one vote; **Governor has a casting vote** in a tie |
| Output | Repo rate decision + stance; minutes published on the **14th day** |

> **Prelims angle:** Before 2016 the RBI Governor alone decided rates. The MPC idea came from the **Urjit Patel Committee (2014)**. The Governor has a **casting vote**, not a veto.

## The tools of monetary policy

### Quantitative (general) tools

| Tool | Meaning | Value (as of Oct 2026 — check the latest) |
|---|---|---|
| **Repo rate** | Rate at which the RBI lends **short-term** money to banks against government securities (repurchase agreement). The **policy rate** | **5.50%** (raised by 25 basis points on **7 October 2026**, after cuts in 2025 had brought it to 5.25%) |
| **Standing Deposit Facility (SDF)** | Rate the RBI pays banks for parking surplus money **without collateral**; the **floor** of the corridor (introduced **April 2022**, replaced fixed reverse repo as the floor) | **5.25%** |
| **Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)** | Emergency overnight borrowing by banks, can dip into SLR securities; the **ceiling** of the corridor (introduced **2011**) | **5.75%** |
| **Bank rate** | Rate for long-term lending to banks without securities; now aligned to MSF; used for penalties | **5.75%** |
| **Reverse repo** | Rate at which RBI borrows from banks against securities; now used only occasionally | Fixed at 3.35% (no longer the floor) |
| **CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio)** | % of banks' **Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL)** kept as **cash with the RBI**; earns **no interest** | **3%** (cut from 4% in stages during 2025) |
| **SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio)** | % of NDTL banks must keep in **liquid assets** — cash, gold, government securities — **with themselves** | **18%** |
| **OMO (Open Market Operations)** | RBI **buys** government securities (injects money) or **sells** them (absorbs money) | — |
| **LAF (Liquidity Adjustment Facility)** | Daily window of repo and reverse repo/SDF operations; includes **VRR/VRRR** (variable rate repo/reverse repo auctions) | — |

Policy **corridor**: SDF (floor) = repo − 0.25; MSF (ceiling) = repo + 0.25.

> **Remember:** Policy rates are the most "check the latest" facts in the whole book. They change at each MPC meeting (six times a year). Note the latest rates in your current-affairs notebook before Prelims.

### Qualitative (selective) tools

- **Margin requirements** — the gap between the value of the security and the loan amount; raised to discourage loans for speculation.
- **Credit rationing** — limits on lending to some sectors.
- **Moral suasion** — persuasion and advice to banks.
- **Direct action** — penalties on banks that do not follow rules.
- **Selective credit control** — e.g. on loans against foodgrain stocks to stop hoarding.

### Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS)

Started **2004** — the RBI issues special government securities to absorb extra liquidity created by large foreign inflows. The money is kept separately and not used by the Government.

### Worked example: repo rate and EMI

A family takes a home loan of **₹10 lakh** linked to the repo rate. The bank's rate = repo + spread of **3%**.

- Repo at **5.25%** → loan rate **8.25%**
- RBI raises repo by **25 basis points** (1 basis point = 0.01%) to **5.50%** → loan rate **8.50%**

Approximate extra yearly interest in the first year = ₹10,00,000 × 0.25% = **₹2,500** a year (about ₹208 a month). The bank either raises the EMI or increases the number of EMIs.

On the other side, an FD of ₹1 lakh may earn a little more interest — savers gain.

### Worked example: CRR and lendable money

A bank has NDTL (deposits) of ₹1,000 crore.

- CRR **3%** → ₹30 crore kept with RBI. SLR **18%** → ₹180 crore in government securities.
- Money left to lend ≈ 1,000 − 30 − 180 = **₹790 crore**.
- If CRR is raised to **4%**, ₹10 crore more gets locked → lendable money falls to **₹780 crore**. Across the whole banking system (NDTL of over ₹200 lakh crore), a 1-point CRR change moves about **₹2 lakh crore** of liquidity.

### Lending rate systems — evolution

| System | From | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **BPLR** (Benchmark Prime Lending Rate) | 2003 | Bank's own rate — opaque |
| **Base Rate** | July 2010 | Minimum rate, cost-based |
| **MCLR** (Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate) | **April 2016** | Bank's marginal cost of funds |
| **EBLR** (External Benchmark Lending Rate) | **1 October 2019** | New floating loans to retail and MSMEs linked to an **external benchmark** such as the **repo rate** — faster transmission |

> **Prelims angle:** "Which lending system made repo changes pass to retail loans quickly?" → **External benchmark (EBLR), October 2019**.

### Why transmission is slow in India

- Banks depend on **fixed deposits** whose rates are fixed for a period.
- **Small savings rates** (post office, PPF) set by Government compete with bank deposits.
- High NPAs earlier made banks reluctant to cut rates.
- Large informal credit market (moneylenders) outside RBI's reach — important in rural Bihar.

## Fiscal measures and supply-side measures against inflation

Monetary policy alone cannot control food inflation caused by a bad monsoon. The Government uses:

- **Buffer stocks** and **Open Market Sale Scheme (OMSS)** of FCI — selling wheat and rice to bring down prices.
- **Price Stabilisation Fund (PSF, 2014–15)** — onion, pulses buffer.
- **Export bans or duties** (e.g. on onion, rice) and **import duty cuts** (edible oil, pulses).
- **Stock limits** on traders to stop hoarding (Essential Commodities Act, 1955).
- Cutting **excise duty** on petrol and diesel.
- Long term: storage, cold chains, e-NAM, better farm productivity.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar's poor households spend a large share on food, so **food inflation** (vegetables, pulses, edible oil) hits them hardest. Bihar is a major producer of **vegetables, maize, makhana and litchi**, but weak cold storage and processing cause **price crashes at harvest and spikes later**. In September 2026 national CPI inflation rose to about **5.5%**, with food inflation near **7.7%**, and rural inflation higher than urban — a typical pattern that hurts rural Bihar (as of 2026 — check the latest). See [Economy of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/economy-of-bihar).
