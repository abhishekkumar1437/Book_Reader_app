Public finance is the study of how the government raises money (taxes, borrowing) and how it spends it. BPSC Prelims asks about budget articles (112, 266, 267, 280), types of deficits, which taxes are direct, and GST basics. Mains GS II asks about fiscal federalism — how much Bihar gets from the Centre and why — and about GST's impact on a consuming state like Bihar.

> **In simple words:** The budget is the government's yearly statement of income and spending. When it spends more than it earns (excluding new loans), the gap is the fiscal deficit, which it fills by borrowing. Taxes are either direct (paid by the person on whom they fall, like income tax) or indirect (passed on to the buyer, like GST). The Finance Commission decides how the Centre's tax money is shared with states like Bihar.

## The budget and the Constitution

The word "budget" does **not** appear in the Constitution. It is called the **Annual Financial Statement**.

| Article | What it says |
|---|---|
| **112** | **Annual Financial Statement** of the Union, laid before Parliament by the President (through the Finance Minister) |
| **202** | Annual Financial Statement of a **state** |
| **113** | Demands for grants voted by the Lok Sabha |
| **114** | **Appropriation Bill** — permission to withdraw money from the Consolidated Fund |
| **110** | Definition of **Money Bill** (Lok Sabha only; Rajya Sabha can delay 14 days) |
| **116** | **Vote on account**, votes of credit, exceptional grants |
| **265** | No tax can be levied or collected **except by authority of law** |
| **266(1)** | **Consolidated Fund of India** — all revenues, loans raised, loan repayments received |
| **266(2)** | **Public Account of India** — money held by government as a trustee (PF, small savings, deposits) |
| **267** | **Contingency Fund of India** — for unforeseen spending; at the disposal of the President; corpus **₹30,000 crore** (raised from ₹500 crore in 2021) |
| **270** | Sharing of central taxes with states (divisible pool) |
| **275** | Grants-in-aid to states |
| **279A** | **GST Council** |
| **280** | **Finance Commission** |
| **292 / 293** | Borrowing by the Union / by states |

Key budget facts:

- The Union Budget is presented on **1 February** (since **2017**; earlier on the last working day of February).
- The **Railway Budget was merged** with the General Budget in **2017** (on the **Bibek Debroy Committee** recommendation; separate railway budget had existed since **1924** on the **Acworth Committee** advice).
- The **Economic Survey** is presented a day before the budget, prepared by the **Chief Economic Adviser** (Ministry of Finance).
- India's **first budget** after independence: **26 November 1947**, by **R.K. Shanmukham Chetty**.
- The first budget of British India: **1860**, by **James Wilson**.
- **Outcome Budget** (2005) — links spending to results. **Gender Budget** statement (2005-06). **Zero-based budgeting** — every expense justified afresh.
- **Union Budget 2026-27** was presented on **1 February 2026** (a Sunday) (as of 2026).

> **Prelims angle:** The **Contingency Fund** is at the disposal of the **President**; the **Consolidated Fund** needs **Parliament's** approval for withdrawal. Expenditure "**charged**" on the Consolidated Fund (salary of President, judges of the Supreme Court, CAG, interest on debt) is **not voted** in Parliament, only discussed.

### Receipts

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue receipts** | Do **not** create a liability or reduce an asset | **Tax revenue** (income tax, corporation tax, GST, customs, excise); **non-tax revenue** (interest received, dividends from PSUs and RBI, fees, fines, spectrum charges) |
| **Capital receipts** | **Create a liability** or **reduce an asset** | **Borrowings** (market loans, small savings), **recovery of loans**, **disinvestment** (sale of shares in PSUs) |

### Expenditure

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue expenditure** | Does **not** create assets | Salaries, pensions, **interest payments** (largest single item of Union revenue spending), subsidies, grants to states (even if states use them for assets) |
| **Capital expenditure (capex)** | **Creates assets** or reduces liabilities | Roads, railways, defence equipment, loans to states and PSUs, repayment of loans |

> **Common mistake:** **Interest payment** is **revenue** expenditure; **repayment of the loan principal** is **capital** expenditure. **Disinvestment** is a **capital** receipt; **dividends** from PSUs are **revenue** (non-tax) receipts.

## The four deficits

<div class="formula">

| Deficit | Formula | What it tells |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue deficit (RD)** | Revenue expenditure − Revenue receipts | Govt borrowing to pay for day-to-day spending — the worst kind |
| **Effective revenue deficit (ERD)** | RD − Grants for creation of capital assets | Part of RD that really goes to consumption (introduced 2011-12) |
| **Fiscal deficit (FD)** | Total expenditure − (Revenue receipts + Non-debt capital receipts) = **Total borrowing needed** | Main measure of the government's borrowing |
| **Primary deficit (PD)** | Fiscal deficit − **Interest payments** | Borrowing for current needs, excluding past debt |

</div>

**Non-debt capital receipts** = recovery of loans + disinvestment + other receipts that are not borrowing.

### Worked example: calculating the deficits

A government's budget (₹ lakh crore):

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Tax revenue (net to the Centre) | 28 |
| Non-tax revenue | 6 |
| **Revenue receipts** | **34** |
| Recovery of loans | 0.3 |
| Disinvestment | 0.7 |
| **Non-debt capital receipts** | **1.0** |
| Revenue expenditure | 40 (includes interest payments of 13, and grants for capital assets of 4) |
| Capital expenditure | 11 |
| **Total expenditure** | **51** |

- **Revenue deficit** = 40 − 34 = **6**
- **Effective revenue deficit** = 6 − 4 = **2**
- **Fiscal deficit** = 51 − (34 + 1) = **16**
- **Primary deficit** = 16 − 13 = **3**

If GDP = ₹350 lakh crore, **fiscal deficit = 16 ÷ 350 × 100 ≈ 4.6% of GDP**.

Interpretation: of the ₹16 lakh crore borrowed, ₹13 lakh crore goes just to pay interest on old debt. This is why high debt is a trap — interest eats the budget.

> **Memory trick:** "**F**iscal = **F**ull borrowing; **P**rimary = **P**resent borrowing (without interest)." If primary deficit is zero, the government is borrowing only to pay interest.

### How the fiscal deficit is financed

- **Market borrowings** — dated government securities (G-secs) and treasury bills.
- **Small savings** (NSSF) — post office savings, PPF.
- **External borrowing** — from World Bank, ADB, etc.
- **Monetisation** (printing money by RBI buying government bonds directly) — **stopped by agreement in 1997** (ad-hoc treasury bills abolished, replaced by **Ways and Means Advances**). Monetisation causes inflation.

### Effects of a high fiscal deficit

- **Crowding out** — Government borrowing raises interest rates, leaving less money for private investment.
- **Inflation** if financed by money creation.
- **Debt trap** — rising interest burden.
- **Lower credit rating**, higher cost of foreign borrowing.
- But a deficit is **useful** in a slowdown if it funds **capex** (roads, power), which raises future growth (**Keynesian** view).

## FRBM: the fiscal rule

**Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003** (in force from **5 July 2004**). It was based on the **E.A.S. Sarma Committee** (2000) recommendations.

Original targets:

- Fiscal deficit to **3% of GDP**.
- **Revenue deficit to zero.**
- Government must place a **Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement**, **Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement**, and **Macroeconomic Framework Statement** with the budget.

Targets were suspended in the **2008 global crisis** and in **2020 (COVID)**.

**N.K. Singh Committee (FRBM Review, 2017)** recommended:

- **Debt as the main anchor**: general government debt **60% of GDP** by 2023 (Centre **40%**, states **20%**).
- Fiscal deficit 3% of GDP; revenue deficit to fall.
- An independent **Fiscal Council**.
- **Escape clause** (deviation up to 0.5% of GDP in war, calamity, collapse of agriculture, etc.).

Current path (as of 2026 — check the latest):

- Union fiscal deficit: **9.2%** of GDP in **2020-21** (COVID peak) → **4.4%** (revised, **2025-26**) → **4.3%** budgeted for **2026-27**.
- From 2026-27 the Centre shifted to a **debt-to-GDP anchor**: Union debt about **55–56%** of GDP in 2026-27, target **50% (±1)** by **2030-31**.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar passed its own **Bihar Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2006**. States can generally borrow up to about **3% of GSDP** (with extra room in some years for power-sector reforms or capex). The **16th Finance Commission** recommends a **3% of GSDP** limit for states and wants off-budget borrowings brought into the budget. Bihar depends on central transfers (tax share + grants) for **around 70% or more** of its revenue receipts — one of the highest dependencies among states (check the latest Bihar budget).

## Taxes: direct and indirect

A **tax** is a compulsory payment to the government without any direct return.

| Basis | Direct tax | Indirect tax |
|---|---|---|
| **Burden (incidence)** | On the person who pays it; **cannot be shifted** | **Shifted** to the buyer |
| Examples | **Income tax, corporation tax**, securities transaction tax, wealth tax (abolished 2015), gift tax | **GST**, customs duty, excise (on petrol, alcohol), stamp duty |
| Nature | Usually **progressive** — higher rate for higher income | Usually **regressive** — same rate for rich and poor |
| Collection | Annual, through returns and TDS | At each transaction |
| Effect on inflation | Reduces demand, anti-inflationary | Raises prices |

### Progressive, proportional and regressive

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Progressive** | Tax rate **rises** with income | Income tax slabs |
| **Proportional** | Same rate for all incomes | A flat 10% tax |
| **Regressive** | Takes a **larger share** of a poor person's income | Same GST on soap for rich and poor |

#### Worked example: why GST on essentials is regressive

GST of 5% on a ₹100 item = ₹5.

- A poor worker earning ₹10,000 a month who buys 20 such items pays ₹100 in tax = **1.0%** of income.
- A rich person earning ₹2,00,000 who buys 40 such items pays ₹200 = **0.1%** of income.

The poor pay a bigger share of their income. That is why basic food items are kept at **0%** (nil) or **5%** GST.

### Major taxes and who levies them

| Tax | Levied by | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Personal income tax | Centre | Shared with states via divisible pool; new tax regime is default since 2023-24 |
| Corporation tax | Centre | Cut to **22%** (and 15% for new manufacturing firms) in **September 2019** |
| Customs duty | Centre | On imports/exports |
| Central excise | Centre | Now only on petroleum products, tobacco (and some others outside GST) |
| CGST, IGST | Centre | Under GST |
| SGST | State | Under GST |
| Stamp duty, registration | State | On property transfer |
| Land revenue | State | — |
| State excise on alcohol | State | Bihar has **prohibition since April 2016**, so it lost a major source of tax |
| Professional tax | State | Max **₹2,500** a year (Article 276) |
| Property tax | Local bodies | — |

> **Prelims angle:** **Corporation tax** has generally been the largest or second-largest source of the Centre's gross tax revenue, close to personal income tax; **GST** is the largest single indirect tax. In recent years **personal income tax** has overtaken corporation tax (check the latest budget). Taxes on **agricultural income** can be levied only by **states**.

### Important tax terms

- **Tax buoyancy** = % change in tax revenue ÷ % change in GDP. If GDP grows 10% and tax revenue 12%, buoyancy = **1.2** (good).
- **Tax–GDP ratio** — India's combined (Centre + states) tax–GDP ratio is about **17–18%**; the Centre's gross tax is about **11–12%** of GDP.
- **Cess and surcharge** — extra levies on top of tax; **not shared with states** (they are outside the divisible pool). States complain that the Centre relies more on cesses.
- **Laffer curve** — beyond a point, higher tax rates reduce total tax collection.
- **Tax expenditure** — revenue lost due to exemptions.
- **Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT)**, **Securities Transaction Tax (STT)**, **Equalisation Levy** (on digital ads; 6% levy abolished from April 2025) — check the latest.
- **Tax Terrorism**, **faceless assessment (2020)**, **Vivad se Vishwas** — tax administration reforms.
- **New Income-tax Act, 2025** replaces the Income-tax Act, 1961 from **1 April 2026** (as of 2026 — check the latest).

## Goods and Services Tax (GST)

### Background

- Idea of a national GST: **Kelkar Task Force (2004)**; Empowered Committee of State Finance Ministers; **Asim Dasgupta** chaired it.
- **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** (Bill was the 122nd).
- GST launched on **1 July 2017** ("**One Nation, One Tax**").
- First state to ratify the amendment: **Assam**. **Bihar** was the **second** state to ratify (August 2016).
- New articles: **246A** (concurrent power of Centre and states to make GST laws), **269A** (IGST on inter-state supply), **279A** (GST Council).

### Structure

| Component | Who collects | When |
|---|---|---|
| **CGST** | Centre | Intra-state supply |
| **SGST / UTGST** | State / UT | Intra-state supply (same rate as CGST) |
| **IGST** | Centre, then shared with destination state | **Inter-state** supply and imports |

GST is a **destination-based** consumption tax: tax goes to the state where goods are **consumed**, not where they are produced. This helps **consuming states** like **Bihar** (large population, few factories).

### Input Tax Credit: worked example

| Stage | Sale price (₹) | GST @18% (₹) | Tax paid on input (₹) | Net GST paid to govt (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raw material seller | 100 | 18 | 0 | 18 |
| Manufacturer | 150 | 27 | 18 | 9 |
| Retailer | 200 | 36 | 27 | 9 |
| **Total** | | | | **36** |

Total GST = **₹36 = 18% of the final price ₹200**. Each seller pays tax only on the **value added**. Before GST, taxes were charged on taxes (**cascading effect**). GST removed this.

### Taxes subsumed and kept out

- **Subsumed:** central excise (mostly), service tax, additional customs duty (CVD), VAT, entry tax, octroi, luxury tax, entertainment tax (by states), purchase tax.
- **Outside GST:** **alcohol for human consumption** (permanently), and **five petroleum products** — crude oil, petrol, diesel, ATF, natural gas (can be brought in only when the GST Council decides), electricity, stamp duty.

### GST Council (Article 279A)

- **Chairperson:** **Union Finance Minister**. Members: Union MoS (Finance) and **Finance Ministers of all states** (and UTs with legislatures).
- Decisions by **three-fourths majority** of weighted votes. **Centre's vote = one-third**; **all states together = two-thirds**.
- Quorum: half of members.
- Supreme Court (**Mohit Minerals case, 2022**): Council's recommendations are **persuasive, not binding** on Parliament and state legislatures.
- **GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT)** set up (check the latest status).

### Rates: GST 2.0 (from 22 September 2025)

The GST Council's 56th meeting (September 2025) simplified the structure:

| Old slabs | New structure (GST 2.0) |
|---|---|
| 0, 5, 12, 18, 28% (+ compensation cess) | **0 (nil), 5% (merit/essential), 18% (standard)** and a special **40%** rate for luxury and sin goods |
| 12% slab | Most items moved to **5%** |
| 28% slab | Most items moved to **18%**; a few luxury/sin goods to 40% |

Special low rates remain for gold (3%) and rough diamonds. Tobacco products moved to the new structure later, after compensation-cess loans are repaid (as of 2026 — check the latest notifications).

### GST compensation

- States were promised **compensation for 5 years** (July 2017–June 2022) for revenue loss, assuming **14% annual growth** over the 2015-16 base, funded by a **compensation cess** on luxury and sin goods.
- The cess was extended to **March 2026** to repay loans taken during COVID; it is being phased out under GST 2.0.

> **Mains angle:** GST and Bihar — **gains**: destination principle favours consuming states; wider tax base; e-way bills and less corruption at check-posts. **Concerns**: loss of fiscal autonomy (states cannot set their own rates), dependence on compensation, slow refunds for small traders, compliance burden. Bihar's GST collections have grown, but its per-capita GST is low because incomes are low.

## The Finance Commission and fiscal federalism

### Vertical and horizontal imbalance

- **Vertical fiscal imbalance**: the Centre collects most taxes (income tax, corporation tax, customs), but states do most of the spending (police, health, education, agriculture). States collect about **35–40%** of revenue but spend about **60%** of total government expenditure.
- **Horizontal imbalance**: rich states (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka) collect more; poor states (Bihar, UP) need more.

### The Finance Commission

- **Article 280** — constituted by the **President every five years** (or earlier).
- **Chairman + 4 members.** Qualifications under the **Finance Commission (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1951**.
- It recommends:
  1. **Vertical devolution** — share of states in the **divisible pool** of central taxes.
  2. **Horizontal devolution** — how that share is divided among states.
  3. **Grants-in-aid** (Article 275) — for revenue deficit, local bodies, disaster management.
  4. Measures to augment state Consolidated Funds to help **panchayats and municipalities** (added by **73rd/74th amendments**).
- Recommendations are **advisory**, but are almost always accepted.
- **First Finance Commission (1951)**: chairman **K.C. Neogy**.

| Finance Commission | Chairman | Period | States' share in divisible pool |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13th | Vijay Kelkar | 2010–15 | 32% |
| **14th** | **Y.V. Reddy** | 2015–20 | **42%** (big jump) |
| **15th** | **N.K. Singh** | 2020–26 | **41%** (1% less because J&K became a UT) |
| **16th** | **Arvind Panagariya** | **2026–31** | **41%** (retained) |

### Horizontal criteria: 15th vs 16th FC

| Criterion | 15th FC weight | 16th FC weight | What it rewards |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Income distance** (distance from highest per-capita income) | 45% | **42.5%** | Poorer states get more — helps **Bihar** the most |
| **Population (2011)** | 15% | **17.5%** | Large states |
| **Demographic performance** | 12.5% | **10%** (now based on population growth 1971–2011) | States that controlled population |
| **Area** | 15% | **10%** | Large states |
| **Forest and ecology** | 10% | **10%** | Forest cover |
| **Tax and fiscal effort** | 2.5% | — (dropped) | — |
| **Contribution to GDP** | — | **10%** (new) | Richer, high-output states |

Source: 16th Finance Commission report (tabled February 2026), summarised by PRS (as of 2026 — check the latest).

Other 16th FC recommendations: **revenue deficit grants and state-specific grants discontinued**; grants to local bodies about **₹7.9 lakh crore** (80% basic, 20% performance); disaster management corpus; Centre's fiscal deficit to **3.5% of GDP by 2030-31**; states' deficit limit **3% of GSDP**.

### Worked example: how devolution works

Suppose the divisible pool (central taxes minus cess, surcharge and collection cost) is **₹30 lakh crore**.

- States' share (41%) = 30 × 0.41 = **₹12.3 lakh crore**
- Bihar's share of this (16th FC: **9.95%**) = 12.3 × 0.0995 ≈ **₹1.22 lakh crore** for that year.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar is the **second-largest recipient** of central tax devolution after **Uttar Pradesh**. Its share was **9.67%** (14th FC), **10.06%** (15th FC) and is **9.95%** under the **16th FC** (2026–31). The slight fall is due to the new "contribution to GDP" criterion and lower area weight, which favour richer states. Because Bihar's own revenue is small (its own tax revenue is only around **5–6% of GSDP** and prohibition removed liquor excise), central transfers are its lifeline. Bihar has long demanded **Special Category Status** or a special package to compensate for the 2000 bifurcation. Link with [Economy of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/economy-of-bihar).

### Other channels of transfer

- **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)** — Centre and states share costs (usually **60:40**, **90:10** for north-eastern and Himalayan states). Bihar must find its 40% share, which strains its budget.
- **Central Sector Schemes** — 100% centrally funded (e.g. PM-KISAN).
- **Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment (SASCI)** — 50-year interest-free loans for capex (since 2020-21).

> **Mains angle:** Fiscal federalism debates for GS II — shrinking divisible pool due to cesses and surcharges; GST reduced states' tax autonomy; conditional transfers through CSS; the new GDP-contribution criterion vs equity for poor states; need for a stronger State Finance Commission for panchayats.

## Public debt

- **Internal debt** (dated securities, T-bills) and **external debt** (from other countries and bodies).
- **Treasury bills** — short-term (91, 182, 364 days); **dated securities** — long-term.
- India's general government (Centre + states) debt is about **80%+ of GDP** (16th FC projects it to fall from about 77% in 2026-27 to 73% in 2030-31 — check the latest).
- Most of India's debt is **domestic** and in **rupees**, which lowers external risk.

## Key terms

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | Government's use of taxes, spending and borrowing to guide the economy |
