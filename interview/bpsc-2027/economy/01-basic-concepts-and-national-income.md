This chapter builds the vocabulary you will use in every other economy chapter. BPSC Prelims asks direct questions on definitions (GDP vs GNP, factor cost vs market price, base year, who publishes national income data). Mains GS II asks you to use these ideas to explain why Bihar's growth is fast but its per-capita income is still low.

> **In simple words:** An economy is the system by which people produce, sell and buy goods and services. National income is the money value of everything a country produces in one year. GDP counts what is produced *inside* the country. GNP counts what is produced by the country's *own people*, wherever they are. Real figures remove the effect of price rise; nominal figures do not.

## What is an economy?

An **economy** is the whole set of activities by which a society uses its scarce resources (land, labour, capital, enterprise) to produce goods and services and distribute them. Every economy must answer three basic questions:

1. **What** to produce (rice or cars? schools or roads?)
2. **How** to produce (by hand labour or machines?)
3. **For whom** to produce (who gets the output?)

### Types of economies

| Type | Who decides | Example | Key feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Capitalist / market economy** | Private owners and the market (prices) | USA | Profit motive, private property, little state role |
| **Socialist / command economy** | The state (central planning) | Former USSR | State owns means of production |
| **Mixed economy** | Both state and market | **India** | Public and private sectors exist together |

India chose a **mixed economy** after independence. The public sector controlled "commanding heights" (steel, heavy industry, railways, banking after 1969), while agriculture and small business stayed private. After **1991** India moved towards a more market-led mixed economy (see [Economic Reforms and Current Issues](/interview/bpsc-2027/economy/economic-reforms-and-current-issues)).

> **Prelims angle:** "India is a mixed economy" — the idea is linked to the Industrial Policy Resolution of **1948** and especially **1956**. The word "socialist" was added to the Preamble by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**.

### Microeconomics and macroeconomics

- **Microeconomics** studies one unit: a household, a firm, the price of one good (demand and supply of onions).
- **Macroeconomics** studies the whole economy: national income, inflation, unemployment, money supply, government budget, balance of payments.

BPSC questions are almost all **macroeconomics**.

## Sectors of the economy

Economic activities are grouped into sectors.

| Sector | What it does | Examples | Also called |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Uses natural resources directly | Agriculture, fishing, forestry, mining | Agriculture & allied |
| **Secondary** | Turns raw material into goods | Manufacturing, construction, electricity, gas, water supply | Industry |
| **Tertiary** | Provides services | Trade, transport, banking, IT, education, health, public administration | Services |

Some books add a **quaternary** sector (knowledge work — research, IT) and **quinary** (top decision makers), but BPSC mostly uses the three-sector model.

> **Common mistake:** In the textbook three-sector model, mining is **primary**. (In some official Indian tables, "mining and quarrying" is grouped under "industry" — read the question carefully.) Construction and electricity are **secondary**, not tertiary.

### Shares of the sectors in India (approximate, stable trend)

- Services: roughly **55%** of Gross Value Added (GVA) — the largest share.
- Industry: roughly **25–28%**.
- Agriculture and allied: roughly **16–18%** of GVA, but it employs about **45%** of workers (PLFS).

This mismatch — agriculture gives a small share of output but a large share of jobs — is the main reason for low rural incomes and **disguised unemployment** (more people working on a farm than needed).

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar's economy is even more services-led in output (services are well over half of Bihar's GSVA) while agriculture still employs about half of the workers, and the manufacturing share is very small. This is called a "jump from agriculture to services without industrialisation". For the latest sector shares see [Economy of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/economy-of-bihar).

### Organised and unorganised; public and private

- **Organised (formal) sector:** registered firms, fixed hours, social security (PF, ESI). Example: a bank, a car factory.
- **Unorganised (informal) sector:** small units, no written contracts, no social security. Example: a roadside dhaba, a brick kiln worker. More than **80–90%** of Indian workers are informal.
- **Public sector:** owned by government (SAIL, Indian Railways). **Private sector:** owned by individuals or companies.

What the diagram teaches:

- Households give **factor services** to firms and receive **factor incomes**: **rent** (for land), **wages** (for labour), **interest** (for capital), **profit** (for enterprise).
- Households spend that income on goods and services made by firms.
- So, in a simple economy, **total output = total income = total expenditure**. This is why national income can be measured in three ways.

In a fuller model we add:

- **Leakages** (money leaving the circle): **savings, taxes, imports**.
- **Injections** (money entering): **investment, government spending, exports**.

When injections are bigger than leakages, the economy grows; when leakages are bigger, it slows down.

> **Prelims angle:** The three methods of measuring national income (production, income, expenditure) all come from this circular flow idea.

## National income: the main aggregates

### GDP — Gross Domestic Product

**GDP** is the market value of all **final** goods and services produced **within the domestic territory** of a country in **one year** (financial year April–March in India).

Four key words:

- **Market value** — measured in money (₹).
- **Final** — goods bought by the final user. Intermediate goods (wheat bought by a bakery to make bread) are not counted separately, to avoid **double counting**.
- **Within the domestic territory** — includes production by foreigners working inside India; excludes Indians working abroad. Domestic territory also includes Indian embassies abroad, Indian ships and aircraft operating between countries.
- **One year** — a flow, not a stock.

#### Avoiding double counting: the value-added method

| Stage | Sale value (₹) | Bought input (₹) | Value added (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Farmer grows wheat | 100 | 0 | 100 |
| Miller makes flour | 150 | 100 | 50 |
| Baker makes bread | 220 | 150 | 70 |
| **Total** | 470 | | **220** |

If you add all sale values you get ₹470 — wrong, because wheat is counted three times. The correct contribution to GDP is the **value of the final good (₹220)** = **sum of value added (100 + 50 + 70 = ₹220)**.

### GNP — Gross National Product

**GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)**

**NFIA** = factor income earned by Indian residents abroad − factor income earned by foreigners in India.

GDP is about **where** production happens (geography). GNP is about **who** produces (nationals/residents). In India's national accounts the term used is **GNI (Gross National Income)**, which is the same idea.

#### Worked example: GDP vs GNP

Suppose in a year:

- Value of final output inside India = ₹300 lakh crore → **GDP = ₹300 lakh crore**
- Indians working in the Gulf send home wage and profit income = ₹6 lakh crore
- Foreign companies in India send home profits and interest = ₹9 lakh crore

NFIA = 6 − 9 = **−₹3 lakh crore**

GNP = 300 + (−3) = **₹297 lakh crore**

So for India, **GNP is usually slightly less than GDP**, because foreign companies and lenders earn more from India than Indians earn as factor income abroad.

> **Common mistake:** Remittances sent home by migrant workers are partly *transfers*, not all factor income, in the balance-of-payments accounts. For the exam, remember the simple rule: GNP = GDP + NFIA, and for India NFIA is **negative**, so GNP < GDP.

### Net values: depreciation

Machines and buildings wear out during production. This loss in value is **depreciation** (also called **Consumption of Fixed Capital**).

- **NDP (Net Domestic Product) = GDP − Depreciation**
- **NNP (Net National Product) = GNP − Depreciation**

**NNP at factor cost** is called **National Income** in the strict textbook sense.

### Market price vs factor cost (and basic price)

- **Market price** = the price buyers pay, which includes indirect taxes and is reduced by subsidies.
- **Factor cost** = what the factors of production actually receive.

**Factor cost = Market price − Net indirect taxes**, where **Net indirect taxes = Indirect taxes − Subsidies**.

Since **2015** (new series with base 2011-12), India measures sector-wise output as **GVA at basic prices**, and headline growth as **GDP at market prices** (constant prices).

- **GVA at basic prices** = GVA at factor cost + production taxes − production subsidies.
- **GDP (market prices) = GVA at basic prices + product taxes − product subsidies.**

#### Worked example: the full chain

| Item | ₹ lakh crore |
|---|---|
| GDP at market prices | 300 |
| NFIA | −3 |
| Depreciation | 30 |
| Indirect taxes | 35 |
| Subsidies | 5 |

- GNP at market prices = 300 − 3 = **297**
- NNP at market prices = 297 − 30 = **267**
- Net indirect taxes = 35 − 5 = **30**
- NNP at factor cost (National Income) = 267 − 30 = **237**

<div class="formula">

| Formula | Meaning |
|---|---|
| GNP = GDP + NFIA | Add income earned abroad by residents, minus income of foreigners here |
| NDP = GDP − Depreciation | Net of wear and tear |
| NNP = GNP − Depreciation | Net national product |
| Factor cost = Market price − (Indirect taxes − Subsidies) | Remove tax effect |
| National Income = NNP at factor cost | Textbook definition |
| Per-capita income = National income ÷ Population | Average income per person |
| GDP (expenditure) = C + I + G + (X − M) | Consumption + Investment + Government + Net exports |

</div>

> **Memory trick:** "**G**ross minus **D**epreciation gives **N**et; **D**omestic plus **N**FIA gives **N**ational." Go from left to right: GDP → (+NFIA) → GNP → (−Dep) → NNP → (−NIT) → NNP fc.

## Three methods of measuring national income

| Method | What is added | Used in India for |
|---|---|---|
| **Production / value-added method** | Value added by each sector (output − intermediate consumption) | Agriculture, industry (most sectors) |
| **Income method** | Rent + wages + interest + profit + mixed income of self-employed | Services like public administration, banking |
| **Expenditure method** | C + I + G + (X − M) | Used as a cross-check, demand side |

India uses a **combination** of production and income methods; the expenditure side is estimated separately. The difference between the production-side and expenditure-side estimates is shown as **"discrepancies"**.

### Worked example: expenditure method

| Component | ₹ lakh crore |
|---|---|
| Private final consumption expenditure (C) | 180 |
| Gross fixed capital formation + change in stocks (I) | 95 |
| Government final consumption expenditure (G) | 30 |
| Exports (X) | 65 |
| Imports (M) | 70 |

GDP = 180 + 95 + 30 + (65 − 70) = **₹300 lakh crore**

Notice that private consumption is the largest part (about **60%** of India's GDP is private consumption). Net exports are usually **negative** for India because imports are more than exports.

### What is NOT counted in GDP

- **Transfer payments** — old-age pension, scholarships, unemployment allowance (no production in return).
- **Sale of second-hand goods** — counted when first produced (but the broker's commission is counted).
- **Purely financial transactions** — buying shares or bonds (only the brokerage service is counted).
- **Household work done by family members for themselves** — a big limitation; unpaid work, mostly by women, is ignored.
- **Illegal activity** and most of the black economy.
- **Intermediate goods** separately.

> **Mains angle:** Limitations of GDP as a welfare measure — ignores income distribution, environment damage, unpaid care work, leisure, quality of life. Alternatives: **Human Development Index (UNDP)**, **Green GDP**, **Gross National Happiness (Bhutan)**, **Multidimensional Poverty Index**. Use these points in any "Is GDP growth enough?" question.

## Nominal vs real GDP

- **Nominal GDP** (GDP at current prices) = output valued at **this year's prices**.
- **Real GDP** (GDP at constant prices) = output valued at **base-year prices**. It removes the effect of inflation, so it shows the true change in the **quantity** of goods and services.

### Worked example: nominal vs real

An economy produces only rice. Base year = Year 1.

| Year | Rice produced (tonnes) | Price per tonne (₹) | Nominal GDP | Real GDP (at Year-1 price ₹100) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 1,000 | 100 | 1,00,000 | 1,00,000 |
| Year 2 | 1,050 | 110 | 1,15,500 | 1,05,000 |

- Nominal growth = (1,15,500 − 1,00,000) ÷ 1,00,000 × 100 = **15.5%**
- Real growth = (1,05,000 − 1,00,000) ÷ 1,00,000 × 100 = **5%**

The extra 10.5 points of nominal growth are only due to price rise.

### The GDP deflator

**GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100**

In Year 2: (1,15,500 ÷ 1,05,000) × 100 = **110** → prices rose **10%** since the base year.

A quick rule: **Nominal growth ≈ Real growth + Inflation (deflator)**. Here 5% + 10% ≈ 15.5% (the extra 0.5 comes from multiplying: 1.05 × 1.10 = 1.155).

The GDP deflator covers **all** goods and services produced in the country, including investment goods, but **not imports**. The CPI covers only a consumer basket but includes imported consumer goods. More in [Inflation and Monetary Policy](/interview/bpsc-2027/economy/inflation-and-monetary-policy).

> **Prelims angle:** "Which index is the widest measure of inflation?" → **GDP deflator** (covers the whole domestic output). "Which is used by the RBI as its target?" → **CPI (combined)**.

## Per-capita income and growth rates

**Per-capita income = National income ÷ Population.** It is an **average**; it does not show inequality.

### Worked example

A state has NSDP = ₹6,00,000 crore and population = 13 crore.

Per-capita income = 6,00,000 crore ÷ 13 crore = **≈ ₹46,150**

If next year NSDP grows by 10% and population by 2%, per-capita income grows by about **10 − 2 = 8%** (exactly: 1.10 ÷ 1.02 − 1 ≈ 7.8%).

This is why a state with **high population growth** (like Bihar, with the highest total fertility rate among large states) needs very fast GDP growth to raise its per-capita income.

### Growth rate calculation

**Growth rate (%) = (This year's value − Last year's value) ÷ Last year's value × 100**

For averages over many years, economists use **CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)**. For percentage calculations practice, see [Percentages](/interview/quant-aptitude/percentages).

## Measuring national income in India: institutions and history

| Year / body | Event |
|---|---|
| **1867–68** | **Dadabhai Naoroji** gave the first estimate of India's per-capita income (₹20 per year) in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* |
| 1931–32 | **V.K.R.V. Rao** made the first scientific estimate (combining income and production methods) |
| **1949** | **National Income Committee** set up under **P.C. Mahalanobis** (members D.R. Gadgil, V.K.R.V. Rao) |
| 1950 | **National Sample Survey (NSS)** started |
| 1951 | **Central Statistical Organisation (CSO)** set up |
| **2019** | CSO and NSSO merged into the **National Statistical Office (NSO)** under **MoSPI** (Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation) |
| 2015 | New series with **base year 2011-12**; GVA at basic prices introduced, international SNA-2008 standards |
| **2026** | New GDP series with **base year 2022-23** released (February 2026); new CPI with **base year 2024** (as of 2026 — check the latest) |

> **Prelims angle:** National income in India is estimated by the **NSO (MoSPI)**, not the RBI or NITI Aayog. **P.C. Mahalanobis** is called the father of Indian statistics; **29 June** (his birthday) is **National Statistics Day**.

### What is a base year and why is it changed?

The **base year** is the reference year whose prices are used to calculate real values. It is changed every few years because:

- The structure of the economy changes (new products like smartphones, UPI services; old ones fade).
- Better and newer data sources become available (GST data, company filings in MCA-21, e-Vahan vehicle data).
- To match international standards.

The base year should be a **"normal" year** — without drought, war or pandemic.

### Release calendar of GDP estimates (stable pattern)

- **First Advance Estimates** — early January (for budget-making).
- **Second Advance Estimates** — end of February.
- **Provisional Estimates** — end of May / early June.
- **Quarterly estimates** — about two months after each quarter.
- First, second and third **revised estimates** in later years.

## India's GDP: where we stand (as of 2026 — check the latest)

- Real GDP growth for **2025-26**: about **7.7%** (provisional estimates, June 2026, new base 2022-23).
- Nominal GDP 2025-26: about **₹346 lakh crore**.
- India is among the **fastest-growing large economies** and is the world's **4th or 5th largest** economy in nominal US dollar terms (rankings depend on the IMF's latest World Economic Outlook — check before the exam). In **PPP** terms India is the **3rd largest** after China and the USA.

> **Common mistake:** Do not memorise growth numbers as permanent facts. Every Budget and Economic Survey updates them. Write the latest figure in your current-affairs notebook.

## Related concepts you must know

### Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

PPP compares countries by what money can actually **buy**. ₹100 buys more in India than its dollar exchange-rate value buys in the USA. So India's GDP in **PPP terms is much larger** than at market exchange rates. The World Bank and IMF use PPP for poverty lines and comparisons.

### Capital formation, savings and investment

- **Gross Capital Formation (GCF)** = investment in machines, buildings, roads + change in stocks. It is the main driver of future growth.
- India's investment rate (GCF/GDP) is roughly **30–33%**; savings rate is roughly **30%**.
- **ICOR (Incremental Capital–Output Ratio)** = extra capital needed to produce one extra unit of output. If ICOR = 4 and investment rate = 32%, growth ≈ 32 ÷ 4 = **8%** (Harrod–Domar idea, used in early five-year plans).

### Economic growth vs economic development

| Growth | Development |
|---|---|
| Increase in real GDP / output | Growth **plus** improvement in quality of life |
| Quantitative | Quantitative + qualitative |
| Measured by GDP growth rate | Measured by HDI, MPI, literacy, life expectancy |
| Can happen without development (jobless growth) | Cannot happen without some growth |

> **Mains angle:** "Growth without development" in Bihar's context — Bihar has recorded fast GSDP growth for years, but per-capita income, industrialisation, and jobs lag; large out-migration continues. Add data from the latest Bihar Economic Survey.

### Human Development Index (HDI)

- Published by **UNDP** in the **Human Development Report** since **1990**.
- Idea by **Mahbub ul Haq** (Pakistan) with **Amartya Sen**.
- Three dimensions: **health** (life expectancy at birth), **education** (mean and expected years of schooling), **standard of living** (GNI per capita, PPP $).
- India is in the **"medium human development"** group; its rank is around **130 out of about 193** (check the latest HDR).

## The Bihar picture: high growth, low base

> **Bihar connection:** According to the **Bihar Economic Survey 2025-26** (presented February 2026), Bihar's GSDP in 2024-25 was about **₹9.9 lakh crore** at current prices, growing about **13% (current prices)** and about **8.6% (constant prices)** — faster than the national average. But Bihar's **per-capita income is the lowest among Indian states** (roughly **one-third** of the national average) because it started from a low base and has the **highest population density** (1,106 per km², Census 2011) and high population growth. Use [Economy of Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/economy-of-bihar) for district-level and sector figures (as of 2026 — check the latest survey).

Why Bihar's per-capita income is low — points for Mains:

1. **Low industrial base** — after the **2000 bifurcation**, most minerals and heavy industries went to **Jharkhand**.
2. **Agriculture with small holdings** — average holding is very small (below 0.5 ha); floods in north Bihar, drought in the south.
3. **High population growth** — highest TFR among states (around 3, NFHS-5).
4. **Low urbanisation** — only about **11.3%** urban (Census 2011), lowest among major states.
5. **Historical factors** — Permanent Settlement (1793) and zamindari, freight equalisation policy (1952–1993) that removed Bihar's mineral advantage.
6. **Low credit–deposit ratio** — banks collect deposits in Bihar but lend much less there.

Positive trends: roads and bridges, electricity access, women's self-help groups (**JEEViKA**), girls' school enrolment, and fast services growth.

- Nominal vs real with a one-line numeric example (15.5% nominal vs 5% real).
- Real GDP removes price effect, shows change in quantity; deflator links the two.
- Conclusion: policy decisions (growth, employment) need real figures.

**3. "GDP is a measure of production, not of welfare." Discuss with reference to India. (38 marks)**

Outline:
- **Intro:** GDP definition; its popularity.
- **Body:** Ignores distribution (inequality); ignores unpaid household and care work (women); ignores environmental damage (Green GDP); counts "bads" (spending on pollution clean-up); ignores informal sector partly; no measure of health, education — HDI, MPI as alternatives; India's improvement in MPI shows non-income progress; Bihar example — fast growth but high MPI.
- **Conclusion:** Use GDP with HDI, MPI, SDG Index together.
