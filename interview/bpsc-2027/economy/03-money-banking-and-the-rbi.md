This chapter explains money, banks and the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). BPSC Prelims asks about money measures (M1, M3), the history of RBI and banks (1935, 1949, 1969, 1975, 1982), NABARD, RRBs, and the meaning of NPA, CRR and SLR. Mains asks about financial inclusion and why banks lend so little in Bihar.

> **In simple words:** Money is anything people accept in payment. The RBI prints currency notes and controls how much money and credit flow in the economy. Commercial banks take deposits and give loans; when they lend, they actually create new money. Bihar's people deposit money in banks, but banks lend back only a little more than half of it inside Bihar.

## What is money?

**Money** is anything that is generally accepted as a **medium of exchange** and a **measure of value**.

### Functions of money

| Function | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Medium of exchange** | Removes the problem of barter ("double coincidence of wants") | You sell rice for ₹, buy cloth with ₹ |
| **Measure of value / unit of account** | Prices of all goods are stated in money | A bicycle = ₹6,000 |
| **Store of value** | Can be saved for the future | Keeping savings in a bank |
| **Standard of deferred payment** | Loans repaid in future in money | EMI on a tractor loan |

**Barter system** = direct exchange of goods for goods. Its main problem is the lack of **double coincidence of wants** (the person who has what you want must also want what you have).

### Kinds of money

- **Commodity money** — gold, silver coins (value of the material itself).
- **Fiat money** — paper notes and coins that are money because the **government says so** (by law). The rupee is fiat money.
- **Legal tender** — money that must be accepted for payment of debt. Coins and RBI notes are legal tender. Cheques, UPI and cards are **not** legal tender (a shopkeeper can refuse them).
- **Bank money / credit money** — demand deposits that can be transferred by cheque or UPI.
- **Digital currency** — **e-Rupee (CBDC, Central Bank Digital Currency)**, piloted by the RBI from **December 2022 (retail)** and **November 2022 (wholesale)**. It is legal tender issued by the RBI in digital form.

> **Prelims angle:** **One-rupee notes and all coins are issued by the Government of India (Ministry of Finance)** and carry the **Finance Secretary's** signature on the ₹1 note. All other notes are issued by the **RBI** and carry the **RBI Governor's** signature. The RBI distributes coins.

### Currency issue system

- RBI follows the **Minimum Reserve System** (since **1957**): it must keep at least **₹200 crore** of gold and foreign exchange reserves, of which at least **₹115 crore must be gold**. Beyond that, it can issue any amount of currency.
- Before 1957 it used the **Proportional Reserve System**.
- Currency printing presses: **Nashik, Dewas** (Govt of India's SPMCIL) and **Mysuru, Salboni** (RBI's BRBNMPL). Coins are minted at **Mumbai, Hyderabad, Kolkata, Noida**.

## Measuring money supply

The RBI publishes **money stock measures**. The traditional four (since **1977**) are still the most asked:

| Measure | What it includes | Liquidity |
|---|---|---|
| **M0 (Reserve money / high-powered money)** | Currency in circulation + bankers' deposits with RBI + other deposits with RBI | Base money |
| **M1 (Narrow money)** | Currency with the public + **demand deposits** with banks + other deposits with RBI | Most liquid |
| **M2** | M1 + savings deposits with **post office** savings banks | |
| **M3 (Broad money)** | M1 + **time (fixed) deposits** with banks | Most used broad measure |
| **M4** | M3 + **all deposits with post office** savings organisation (excluding NSC) | Least liquid |

- **Liquidity** = how quickly something can be turned into cash. **M1 is the most liquid**, M4 the least.
- **M3 is called "broad money"** and is the most commonly used measure of money supply.
- **M0 is "high-powered money"** because banks create several times more credit on its base.

> **Memory trick:** "**1 is Narrow, 3 is Broad**." Going up the numbers you add less liquid deposits: demand deposits → post office savings → time deposits → all post office deposits.

> **Common mistake:** "Demand deposits" (current and savings accounts payable on demand) are in **M1**. "Time deposits" (fixed deposits) come in **M3**, not M1.

## How banks create money (credit creation)

Banks keep only a part of each deposit as reserve (cash with RBI, CRR) and lend the rest. The borrower spends it; it comes back as a deposit in some bank; that bank lends again. This chain creates many times the original deposit.

**Money multiplier ≈ 1 ÷ Reserve ratio**

### Worked example

Assume the required reserve ratio is **10%** and someone deposits ₹1,000.

| Round | Deposit (₹) | Reserve kept 10% (₹) | Loan given (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000 | 100 | 900 |
| 2 | 900 | 90 | 810 |
| 3 | 810 | 81 | 729 |
| … | … | … | … |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **1,000** | **9,000** |

Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.10 = **10**, so ₹1,000 can become **₹10,000** of total deposits.

If the RBI raises the reserve ratio to **20%**, multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.20 = **5** → total deposits only ₹5,000. This is why changing **CRR** changes the money supply.

In practice the multiplier is smaller, because people hold some cash and banks keep extra reserves.

## The Reserve Bank of India

### History

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1926** | **Hilton Young Commission** (Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance) recommended a central bank |
| **1934** | **RBI Act, 1934** passed |
| **1 April 1935** | RBI started functioning, headquarters **Calcutta** |
| **1937** | Headquarters moved to **Bombay (Mumbai)** |
| **1 January 1949** | RBI **nationalised** (until then privately owned by shareholders) |
| 2016 | **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** set up by amending the RBI Act |

- **First Governor:** **Sir Osborne Smith**. **First Indian Governor:** **C.D. Deshmukh** (1943–49).
- **Governor (as of 2026 — check the latest):** **Sanjay Malhotra** (26th Governor, since December 2024).
- The idea of the RBI is linked to **B.R. Ambedkar's** book *The Problem of the Rupee* (1923) — a popular Prelims fact.
- The RBI is governed by a **Central Board of Directors** (Governor, up to 4 Deputy Governors, directors nominated by Government). It has **4 local boards** (Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, New Delhi).
- **Financial year of RBI:** changed to **April–March** from 2020-21 (earlier July–June).

### Functions of the RBI

| Function | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Bank of issue** | Sole authority to issue currency notes (except ₹1) |
| **Banker to the government** | Manages accounts, public debt and borrowing of Centre and states (by agreement); gives **Ways and Means Advances (WMA)** — short-term loans to governments |
| **Banker's bank** | Holds banks' cash reserves; **lender of last resort** |
| **Monetary authority** | Controls money supply and interest rates to keep inflation at target (see next chapter) |
| **Regulator and supervisor** | Licenses and supervises banks, NBFCs (under the **Banking Regulation Act, 1949**) |
| **Manager of foreign exchange** | Manages forex reserves; administers **FEMA 1999** |
| **Payment system regulator** | Under the **Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007**; NEFT, RTGS run by RBI; UPI by NPCI (an RBI- and IBA-promoted body) |
| **Developmental role** | Financial inclusion, priority sector lending, rural credit (set up NABARD, earlier IDBI etc.) |

> **Prelims angle:** RTGS (Real Time Gross Settlement) — large value, real time; NEFT — batch-wise, now 24×7 (since December 2019). Both run by RBI. **UPI** and **RuPay** are run by **NPCI (2008)**.

- **Scheduled banks** — listed in the **Second Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934**; need paid-up capital and reserves of at least **₹5 lakh**; can borrow from RBI.
- **Public sector banks (PSBs)** — Government holds majority share. After the mergers of **2017–2020**, there are **12 PSBs** (SBI, PNB, Bank of Baroda, Canara Bank, Union Bank of India, Bank of India, Indian Bank, Central Bank of India, Indian Overseas Bank, UCO Bank, Bank of Maharashtra, Punjab & Sind Bank) (as of 2026 — check for further mergers).
- **Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)** — set up in **1975** (Narasimham Working Group), **RRB Act 1976**; first RRB **Prathama Bank**, Moradabad, **2 October 1975**. Ownership: **Centre 50%, sponsor bank 35%, state government 15%**.
- **Small Finance Banks** and **Payments Banks** — licensed from **2015** (ideas from the **Nachiket Mor Committee**, 2014). Payments banks can take deposits (up to ₹2 lakh per customer) but **cannot give loans**. Example: India Post Payments Bank (2018).
- **Cooperative banks** — three-tier rural structure: **State Cooperative Bank → District Central Cooperative Bank → Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS)**. Dual control by RBI and Registrar of Cooperative Societies (RBI's role strengthened by the Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020).

> **Bihar connection:** Under the "**One State, One RRB**" policy, **Uttar Bihar Gramin Bank** and **Dakshin Bihar Gramin Bank** were merged into a single **Bihar Gramin Bank** (HQ **Patna**, sponsor **Punjab National Bank**) with effect from **1 May 2025** (as of 2026 — check the latest). The **State Level Bankers' Committee (SLBC)** of Bihar is convened by **State Bank of India**.

## Bank nationalisation and lead bank scheme

- **1955** — **Imperial Bank of India** became **State Bank of India** (on the recommendation of the **All India Rural Credit Survey Committee, A.D. Gorwala**).
- **19 July 1969** — **14 banks** with deposits over **₹50 crore** nationalised (PM Indira Gandhi).
- **15 April 1980** — **6 more banks** with deposits over **₹200 crore** nationalised.
- Goals: take banking to rural areas, lend to agriculture and small industry, end control by business houses.
- **Lead Bank Scheme (1969)** — based on the **Gadgil Study Group** and **Nariman Committee**; each district has a lead bank that coordinates credit planning.
- **Priority Sector Lending (PSL)** — banks must lend **40%** of their adjusted net bank credit to priority sectors: agriculture (**18%**, with **10%** to small and marginal farmers), micro enterprises (**7.5%**), weaker sections (**12%**), plus education, housing, renewable energy, etc. (Regional rural banks and small finance banks have a **75%** target.)

> **Prelims angle:** Number of banks nationalised in 1969 = **14**; in 1980 = **6**. SBI was formed in **1955**, not nationalised in 1969.

## Banking sector reforms and bad loans

### Committees

| Committee | Year | Key recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| **Narasimham Committee I** | 1991 | Reduce CRR and SLR, deregulate interest rates, allow private banks, capital adequacy norms, Asset Reconstruction Funds |
| **Narasimham Committee II** | 1998 | Merge strong banks, stricter NPA norms, reduce government stake |
| **Nachiket Mor Committee** | 2014 | Financial inclusion; payments banks; universal bank account |
| **P.J. Nayak Committee** | 2014 | Governance of PSBs; **Bank Boards Bureau** (2016) |
| **Urjit Patel Committee** | 2014 | Flexible inflation targeting, CPI as anchor |
| **Bimal Jalan Committee** | 2019 | RBI's economic capital framework; surplus transfer to Govt |

### Non-Performing Assets (NPAs)

- An **NPA** is a loan whose interest or principal is **overdue for more than 90 days**.
- Categories: **Sub-standard** (NPA up to 12 months) → **Doubtful** (more than 12 months) → **Loss** (unrecoverable).
- **Gross NPA** = total bad loans; **Net NPA** = Gross NPA − provisions.
- Indian banks' gross NPA ratio peaked around **11–12% in 2018** and fell to around **2–2.5%** by 2025 — a multi-decade low (as of 2026 — check the latest RBI Financial Stability Report).
- **Twin balance-sheet problem** (Economic Survey 2016-17): both over-borrowed companies and bad-loan-laden banks.

Tools to recover bad loans:

| Tool | Year |
|---|---|
| Debt Recovery Tribunals | 1993 |
| **SARFAESI Act** (banks can seize secured assets) | **2002** |
| Lok Adalats | — |
| **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC)** — time-bound resolution through NCLT | **2016** |
| **National Asset Reconstruction Company (NARCL — "bad bank")** | 2021 |
| Recapitalisation and **4R strategy** (Recognition, Resolution, Recapitalisation, Reforms) | 2015 onwards |

### Capital adequacy: Basel norms

- **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision** sits at the **Bank for International Settlements (BIS), Basel, Switzerland**.
- **Capital to Risk-weighted Assets Ratio (CRAR)** — Basel III minimum is **8%**; the RBI requires **9%** (plus a capital conservation buffer of 2.5%, total **11.5%**).
- Basel I (1988), Basel II (2004), **Basel III (2010)** after the 2008 crisis.

### Deposit insurance

- **DICGC** (Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation), a subsidiary of the RBI, insures bank deposits up to **₹5 lakh per depositor per bank** (raised from ₹1 lakh in **February 2020**) (as of 2026 — check the latest).

## Financial inclusion

**Financial inclusion** means giving affordable bank accounts, credit, insurance and pension to all, especially the poor.

| Scheme | Year | What |
|---|---|---|
| **Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY)** | **28 August 2014** | Zero-balance account, RuPay card, accident cover; over 55 crore accounts (check latest) |
| **PM Jeevan Jyoti Bima Yojana** | 2015 | Life cover ₹2 lakh, low premium |
| **PM Suraksha Bima Yojana** | 2015 | Accident cover ₹2 lakh |
| **Atal Pension Yojana** | 2015 | Pension for unorganised workers |
| **PM MUDRA Yojana** | 2015 | Loans to micro units — Shishu, Kishore, Tarun, Tarun Plus (loan limit raised to ₹20 lakh in 2024) |
| **Stand-Up India** | 2016 | Loans ₹10 lakh–₹1 crore to SC/ST and women entrepreneurs |
| **JAM trinity** | — | **J**an Dhan + **A**adhaar + **M**obile → Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) |

The RBI publishes a **Financial Inclusion Index (FI-Index)** every year (launched **2021**), with three parameters: **access, usage, quality**.

> **Bihar connection:** **JEEViKA** (Bihar Rural Livelihoods Project, started **2006–07** with World Bank support) has organised over **one crore women** into **self-help groups (SHGs)** linked to banks (check latest numbers). SHG–bank linkage is a major channel of credit for poor women in Bihar. The **Bihar Student Credit Card Scheme** (part of Saat Nischay, **2016**) gives education loans up to ₹4 lakh through the state's own **Bihar State Education Finance Corporation**.

## Bihar's credit–deposit ratio problem

The **Credit–Deposit (C-D) ratio** = (Total loans given by banks in an area ÷ Total deposits collected there) × 100.

### Worked example

Banks in a district collect ₹10,000 crore in deposits and lend ₹5,800 crore in the same district.

C-D ratio = 5,800 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = **58%**

It means ₹42 of every ₹100 deposited by local people is lent **outside** the district — the savings of a poor region finance richer regions.

- India's average C-D ratio is roughly **75–80%**. Bihar's C-D ratio has long been among the **lowest of all states**; reports from the state banking review put it at about **58–60% in 2025-26**, the highest Bihar has reached (as of 2026 — check the latest SLBC data).
- Reasons: few large industries to borrow, small landholdings and lack of land records for collateral, risk of floods, low branch density in rural areas, high deposits from migrant remittances.
- Remedies discussed: credit camps, Kisan Credit Cards, land-record digitisation, SHG–bank linkage, credit guarantee schemes, more branches and banking correspondents.

> **Mains angle:** "Low C-D ratio = capital flight from Bihar." Link it to industrialisation, agricultural credit and financial inclusion. Suggest district-wise credit plans (lead bank), priority to agro-processing, and digital land records as collateral.
