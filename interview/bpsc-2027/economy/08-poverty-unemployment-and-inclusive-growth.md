Poverty and unemployment are the centre of every Mains answer about Bihar. Prelims asks about committees on the poverty line (Tendulkar, Rangarajan), the dimensions of the Multidimensional Poverty Index, the meaning of disguised and seasonal unemployment, PLFS terms, and employment schemes. Mains asks why Bihar has high poverty and migration and what inclusive growth means.

> **In simple words:** A person is poor if he or she cannot afford a minimum level of food, clothing, housing, health and education. India once measured poverty only by money spent on consumption; it now also uses a multidimensional index that looks at health, education and living standards. Unemployment means people who want work and are looking for it do not find it. Bihar has the highest share of multidimensionally poor people, but also the largest fall in poverty in recent years.

## What is poverty?

- **Absolute poverty** — income or consumption below a fixed minimum (the **poverty line**). Used in India.
- **Relative poverty** — being poor compared with others in the society (e.g. bottom 20%). Used more in rich countries.
- **Chronic** vs **transient** poverty — always poor vs poor for a period (after flood, illness).
- **Head Count Ratio (HCR)** — percentage of the population below the poverty line.
- **Poverty gap** — how far below the line the poor are, on average.

### Worked example: head count ratio

A village has 1,000 people. The poverty line is ₹1,800 per person per month (consumption). 230 people spend less than that.

HCR = 230 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **23%**

If average spending of the poor is ₹1,500, the average poverty gap is ₹300 per person (≈ 17% below the line).

## Measuring poverty in India: a history

| Year | Committee / estimate | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| 1901 | **Dadabhai Naoroji** | First poverty line idea ("jail cost of living") in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* |
| 1962 | Planning Commission Working Group | ₹20 per person per month (1960-61 prices) |
| **1971** | **V.M. Dandekar and N. Rath** | First systematic study; **2,250 calories** per day |
| **1979** | **Y.K. Alagh** Task Force | Calorie norms: **2,400 rural, 2,100 urban** |
| **1993** | **D.T. Lakdawala** | State-specific poverty lines; CPI-AL (rural) and CPI-IW (urban) for updating |
| **2009** | **Suresh Tendulkar** | Moved away from calories to a **basket including health and education**; uniform basket (Urban PLB) for rural and urban; **2011-12: 21.9%** poor (rural 25.7, urban 13.7); line ≈ **₹27/day rural, ₹33/day urban** |
| **2014** | **C. Rangarajan** | **2011-12: 29.5%** poor; line ≈ **₹32/day rural, ₹47/day urban**; government did not formally adopt it |
| 2015 | NITI Aayog Task Force (**Arvind Panagariya**) | Could not agree on a single line; suggested tracking the bottom 30% |

- India has **no official poverty line** after 2011-12 because the **2017-18 consumption survey** results were **not released** (data quality concerns).
- **Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES)** was done in **2022-23** and **2023-24** (NSO). It showed average monthly per-capita consumption (MPCE) for 2023-24 of about **₹4,122 (rural)** and **₹6,996 (urban)**, and a narrowing rural–urban gap. The share of food in spending fell below 50% even in rural areas (check the latest).

> **Bihar connection:** By the Tendulkar method, **33.7%** of Bihar's people were poor in **2011-12** — among the highest after Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Manipur, Arunachal Pradesh and Odisha. By the Rangarajan method the figure was about **41%**. Bihar's MPCE in HCES 2023-24 is among the **lowest** of all states.

### International poverty line

- The **World Bank** sets the **international extreme poverty line**. In **June 2025** it was raised from **$2.15** to **$3.00 per person per day** (in **2021 PPP** dollars).
- On this new line, World Bank estimates show India's extreme poverty fell from about **27% in 2011-12** to about **5.3% in 2022-23** (as of 2025 — check the latest Poverty and Equity Brief).
- **SDG Goal 1**: end poverty in all its forms everywhere by **2030**.

## Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)

The **MPI** looks at deprivations in non-income areas. It was developed by **OPHI (Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative)** and **UNDP** in **2010** using the **Alkire–Foster method**.

- A person is **multidimensionally poor** if deprived in at least **one-third (33.33%)** of the weighted indicators.
- **MPI = H × A**, where **H** = headcount ratio (share of poor people) and **A** = intensity (average share of deprivations among the poor).

### Worked example: MPI

In a district, 20% of people are multidimensionally poor (H = 0.20), and on average they are deprived in 45% of the weighted indicators (A = 0.45).

MPI = 0.20 × 0.45 = **0.090**

If H falls to 0.15 and A to 0.42, MPI = 0.063 — poverty has fallen both in **spread** and in **depth**.

### India's national MPI (NITI Aayog)

**Three dimensions, 12 indicators** (global MPI has 10; India adds **maternal health** and **bank account**):

| Dimension (weight) | Indicators |
|---|---|
| **Health (1/3)** | Nutrition, child and adolescent mortality, **maternal health** |
| **Education (1/3)** | Years of schooling, school attendance |
| **Standard of living (1/3)** | Cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets, **bank account** |

Key results (NITI Aayog):

| Report | Finding |
|---|---|
| **National MPI 2023** (NFHS-4 2015-16 vs NFHS-5 2019-21) | Poor fell from **24.85%** to **14.96%**; about **13.5 crore** people escaped poverty |
| **Discussion paper, January 2024** (2013-14 to 2022-23) | Poor fell from **29.17%** to **11.28%**; about **24.82 crore** people escaped multidimensional poverty in nine years |

- **UP, Bihar, MP, Odisha, Rajasthan** saw the biggest absolute falls. **UP** had the largest number lifted out (about 5.9 crore), followed by **Bihar** (about **3.8 crore**).
- **Bihar had the highest share of MPI poor** in NFHS-5 (about **33.8%** in 2019-21), down from about **51.9%** in 2015-16 — the **fastest decline** among states in percentage-point terms.

> **Prelims angle:** India's national MPI has **12** indicators; the global MPI has **10**. National MPI is prepared by **NITI Aayog** with OPHI and UNDP; data from **NFHS**. Global MPI is published by **UNDP and OPHI**.

## Inequality

- **Lorenz curve** — shows the cumulative share of income held by the cumulative share of people. The straight 45° line is perfect equality. The more the curve bends away, the more unequal the society.
- **Gini coefficient** — area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve ÷ total area below the line of equality. **0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality** (sometimes written 0–100).

- India's consumption Gini is relatively low (about **0.25–0.30** by World Bank estimates from HCES 2022-23), but **income and wealth inequality are much higher** (World Inequality Lab estimates show the top 10% hold about 57–60% of income).
- **Kuznets curve** — inequality first rises with development and then falls (inverted-U).

## Unemployment

**Unemployment** = a person who is **able and willing to work** at the current wage and is **actively looking** for work, but does not get it.

### Types of unemployment

| Type | Meaning | Example in India |
|---|---|---|
| **Disguised** | More people working than needed; **marginal productivity zero** | 6 family members on a 1-acre plot where 3 are enough — common in **Bihar's agriculture** |
| **Seasonal** | No work in off-season | Farm workers idle between sowing and harvest |
| **Structural** | Mismatch between skills and jobs, or lack of capital | Unskilled youth in a region with no factories |
| **Frictional** | Short gap while changing jobs | Worker moving between two jobs |
| **Cyclical** | Due to a slowdown in the business cycle | Job losses in 2008, COVID-2020 |
| **Educated unemployment** | Graduates without suitable jobs | Common among youth |
| **Open unemployment** | Visible, no work at all | Urban job seekers |
| **Underemployment** | Working less than full time or below skill level | A graduate working as a delivery rider part time |
| **Technological** | Jobs lost to machines/automation | AI, robots |
| **Voluntary** | Not willing to work at the current wage | — |

> **Common mistake:** In **disguised** unemployment the person *appears* employed. Removing the extra worker does **not** reduce output. In **seasonal** unemployment there is no work in some months.

### Measuring employment: PLFS

- **Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS)** by **NSO (MoSPI)**, started **2017-18** (replaced the five-yearly NSSO employment surveys and Labour Bureau surveys).
- From **2025**, PLFS gives **monthly** estimates (rural + urban) and **quarterly** ones, besides the annual report.

| Status | Reference period |
|---|---|
| **Usual Status (UPSS — principal + subsidiary)** | Last **365 days**; **major time** criterion (principal) + at least 30 days (subsidiary) |
| **Current Weekly Status (CWS)** | Last **7 days**; employed if worked at least 1 hour on any day |
| **Current Daily Status (CDS)** | Each day of the last week (half-day units) |

Key ratios:

<div class="formula">

| Ratio | Formula |
|---|---|
| **LFPR** (Labour Force Participation Rate) | (Employed + Unemployed) ÷ Population × 100 |
| **WPR** (Worker Population Ratio) | Employed ÷ Population × 100 |
| **UR** (Unemployment Rate) | Unemployed ÷ Labour force × 100 |

</div>

#### Worked example: PLFS ratios

A town has 10,000 people aged 15+. 5,500 are employed and 300 are looking for work.

- Labour force = 5,500 + 300 = **5,800**
- LFPR = 5,800 ÷ 10,000 = **58%**
- WPR = 5,500 ÷ 10,000 = **55%**
- UR = 300 ÷ 5,800 × 100 ≈ **5.2%**

Notice that UR is calculated on the **labour force**, not on the total population.

National picture (as of 2026 — check the latest PLFS):

- Unemployment rate (usual status, 15+) about **3–3.5%**; much higher among **educated youth** (15–29 years) in urban areas.
- **Female LFPR** has risen sharply — from about **23% in 2017-18** to about **40%+** in recent years, mostly as **self-employed** women in rural areas.
- About **58%** of workers are **self-employed**, about **22%** regular salaried, about **20%** casual labourers.
- Agriculture's share in employment is about **45%**.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar has one of the **lowest LFPR and WPR** in India, and the **lowest female LFPR** among large states. A low unemployment rate in Bihar is **misleading**: many people (especially women) are **not in the labour force** at all, and many youth migrate out. This is called the **discouraged-worker effect**. Use the latest PLFS state tables for exact numbers.

## Migration from Bihar

- Bihar is one of the **largest sources of inter-state migrants** in India (with UP). Census 2011 migration tables and the **Economic Survey 2016-17** (using railway passenger data) show heavy flows to **Delhi, Maharashtra, Punjab, Haryana, Gujarat, West Bengal, Kerala**.
- Types: **seasonal/circular** (for harvest in Punjab, brick kilns), **long-term** (construction, factories, security guards), **student migration** (to Kota, Delhi).
- **Push factors**: small holdings, floods, lack of non-farm jobs, low wages, caste-based landholding patterns.
- **Pull factors**: higher wages, demand for construction and factory labour.
- Effects: **remittances** support rural consumption (Bihar is among the top recipients of domestic remittances); **feminisation of agriculture**; child education may suffer; migrants face poor housing and exclusion (COVID-2020 reverse migration).
- Steps: **e-Shram** portal (**August 2021**) — national database of unorganised workers; Bihar is among the states with the **most registrations** (UP first; check the latest). **ONORC** for ration portability; **Bihar Pravasi Majdoor Durghatna Anudan Yojana** for migrant accident relief.

> **Mains angle:** "Migration from Bihar — distress or opportunity?" Distress: push factors and exploitation. Opportunity: remittances, skills, social change (women gain decision-making roles). Policy: skill-based migration, safe-migration support centres, portable welfare, local non-farm jobs.

### Evolution (Prelims timeline)

| Year | Programme |
|---|---|

| 1960s | Intensive Agricultural District Programme |
| 1970s | SFDA, MFAL, **Food for Work (1977)**, **Minimum Needs Programme (5th Plan)** |
| **1978–80** | **IRDP** (Integrated Rural Development Programme) — assets to poor families |
| 1979 | **TRYSEM** — training rural youth for self-employment |
| 1980 | **NREP**; 1983 **RLEGP** |
| **1989** | **Jawahar Rozgar Yojana (JRY)** — NREP and RLEGP merged |
| **1993** | **Employment Assurance Scheme**; **PMRY** (urban educated youth) |
| **1999** | **SGSY** (Swarnjayanti Gram Swarozgar Yojana) — replaced IRDP and others |
| **2001** | **Sampoorna Grameen Rozgar Yojana (SGRY)** |
| **2005** | **NREGA** (renamed **MGNREGA** in **2009**) — legal guarantee of **100 days** |
| **2011** | **Aajeevika — National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM)**, renamed **DAY-NRLM** (2016) — replaced SGSY |
| 2013 | **National Urban Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NULM)** |
| **2014** | **DDU-GKY** — placement-linked skilling of rural youth |
| **2015** | **PMKVY** (Skill India) |
| 2020 | **PM SVANidhi** — loans for street vendors |
| **2025 Act; in force 1 July 2026** | **Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025 (VB-G RAM G)** replaces MGNREGA |

### MGNREGA (2005–2026) — key features

- **Act passed** in **September 2005**, launched on **2 February 2006** in **200 districts**, first at **Bandlapalli, Anantapur (Andhra Pradesh)**. Extended to all districts by **April 2008**.
- **100 days** of guaranteed wage employment per rural household per year for adult members who volunteer for **unskilled manual work**.
- Work within **5 km**; if more, 10% extra wage. Work within **15 days** of application, else **unemployment allowance**.
- At least **one-third** of beneficiaries to be **women**.
- **Gram Sabha** plans works; **Gram Panchayat** executes at least 50% of works by cost.
- **Social audit** by the Gram Sabha; payments by **DBT**.
- Funding: Centre paid **100% of unskilled wages** and **75% of material** cost.

### VB-G RAM G Act (from 1 July 2026)

The **Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025** came into force across rural India on **1 July 2026**, **repealing MGNREGA** (as of 2026 — check the latest rules):

- Guarantee raised to **125 days** per rural household per year.
- Funding mostly shared **60:40** (Centre:state); **90:10** for north-eastern and Himalayan states.
- Ongoing MGNREGA works carried over; existing job cards valid until new **Gramin Rozgar Guarantee Cards** are issued.
- Debate: supporters cite more days and a focus on durable assets; critics worry that the **higher state share** will burden poorer states like **Bihar** and that the demand-driven nature may weaken.

> **Remember:** Many Prelims questions from older years still say "MGNREGA". Learn both: MGNREGA (2005, 100 days) and its replacement VB-G RAM G (2025 Act, 125 days, in force 1 July 2026). Track the rules and the state share in current affairs.

It runs Didi ki Rasoi canteens, Jeevika producer companies, and helped in the prohibition campaign of 2016. ## Inclusive growth

**Inclusive growth** is growth that **benefits all sections of society**, especially the poor, women, SC/ST, minorities and backward regions, and creates **broad-based opportunities**. It was the theme of the **11th Plan (2007–12)**.

Pillars:

1. **Employment-intensive growth** (agriculture, MSME, construction, labour-intensive manufacturing).
2. **Human development** — health and education for all.
3. **Social protection** — food, pensions, insurance.
4. **Financial inclusion** — Jan Dhan, credit.
5. **Regional balance** — backward area programmes, Aspirational Districts.
6. **Gender equality** — women's work, property and safety.
7. **Good governance** — DBT, social audit, RTI.

### Jobless growth

**Jobless growth** = GDP grows but employment grows very slowly. India's **employment elasticity** (% change in jobs ÷ % change in GDP) has been low. Reasons: capital-intensive industry, services-led growth, slow manufacturing, labour-law rigidities, skill gaps.

> **Mains angle:** For "Is India's growth inclusive?", use: falling MPI (24.82 crore out of poverty), rising female LFPR, DBT and JAM; but also rising wealth inequality, informal work (90%), youth unemployment, regional gaps (Bihar vs Maharashtra), and malnutrition. Conclude with a balanced view.
