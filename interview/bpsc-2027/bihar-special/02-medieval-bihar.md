Medieval Bihar (about 1200 to 1757) was a frontier province between Delhi and Bengal. It gave India one of its best administrators, **Sher Shah Suri**, and the tenth Sikh Guru, **Guru Gobind Singh**. BPSC asks about Sher Shah's battles and reforms, the Sufi saints of Maner and Bihar Sharif, Mithila's dynasties and Vidyapati, Mughal Patna and the Sikh shrines.

> **In simple words:** After Bakhtiyar Khilji's raids (c. 1193–1200), Bihar came under the Delhi Sultans, then under the Sharqi kings of Jaunpur and Afghan chiefs. North Bihar (Mithila) kept its own Hindu kings for a long time. An Afghan from Sasaram, Sher Shah, defeated the Mughal emperor Humayun and ruled India for five years. Then Akbar made Bihar a Mughal province (subah) with Patna as its capital.

All-India background: [Delhi Sultanate](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/delhi-sultanate), [Mughal Empire and Sher Shah](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/mughal-empire-and-sher-shah), [Bhakti, Sufi and Regional Kingdoms](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/bhakti-sufi-and-regional-kingdoms).

## How Bihar got its name

The word **Bihar** comes from **vihara**, meaning a Buddhist monastery. The region had so many viharas (Nalanda, Odantapuri, Vikramshila) that Turkish writers called the town of Odantapuri "Bihar". The town became **Bihar Sharif**, and the name later spread to the whole province. The chronicler **Minhaj-us-Siraj** in *Tabaqat-i-Nasiri* describes Bakhtiyar's men taking a walled vihara which they thought was a fort.

> **Prelims angle:** "The name Bihar is derived from" — **vihara** (monastery). Bihar Sharif was the early Turkish headquarters in the region.

## Bakhtiyar Khilji and the Delhi Sultanate in Bihar

- **Ikhtiyar-ud-din Muhammad bin Bakhtiyar Khilji**, a Turkish commander serving **Qutbuddin Aibak**, raided south Bihar in the 1190s. His forces sacked **Odantapuri**, **Nalanda** and later **Vikramshila** (c. 1193–1203). He then attacked **Nadia** in Bengal (c. 1204), defeating **Lakshmana Sena**.
- **Iltutmish** (1211–36) brought Bihar firmly under Delhi and appointed governors.
- Bihar was often controlled by the governors of **Lakhnauti (Bengal)**, who sometimes rebelled against Delhi.
- **Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq** conquered **Tirhut (Mithila)** in **1324**, ending the **Karnat** dynasty; its king **Harisimhadeva** fled to Nepal.
- **Muhammad bin Tughlaq** and **Firoz Shah Tughlaq** kept Bihar under Delhi. The tomb of the Tughlaq governor **Ibrahim Malik Bayu** (d. 1353) stands on a hill at **Bihar Sharif** (Pir Pahari).
- In the 15th century, the **Sharqi Sultans of Jaunpur** ruled much of west and north Bihar. **Sikandar Lodi** later brought Bihar back under Delhi (1490s).
- In the early 16th century, the **Lohani Afghans** ruled south Bihar from Patna region; **Bahar Khan Lohani** declared himself **Sultan Muhammad**. Sher Khan (later Sher Shah) rose in his service.

## Mithila: the Karnat and Oinwar dynasties

North Bihar (Mithila or Tirhut) had its own Hindu kingdoms and a rich Maithili and Sanskrit culture.

| Dynasty | Period | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Karnat** (Karnata) | **1097–1324** | Founded by **Nanyadeva** in **1097**; capital **Simraongarh** (now in Nepal's Terai); **Harisimhadeva** started the **Panji** system (genealogical records of Maithil Brahmins and Kayasthas, used for marriage rules); court poet **Jyotirishwar Thakur** wrote ***Varnaratnakara*** (early 14th c), the **earliest known Maithili prose** work; ended by Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq in 1324 |
| **Oinwar (Oiniwar)** | c. 1325–1526 | Brahmin rulers who ruled as vassals of Delhi/Jaunpur; **Kameshwar Thakur** founder; **Shivasimha** (Shiva Singh) was the patron of **Vidyapati**; Vidyapati's ***Kirtilata*** praises Kirtisimha |
| **Khandavala** (later **Darbhanga Raj**) | from the 16th century | Traditionally **Mahesh Thakur** received Mithila from **Akbar**; later the Darbhanga Maharajas became among the largest zamindars of British India |

**Vidyapati** (c. 1352–1448) — "**Maithil Kavi Kokil**" (the cuckoo poet of Mithila) — wrote love songs of Radha and Krishna (***Padavali***), Shiva songs (**Nachari**), and works in Sanskrit (*Purusha Pariksha*, *Likhanavali*, *Bhu-Parikrama*) and **Avahattha** (*Kirtilata*, *Kirtipataka*). His Padavali influenced Bengali Vaishnava poetry and Chaitanya's followers. More in [Art, Culture, Festivals and Languages](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/art-culture-festivals-and-languages).

> **Bihar connection:** The Panji system and Vidyapati's songs are still alive in Mithila — Vidyapati songs are sung at weddings, and "Vidyapati Parva" is celebrated each year.

## Sufi saints and centres in Bihar

**Sufis** are Muslim mystics who seek closeness to God through love, music and service. A **silsila** is a Sufi order (chain of teachers). A **dargah** is a saint's shrine. A **khanqah** is a Sufi hospice.

| Saint / place | Order | Facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Makhdum Yahya Maneri** | Suhrawardi / Firdausi links | Settled at **Maner** (Patna); his shrine is the **Badi Dargah** |
| **Makhdum Sharfuddin Ahmad Yahya Maneri** (1263–1381) | **Firdausi** | Most famous Sufi of Bihar; lived and died at **Bihar Sharif**; his letters ***Maktubat-i-Sadi*** ("Hundred Letters") are a classic of Sufi literature; called "Makhdum-ul-Mulk" |
| **Shah Daulat (Makhdum Daulat)** | — | Shrine at Maner known as the **Chhoti Dargah**, built **1616** by **Ibrahim Khan**, Jahangir's governor of Bihar — a fine Mughal tomb, sometimes called the "Taj of Bihar" |
| **Shah Arzani** | — | Dargah in Patna; annual urs |

The **Firdausi silsila** became the most important Sufi order in Bihar. Its saints wrote in Persian but spoke to common people in local speech, and helped make Bihar Sharif a centre of learning after Nalanda's fall.

> **Prelims angle:** "Maktubat-i-Sadi is associated with" — **Sharfuddin Yahya Maneri**, **Firdausi** order, **Bihar Sharif**. The **Chhoti Dargah** of Maner (1616) is Mughal; the **Badi Dargah** is the older shrine of Yahya Maneri.

## Sher Shah Suri — Bihar's great ruler

**Sher Shah Suri** is the most important medieval figure from Bihar. BPSC asks about him almost every cycle, in Prelims and in Mains culture/administration questions. Full Mughal context is in [Mughal Empire and Sher Shah](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/mughal-empire-and-sher-shah).

### Early life and rise

- Born **Farid Khan** (c. 1472 or 1486; sources differ; his birthplace is also debated — many accounts say **Sasaram**, some say Hisar/Narnaul in Haryana). His father **Hasan Khan Sur** held the jagir (land grant) of **Sasaram** and **Khawaspur Tanda** (Rohtas region). For the exam, **Sher Shah = Sasaram**.
- He managed his father's jagir and learnt revenue work at first hand.
- He got the title "**Sher Khan**" from **Bahar Khan Lohani** for killing a tiger (or lion).
- He became guardian (deputy) of Bahar Khan's young son **Jalal Khan**, and in practice ruled south Bihar.

### Battles

| Year | Battle / event | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1534** | **Battle of Surajgarh** (near Munger/Lakhisarai) | Sher Khan defeated the Sultan of **Bengal** (Mahmud Shah) and the Lohanis — he became master of Bihar |
| 1538 | Captured **Rohtasgarh** (Rohtas fort) | Strong base in the Kaimur hills |
| **26 June 1539** | **Battle of Chausa** (Buxar, on the Karmanasa–Ganga) | Defeated **Humayun**; took the title **Sher Shah** and had the khutba read in his name |
| **17 May 1540** | **Battle of Kannauj (Bilgram)** | Defeated Humayun again; Humayun fled to Persia; Sher Shah became emperor of north India |
| 1545 | Siege of **Kalinjar** | Died from a gunpowder explosion; succeeded by **Islam Shah** |

### Administration and reforms

Sher Shah ruled only about **five years (1540–45)**, but many of his systems were adopted by Akbar and even the British.

| Area | Reform |
|---|---|
| **Provinces** | Empire divided into **sarkars**; sarkars into **parganas**; each pargana had a **shiqdar** (law and order), **amin/munsif** (revenue), a treasurer and two writers (one Persian, one Hindi) |
| **Land revenue** | Land **measured** (using the sikandari gaz); revenue fixed at about **one-third** of average produce, based on crop rates (**ray**); peasants received a **patta** (title deed) and signed a **qabuliyat** (agreement) |
| **Currency** | Issued the silver **rupiya** (about 178 grains) and copper **dam** — the rupee name comes from him |
| **Roads** | Rebuilt the **Sadak-e-Azam** (later the **Grand Trunk Road**) from **Sonargaon (Bengal) to Peshawar region**, and other roads to Agra, Jodhpur, Multan; built about **1,700 sarais** (rest houses) with separate quarters for Hindus and Muslims |
| **Post** | Sarais also served as **dak chowkis** (post stations); horses carried news |
| **Police / justice** | Village headmen made responsible for local crime; strict justice |
| **Army** | Branding of horses (**dagh**) and descriptive rolls of soldiers (**chehra**), copied from Alauddin Khilji |
| **Patna** | Built a fort and laid out **Patna** as a city on the Ganga in **1541**, seeing its strategic position; the **Sher Shah Suri Masjid** in Patna is linked to him |

### Sher Shah's tomb at Sasaram

- An **octagonal** tomb standing in the middle of an **artificial lake**, built in red sandstone; completed in the reign of his son **Islam Shah** (1545).
- The architect is usually named as **Aliwal Khan**.
- It is one of the finest examples of **Indo-Afghan (Lodi–Sur) architecture**, a link between Lodi tombs and Mughal tombs like Humayun's.
- Sasaram also has the tomb of his father **Hasan Khan Sur** and the unfinished tomb of **Islam Shah**.

> **Mains angle:** "Sher Shah's administration was a bridge between the Sultanate and the Mughals." Points: rupiya; measured land revenue with patta–qabuliyat (adopted by Todar Mal under Akbar); roads and sarais; dak system; sarkar–pargana units; the Bihar base (Sasaram jagir experience). Close with: Akbar built on his foundation.

> **Memory trick:** "**S**asaram **S**her **S**hah — **S**arai, **S**adak, **S**ilver rupiya, **S**urajgarh 1534" — four S's for his achievements and his first great win.

## Mughal Bihar

- After Humayun's return (1555) and Akbar's rise, Bihar was held by Afghan **Karrani** rulers of Bengal. **Munim Khan** and **Akbar** himself captured **Patna** from **Daud Khan Karrani** in **1574**.
- In **1580**, Akbar made **Bihar one of the original twelve subahs** (provinces) of the empire, with **Patna** as capital.
- In **1580–81**, Mughal officers in Bihar and Bengal rebelled over strict branding rules and pay; Akbar's forces crushed the revolt.
- **Raja Man Singh** served as governor of Bihar (1587–94) and used the **Rohtas fort** as a base before going to Bengal.
- **Jahangir's** son **Parvez** was governor; the **Patthar ki Masjid** ("stone mosque") on the Ganga bank at Patna was built in his time, in **1621**.
- **Saif Khan**, Shah Jahan's governor, built a madrasa and mosque in Patna (1630s).
- **Azim-us-Shan**, grandson of Aurangzeb and governor of Bengal and Bihar, renamed Patna "**Azimabad**" in **1704**.

### Patna as a trading city

**Patna** became one of the biggest trade centres of north India. Its goods — **saltpetre** (used to make gunpowder), **cotton cloth**, **opium**, sugar, indigo and grain — went down the Ganga to Bengal and overseas.

| Visitor / company | Year | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Ralph Fitch** (English traveller) | 1586 | Described Patna's cotton and sugar trade |
| **English East India Company** | **1620** (factory), restarted **1651** | Patna factory for saltpetre and textiles (the later opium factory site at Gulzarbagh is now a government press) |
| **Dutch** | 17th century | Also had a factory at Patna for saltpetre |
| **Peter Mundy** | 1632 | Described Patna as the greatest city of the region |

> **Bihar connection:** Because of **saltpetre**, Bihar mattered in European wars — every European company wanted Patna's saltpetre for gunpowder. This is one reason the British fought hard for Bihar after Plassey.

### Bihar under the Nawabs of Bengal

In the 18th century the Mughal empire weakened. Bihar was ruled by a **naib nazim** (deputy governor) under the Nawab of Bengal.

- **Alivardi Khan** was naib nazim of Bihar (from 1733) before seizing the Bengal throne in **1740**.
- Afghan soldiers rebelled at Patna in **1748** and killed **Zainuddin Ahmad**, father of Siraj-ud-Daulah.
- **Raja Ram Narayan** was deputy governor of Bihar under Siraj and Mir Jafar.
- The story continues with Plassey, Buxar and Company rule in [Modern Bihar and 1857](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/modern-bihar-and-1857).

## The Sikh connection — Patna Sahib

Bihar holds one of the **five Takhts** (seats of authority) of Sikhism.

- **Guru Nanak** (1st Guru) visited Patna in the early 16th century; **Gurudwara Gai Ghat** marks his stay.
- **Guru Tegh Bahadur** (9th Guru) came to Patna with his family in the 1660s on his eastern tour.
- **Guru Gobind Singh** (10th Guru) was **born at Patna on 22 December 1666** (Poh Sudi Saptami by the lunar calendar). He spent his early childhood here before leaving for Anandpur (Punjab) around 1672.
- **Takhat Sri Harmandir Ji, Patna Sahib** marks his birthplace. The shrine was built by **Maharaja Ranjit Singh** in the 19th century; after damage in the **1934 earthquake**, the present building was completed in the 1950s.
- Other Patna gurudwaras: **Bal Lila** (childhood play), **Guru ka Bagh**, **Kangan Ghat** (where the child Guru threw a bangle into the Ganga), **Handi Sahib** (Danapur).
- The **350th Prakash Parv** (birth anniversary) of Guru Gobind Singh was celebrated at Patna in **January 2017**.

| Takht | Place |
|---|---|
| Akal Takht | Amritsar (Punjab) |
| **Takht Sri Harmandir Ji (Patna Sahib)** | **Patna (Bihar)** |
| Takht Sri Kesgarh Sahib | Anandpur Sahib (Punjab) |
| Takht Sri Hazur Sahib | Nanded (Maharashtra) |
| Takht Sri Damdama Sahib | Talwandi Sabo (Punjab) |

> **Prelims angle:** "Patna Sahib is the birthplace of" — **Guru Gobind Singh** (not Guru Nanak, not Guru Tegh Bahadur). It is the only Takht in eastern India.

### Mains questions

**1. Evaluate the administrative reforms of Sher Shah Suri and their lasting impact. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** An Afghan from Sasaram who ruled just five years (1540–45) but left systems that lasted centuries.
- **Body:** Early training in the Sasaram jagir; sarkar–pargana structure with shiqdar, amin, qanungo; measured land and one-third share; patta–qabuliyat; rupiya and dam; Sadak-e-Azam and 1,700 sarais; dak chowkis; branding of horses; local responsibility for crime; fair treatment of peasants.
- **Conclusion:** Akbar and Todar Mal built on his revenue system; the rupee and the GT Road remain — a proud Bihar contribution to Indian administration.

**2. Discuss the contribution of Sufi saints to the society and culture of medieval Bihar. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Bihar became a major Sufi centre after the 13th century.
- **Body:** Firdausi silsila; Yahya Maneri at Maner; Sharfuddin Maneri at Bihar Sharif and *Maktubat-i-Sadi*; khanqahs as centres of learning and charity; use of local speech; message of love, equality and service; Hindu–Muslim cultural mixing; architecture — Badi and Chhoti Dargah; urs festivals.
- **Conclusion:** Sufis helped create Bihar's syncretic (mixed) culture, seen even today at Maner and Bihar Sharif.

**3. "Mithila preserved a distinct cultural identity in medieval times." Explain. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** While south Bihar came under Turkish and Afghan rulers, Mithila kept Hindu ruling houses.
- **Body:** Karnat dynasty (Nanyadeva, 1097); Panji system; *Varnaratnakara*; Nyaya and Mimamsa scholarship; Oinwar dynasty and Vidyapati; Maithili songs and paintings; later Darbhanga Raj patronage.
- **Conclusion:** This tradition led to Maithili's inclusion in the Eighth Schedule (2003) and the global fame of Mithila painting.
