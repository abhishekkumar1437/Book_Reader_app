For about a thousand years (roughly 600 BCE to 500 CE), the history of India was written mostly in Bihar. Magadha became India's first great empire, Pataliputra was its capital, Vaishali was one of the world's earliest republics, and Nalanda was the most famous university of Asia. BPSC asks Prelims questions on dynasties, founders, councils, sites and inscriptions in Bihar, and Mains questions on why Magadha rose and what Bihar gave to Indian civilisation.

> **In simple words:** Ancient Bihar had three powerful regions — Magadha (south of the Ganga, around Rajgir, Gaya and Patna), Vajji/Videha (north of the Ganga, around Vaishali and Mithila) and Anga (east, around Bhagalpur). Magadha swallowed the others and built empires under the Haryankas, Nandas, Mauryas and Guptas. Buddhism and Jainism grew here. Later, the Pala kings made Bihar a world centre of Buddhist learning.

This chapter gives the **Bihar-specific** facts. For the all-India story read the history book: [Mahajanapadas, Buddhism and Jainism](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/mahajanapadas-buddhism-and-jainism), [The Mauryan Empire](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/mauryan-empire), [Post-Mauryan and Gupta Age](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/post-mauryan-and-gupta-age) and [Harsha and Early Medieval India](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/harsha-and-early-medieval-india).

## Prehistoric and early sites of Bihar

Long before kingdoms, people lived in Bihar's river valleys and hills. **Archaeology** (the study of the past through objects dug from the ground) gives us these sites.

| Site | District | Period | Why it is famous |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chirand** | Saran | Neolithic (New Stone Age) to later periods | Bihar's best-known Neolithic site, near the meeting of the Ghaghara and the Ganga; **bone tools**, pottery, early farming |
| **Chechar** | Vaishali | Neolithic–Chalcolithic | Village settlement remains on the Ganga |
| **Senuar** | Rohtas | Neolithic–Chalcolithic | Evidence of early rice farming and stone tools |
| **Taradih** | Gaya (near Bodh Gaya) | Neolithic to historic | Continuous layers of settlement under the Mahabodhi area |
| **Maner** | Patna | Chalcolithic onwards | Settlement on the old Son–Ganga confluence |
| Hills of Munger, Jamui, Nawada, Kaimur | South Bihar | Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age) | Stone tools; **rock paintings** in the Kaimur hills |

**Neolithic** means the New Stone Age, when people began farming and keeping animals and used polished stone tools. **Chalcolithic** means the "copper-stone" age, when copper came into use alongside stone.

> **Prelims angle:** "Chirand is in which district?" — **Saran**. Chirand is the Neolithic site most often asked from Bihar. Do not confuse it with Chechar (Vaishali).

## Bihar in the Vedic age

The **Vedic texts** (the oldest sacred texts of Hinduism, c. 1500–600 BCE) show the Aryan culture moving east from the Punjab into the Ganga plain.

- **Rigveda** mentions the **Kikatas**, a people whose ruler was **Pramaganda**; Kikata is usually identified with **Magadha**.
- The **Atharvaveda** mentions **Magadha** and **Anga** as distant lands, and calls the Magadhans "Vratyas" (people outside orthodox Vedic rules). This shows that early Vedic society looked down on the east.
- The **Shatapatha Brahmana** tells the story of **Videgha Mathava**, who carried the sacred fire (Agni) east and crossed the river **Sadanira** (identified with the **Gandak**). East of it he founded **Videha** (Mithila). This story is read as the spread of Vedic farming and settlement into north Bihar.
- **Videha / Mithila**: King **Janaka** held philosophical debates. The sage **Yajnavalkya** (a key teacher of the *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad*) and women scholars **Gargi** and **Maitreyi** are linked to his court. Sita of the Ramayana is called Janaki and Vaidehi, after Janaka and Videha.

> **Bihar connection:** The Upanishadic debates at Janaka's court are an early example of open intellectual discussion in Bihar — useful for a Mains culture answer or an essay on Bihar's knowledge tradition.

## The Mahajanapadas in Bihar

By the 6th century BCE, north India had **sixteen Mahajanapadas** ("great realms"), listed in the Buddhist text *Anguttara Nikaya*. Three of them lay in present-day Bihar.

| Mahajanapada | Capital | Present area | Type | Fate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Magadha** | **Girivraja / Rajagriha** (Rajgir), later **Pataliputra** | Patna, Nalanda, Gaya region | Monarchy | Became the empire that absorbed the rest |
| **Anga** | **Champa** (near Bhagalpur) | Bhagalpur–Munger region | Monarchy | Annexed by **Bimbisara** of Magadha |
| **Vajji (Vrijji)** | **Vaishali** | Vaishali–Muzaffarpur–Mithila | **Republic (gana-sangha)** | Defeated by **Ajatashatru** |

The **Malla** republic (capitals Kushinagar and Pava) lay mostly in present Uttar Pradesh, just west of Bihar.

### Vaishali and the Vajji republic

- The **Vajji confederacy** was a union of **eight (or nine) clans**. The most powerful were the **Lichchhavis**; others included the **Videhas**, the **Jnatrikas** (Mahavira's clan) and the Vajjis proper.
- Power lay with an assembly of clan heads, which met in a hall called the **santhagara**. Pali tradition speaks of **7,707 rajas** (clan heads) in Vaishali.
- Because decisions were made by an assembly and not one king, Vaishali is often called **one of the world's earliest republics**. (Historians say "republic" here means rule by a group of clan elites, not a modern democracy with votes for all.)
- The Buddha praised the Vajjis' **seven conditions of welfare** (meeting often, acting in harmony, respecting elders and women, keeping old customs). He said that as long as they followed these, they could not be defeated.
- The courtesan **Amrapali** of Vaishali gifted a mango grove to the Buddha and became a nun.
- The **Abhishek Pushkarini** (coronation tank) at Vaishali was where new Lichchhavi leaders were consecrated.

> **Common mistake:** Students write that Vaishali was the capital of Magadha. It was the capital of the **Vajji republic**. It became a temporary seat of Magadha only under **Shishunaga**.

1. **Iron**: rich iron deposits lay just to the south (today's Jharkhand). Iron gave better weapons and tools for clearing forests.
2. **Fertile soil and rain**: the middle Ganga plain grew rice in plenty, so kings could collect large land revenue and feed big armies.
3. **Rivers**: the **Ganga, Son, Gandak and Punpun** carried trade and served as natural moats.
4. **Elephants**: forests supplied war elephants, which Magadha used on a large scale.
5. **Strong capitals**: **Rajgir** was surrounded by **five hills** (Vaibhara, Ratna, Saila, Sona, Vipula); **Pataliputra** stood where the Ganga, Son and Gandak met — a "water fort" (jaladurga).
6. **Society and rulers**: Magadha's society was less bound by orthodox rules, and a series of ambitious kings (Bimbisara, Ajatashatru, Mahapadma Nanda, Chandragupta) used every method — marriage, war and diplomacy.

### Dynasties of Magadha (traditional dates)

Dates before the Mauryas are approximate. Exams use the traditional dates below.

| Dynasty | Key ruler | Main facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Brihadratha** (legendary) | **Jarasandha** | Mentioned in the Mahabharata; capital Girivraja. "Jarasandha ka Akhara" at Rajgir |
| **Haryanka** (c. 544–412 BCE) | **Bimbisara** (c. 544–492 BCE) | Founder of Magadha's greatness; marriage alliances with Kosala (Kosala Devi — Kashi village in dowry), the Lichchhavis (**Chellana**) and Madra (Khema); annexed **Anga**; sent royal physician **Jivaka** to treat Pradyota of Avanti; contemporary and patron of the Buddha (gifted **Venuvana**) |
| | **Ajatashatru** (c. 492–460 BCE) | Killed his father (tradition); defeated **Vajji** using new weapons **mahashilakantaka** (stone-throwing engine) and **rathamusala** (chariot with maces); built a fort at **Pataligrama**; **First Buddhist Council** at Rajgir held in his reign |
| | **Udayin** (c. 460–444 BCE) | Founded **Pataliputra** at the Ganga–Son confluence and shifted the capital there |
| **Shishunaga** (c. 412–344 BCE) | **Shishunaga** | Destroyed the power of **Avanti**; temporarily made **Vaishali** a capital |
| | **Kalashoka (Kakavarnin)** | **Second Buddhist Council** at **Vaishali** (c. 383 BCE); capital back to Pataliputra |
| **Nanda** (c. 344–322 BCE) | **Mahapadma Nanda** | Called "**Ekarat**" (sole ruler) and "**Sarva-kshatrantaka**" (destroyer of all Kshatriyas); first empire-builder of non-Kshatriya origin; the Hathigumpha inscription of Kharavela says a Nanda king dug a canal in Kalinga |
| | **Dhana Nanda** | Huge army and treasury; ruled when **Alexander** reached the Beas (326 BCE); overthrown by Chandragupta Maurya and Chanakya |

> **Memory trick:** "**B**ig **A**mbition **U**nder **S**trong **N**andas" — **B**imbisara, **A**jatashatru, **U**dayin, **S**hishunaga, **N**anda — the order of Magadha's rise.

> **Prelims angle:** Pairs that are asked: First Buddhist Council — **Rajgir, Ajatashatru, Mahakassapa**; Second — **Vaishali, Kalashoka, Sabakami**; Third — **Pataliputra, Ashoka, Moggaliputta Tissa**; Fourth — Kashmir, Kanishka (not Bihar). Founder of Pataliputra — **Udayin**, though Ajatashatru built the first fort at Pataligrama.

## The Mauryan empire and Pataliputra

**Chandragupta Maurya** (c. 322–298 BCE), guided by **Chanakya (Kautilya, Vishnugupta)**, overthrew Dhana Nanda and made Pataliputra the capital of an empire stretching from Afghanistan to Bengal. Full details are in [The Mauryan Empire](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/mauryan-empire). Here are the Bihar facts.

### Pataliputra as described by Megasthenes

**Megasthenes**, the Greek ambassador of **Seleucus Nicator** at Chandragupta's court, wrote *Indica* (now lost; known through quotations by later Greek writers). He called the city **Palibothra** and said:

- It was shaped like a parallelogram along the Ganga, about **14.5 km long and 2.5 km wide** (as quoted by later writers).
- It had a **wooden wall (palisade)** with **64 gates and 570 towers**, and a deep moat.
- The city was run by a **committee of 30 members in 6 boards of 5 each** (industry, foreigners, births and deaths, trade, manufactured goods, tax on sales). This is often called the first known description of **municipal administration** in India.

### Mauryan remains in Bihar

| Site | District | What is there |
|---|---|---|
| **Kumhrar** | Patna | Remains of an **80-pillared hall** of the Mauryan palace; wooden palisade remains also found at **Bulandibagh** (Patna) |
| **Didarganj Yakshi** | Found at Didarganj, Patna (1917) | Life-size female figure (chauri bearer) in **polished sandstone** with the famous **Mauryan polish**; now in the **Bihar Museum**, Patna |
| **Lauriya Nandangarh** | West Champaran | Ashokan **pillar with a lion capital**, still in place; pillar edicts inscribed |
| **Lauriya Araraj** | East Champaran | Ashokan pillar with edicts (capital lost) |
| **Rampurva** | West Champaran | Two pillars; the **bull capital** is now at **Rashtrapati Bhavan**, the lion capital in the Indian Museum, Kolkata |
| **Kolhua (Vaishali)** | Vaishali | Ashokan pillar with a **single lion** facing north; no edict inscribed; stupa and tank nearby |
| **Barabar caves** | Jehanabad (Makhdumpur area) | India's **oldest surviving rock-cut caves**; Ashoka gifted them to the **Ajivikas**; **Lomas Rishi cave** has a carved doorway copying wooden architecture; nearby **Nagarjuni caves** were gifted by Ashoka's grandson **Dasharatha** |
| **Sasaram** | Rohtas | Ashoka's **Minor Rock Edict** |

**Ashoka** (c. 268–232 BCE) held the **Third Buddhist Council** at Pataliputra (c. 250 BCE) under **Moggaliputta Tissa**. After it, missionaries went to Sri Lanka (his son Mahinda and daughter Sanghamitra), Suvarnabhumi and other lands.

> **Bihar connection:** India's national emblem comes from Ashoka's Sarnath lion capital (in UP), but **Bihar has the most Ashokan pillars still standing in a group** — Lauriya Nandangarh, Lauriya Araraj, Rampurva and Kolhua. The **Barabar caves** inspired E. M. Forster's "Marabar Caves" in *A Passage to India*.

## Post-Mauryan Bihar

- **Shungas**: **Pushyamitra Shunga** killed the last Maurya, **Brihadratha**, around 185 BCE and ruled from Pataliputra. The grammarian **Patanjali** (author of the *Mahabhashya*) is linked to his time.
- **Kanvas** followed the Shungas.
- **Kharavela** of Kalinga (Odisha) claims in his **Hathigumpha inscription** to have attacked Magadha and brought back a Jain image taken by a Nanda king.
- Buddhist legend says **Kanishka** (Kushana) took the poet **Ashvaghosha** from Pataliputra to his court.

## The Gupta age — Bihar's "golden age" connection

The Guptas rose in the Magadha–eastern UP region. Full story: [Post-Mauryan and Gupta Age](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/post-mauryan-and-gupta-age).

| Ruler | Bihar facts |
|---|---|
| **Sri Gupta, Ghatotkacha** | Early rulers, small kingdom |
| **Chandragupta I** (c. 319–335 CE) | Started the **Gupta era (319–320 CE)**; married the **Lichchhavi princess Kumaradevi** — the "king-queen" gold coins show both; the Lichchhavi link raised his status |
| **Samudragupta** | Proudly called himself "**Lichchhavi-dauhitra**" (grandson through daughter of the Lichchhavis); his conquests are in the **Allahabad (Prayag) pillar** inscription by **Harishena** |
| **Chandragupta II** | Chinese pilgrim **Fa-Hien (Faxian)** visited Pataliputra and praised its free hospitals and Ashoka's palace, which he thought was built by spirits |
| **Kumaragupta I** (c. 415–455 CE) | Founded **Nalanda Mahavihara** (5th century CE) |

**Aryabhata** (born 476 CE) wrote the *Aryabhatiya* (499 CE) at **Kusumapura** (Pataliputra). He said the earth rotates on its axis, explained eclipses scientifically and gave a value of π ≈ 3.1416. **Taregna** near Masaurhi (Patna) is traditionally linked with his observatory; it was a viewing point for the total solar eclipse of 22 July 2009. India's first satellite (1975) was named after him, and **Aryabhatta Knowledge University** (Patna, 2008) carries his name.

## Buddha, Mahavira and Bihar

Bihar is the land of both great "shramana" (ascetic) religions. Basics are in [Mahajanapadas, Buddhism and Jainism](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/mahajanapadas-buddhism-and-jainism).

### Buddhist sites in Bihar

| Place | District | Link with the Buddha / Buddhism |
|---|---|---|
| **Bodh Gaya** (ancient **Uruvela**) | Gaya | **Enlightenment** under the **pipal (Bodhi) tree** on the bank of the **Niranjana (Phalgu)**; **Mahabodhi Temple** — **UNESCO World Heritage Site (2002)** |
| **Rajgir** (Rajagriha) | Nalanda | **Griddhakuta (Vulture's Peak)** where he preached; **Venuvana** bamboo grove gifted by Bimbisara; **Saptaparni cave** — First Council; Vishwa Shanti Stupa (modern) |
| **Vaishali** | Vaishali | Frequent visits; Amrapali's gift; announced his coming death (**mahaparinirvana**) here; women first admitted to the Sangha here (Mahaprajapati Gautami, as per tradition); **Second Council** |
| **Pataligrama** | Patna | The Buddha predicted that this village would become a great city |
| **Kesariya** | East Champaran | Huge brick **stupa**, among the tallest excavated stupas in the world; marks where he gave his alms-bowl to the Lichchhavis (tradition) |
| **Nalanda** | Nalanda | Birthplace of disciple **Sariputta**; later the great university |
| **Gurpa / Kukkutapada hill** | Gaya | Linked with Mahakassapa |

The four main sites of the Buddha's life: birth — **Lumbini** (Nepal); enlightenment — **Bodh Gaya** (Bihar); first sermon — **Sarnath** (UP); death — **Kushinagar** (UP).

### Jain sites in Bihar

- **Mahavira** (24th Tirthankara), traditionally **599–527 BCE** (some scholars: c. 540–468 BCE), was born at **Kundagrama** near **Vaishali**. Father **Siddhartha** (head of the **Jnatrika** clan), mother **Trishala**, a Lichchhavi princess, sister of the Lichchhavi chief **Chetaka**.
- He gained **kaivalya** (supreme knowledge) near **Jrimbhikagrama** on the **Rijupalika** river (exact place debated).
- He attained **nirvana** at **Pawapuri** (Nalanda district); the **Jal Mandir** stands in a lotus tank there.
- The **First Jain Council** was held at **Pataliputra** (c. 300 BCE) under **Sthulabhadra**; the canon was arranged into **12 Angas**. During a famine, **Bhadrabahu** went south with Chandragupta Maurya — this is linked to the later **Digambara–Svetambara** split.
- The **Ajivika** sect of **Makkhali Gosala** was strong in Magadha; the Barabar caves were made for them.

> **Bihar connection:** **Sammed Shikhar (Parasnath)**, where 20 Tirthankaras attained nirvana, is now in **Jharkhand** (Giridih). It was in Bihar before 2000. Check the question's wording: "present-day Bihar" or "undivided Bihar".

## The great Mahaviharas (universities)

A **mahavihara** is a large Buddhist monastery that also worked as a residential university.

| University | Place | Founder (as usually given) | Key facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nalanda** | Bargaon, Nalanda district | **Kumaragupta I** (5th century CE) | Patronised by Harsha and the Palas; **Hiuen Tsang (Xuanzang)** studied here (7th c) under **Shilabhadra**; **I-tsing (Yijing)** came later in the 7th century; library "**Dharmaganja**" with three buildings — **Ratnasagara, Ratnodadhi, Ratnaranjaka**; destroyed by **Bakhtiyar Khilji** (c. **1193**; some give c. 1200) |
| **Vikramshila** | **Antichak**, Bhagalpur | **Dharmapala** (Pala, late 8th–early 9th c) | Centre of **Vajrayana (Tantric) Buddhism**; head **Atisha Dipankara Srijnana** went to Tibet (c. 1042) and reformed Tibetan Buddhism; destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khilji (c. 1203) |
| **Odantapuri** | **Bihar Sharif**, Nalanda | **Gopala** (some sources: Dharmapala) | Model for Tibet's first monastery **Samye**; Bakhtiyar Khilji's army took it, thinking it a fort |

More on Nalanda:

- Hiuen Tsang wrote that admission was by a tough oral test at the gate by a **dvarapandita** (gate scholar); most applicants failed. He reported thousands of monks and teachers (figures like **10,000 students and 1,500 teachers** are commonly quoted).
- Subjects: Buddhist philosophy (Mahayana and others), Vedas, logic (hetuvidya), grammar, medicine, astronomy.
- **Devapala** (Pala) gave five villages to a monastery built at Nalanda by **Balaputradeva**, the Shailendra king of **Sumatra–Java** — recorded in the **Nalanda copper plate**. This shows Bihar's links with Southeast Asia.
- "**Archaeological Site of Nalanda Mahavihara**" became a **UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016**.
- A new **Nalanda University** was set up under the **Nalanda University Act, 2010** (Parliament) at **Rajgir**, with support from East Asia Summit countries; its new campus was inaugurated on **19 June 2024**.

> **Mains angle:** For "Bihar as a centre of learning", use: Nalanda (international students from China, Korea, Tibet, Sumatra), Vikramshila (Atisha — Bihar's link to Tibet), Odantapuri (model for Samye), Aryabhata at Kusumapura, Chanakya's *Arthashastra*, and the 2010 revival of Nalanda.

## The Pala age in Bihar (c. 750–1160s CE)

After a period of chaos called **matsya-nyaya** ("law of the fish" — big fish eat small fish), the nobles of Bengal **elected Gopala** as king around 750 CE. The Palas ruled Bengal and Bihar for about four centuries.

| Ruler | Bihar facts |
|---|---|
| **Gopala** | Elected founder; Odantapuri linked to him |
| **Dharmapala** (c. 770–810) | Founded **Vikramshila**; fought the **tripartite struggle** for Kannauj with the Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas |
| **Devapala** | Nalanda copper plate; patron of Buddhism |
| **Mahipala I** | Revived the dynasty; Bihar inscriptions |
| **Ramapala** | Subject of *Ramacharita* by **Sandhyakar Nandi** |

**Pala art** is a major culture topic: smooth **black basalt** sculptures of Buddha, Tara and Hindu gods, bronze images from **Kurkihar** (Gaya) and Nalanda, and painted **palm-leaf manuscripts**. Pala art influenced Nepal, Tibet, Myanmar and Java.

Other early medieval powers in Bihar: the **Later Guptas of Magadha**; **Shashanka of Gauda** (Bengal), who according to Hiuen Tsang cut down the Bodhi tree; **Harsha**, a patron of Nalanda; and from 1097 CE the **Karnat dynasty** of Mithila (see [Medieval Bihar](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/medieval-bihar)).

### Mains questions

**1. "Geography and leadership together made Magadha the first empire of India." Discuss. (38 marks, about 300 words)**

- **Intro:** In the 6th century BCE, 16 Mahajanapadas competed; within two centuries Magadha ruled almost all of north India.
- **Body:**
  - Iron ores to the south — better weapons and farm tools.
  - Fertile, rain-fed alluvial soil — surplus rice, large revenue, big armies.
  - Rivers Ganga, Son, Gandak — trade routes and natural defence; Pataliputra as a water fort; Rajgir's five hills.
  - Forests — war elephants.
  - Less orthodox society — open to new ideas and people.
  - Rulers: Bimbisara's marriage diplomacy and annexation of Anga; Ajatashatru's new weapons against Vajji; Shishunaga's defeat of Avanti; Mahapadma Nanda's huge army; Chandragupta and Chanakya's organisation.
- **Conclusion:** Geography gave the resources; a line of able rulers turned them into power — the base on which the Mauryan empire was built.

**2. Assess the contribution of ancient Bihar to Indian knowledge and culture. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Bihar was the intellectual heart of ancient India.
- **Body:** Upanishadic debates at Janaka's court; birth of Buddhism and Jainism (Bodh Gaya, Vaishali, Pawapuri); Buddhist and Jain councils; Vaishali's republican tradition; Kautilya's *Arthashastra* and Megasthenes' account of Pataliputra's administration; Aryabhata's astronomy; Nalanda, Vikramshila, Odantapuri and their foreign students; Mauryan art (polish, pillars, Barabar caves) and Pala art spreading to Tibet and Southeast Asia.
- **Conclusion:** Link to today — revival of Nalanda University (2010), Buddhist circuit tourism, and Bihar's soft power in Asia.

**3. Describe the role of Nalanda and Vikramshila in spreading Indian culture to Asia. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Two Mahaviharas that made Bihar a centre of Asian Buddhism.
- **Body:** Founders and patrons (Kumaragupta I, Harsha, Palas); curriculum; Chinese visitors (Hiuen Tsang, I-tsing) and their records; Balaputradeva's monastery (Sumatra link); Atisha and the Tibetan revival; Vajrayana from Vikramshila; Pala art carried abroad; destruction by Bakhtiyar Khilji.
- **Conclusion:** Their legacy survives in Tibetan, Chinese and Southeast Asian Buddhism; UNESCO status and the new university keep it alive.
