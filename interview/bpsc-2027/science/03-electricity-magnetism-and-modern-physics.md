This chapter covers the electricity you use every day (circuits, fuses, the electricity bill), magnetism and electromagnetism (motors, generators, transformers), and "modern physics" — the atom, radioactivity, nuclear energy, X-rays, lasers and semiconductors. BPSC asks units, inventors, devices and their principles, and simple Ohm's-law calculations. Modern physics also supports the S&T chapters later (nuclear power, electronics).

> **In simple words:** Electric current is the flow of charge, pushed by voltage and slowed by resistance (V = IR). A current makes a magnet, and a moving magnet makes a current — this two-way link runs every motor and power station. Inside the atom, a heavy nucleus can split or join and release huge energy.

## Electric charge and static electricity

- There are two kinds of charge: **positive** and **negative**. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
- The **electron** carries a negative charge; the **proton** carries an equal positive charge. Charge of an electron = **1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulomb**. SI unit of charge: **coulomb (C)**.
- Rubbing a plastic comb on dry hair transfers electrons; the comb can then pick up bits of paper (**static electricity**).
- **Lightning** is a huge static discharge between clouds and the ground. **Benjamin Franklin** showed with his kite experiment (1752) that lightning is electrical and invented the **lightning conductor** (a pointed copper rod connected to the earth).
- A car or aeroplane is a safe place during lightning because charge stays on the outer metal body (a **Faraday cage**).
- Petrol tankers have a chain touching the ground to discharge static charge.

## Electric current, voltage and resistance

| Quantity | Meaning | SI unit | Measured by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current (I) | Rate of flow of charge (I = Q/t) | ampere (A) | **Ammeter** — connected in **series** |
| Potential difference / voltage (V) | Work done to move unit charge; the "push" | volt (V) | **Voltmeter** — connected in **parallel** |
| Resistance (R) | Opposition to current | ohm (Ω) | Ohmmeter / multimeter |
| Electromotive force (emf) | Voltage provided by a cell/battery | volt | — |

By convention, current flows from the positive to the negative terminal (**conventional current**); electrons actually move the other way.

**Conductors** allow current easily: silver (best), copper, aluminium, graphite (a non-metal conductor), the human body, salty water. **Insulators** do not: rubber, plastic, dry wood, glass, mica, porcelain. **Semiconductors** (silicon, germanium) are in between.

### Ohm's law

**At constant temperature, the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it: V = I × R.** Given by **Georg Simon Ohm** (1827).

### What resistance depends on

R = ρ × L ÷ A, where ρ (rho) is **resistivity** (a property of the material), L is length and A is cross-section area.
- **Longer wire → more resistance**; **thicker wire → less resistance**.
- Resistance of metals **increases with temperature**; that of semiconductors **decreases**.
- **Nichrome** (nickel + chromium alloy) has high resistivity and a high melting point — used in **heater coils, irons, toasters**.
- **Tungsten** (melting point about 3,400 °C) is used for **bulb filaments**. Bulbs are filled with inert gases (argon/nitrogen) to stop the filament burning.
- **Copper and aluminium** (low resistivity) are used for **transmission and house wiring**.
- **Superconductors** have **zero resistance** below a very low critical temperature (discovered by **Kamerlingh Onnes**, 1911, in mercury). Used in MRI machines and maglev trains.

### Worked example 1 — Ohm's law

A bulb in Muzaffarpur is connected to a 220 V supply and a current of 0.5 A flows. Find its resistance.
- R = V ÷ I = 220 ÷ 0.5 = **440 Ω**

| Feature | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Total resistance | R = R₁ + R₂ + … (increases) | 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … (decreases) |
| Current | **Same** through each part | Divides among branches |
| Voltage | Divides | **Same** across each branch |
| If one bulb fuses | **All go off** (old decorative serial lights) | Others keep working |
| Use | Fuse in series with appliance; old festival lights | **All household wiring** |

> **Prelims angle:** "Household appliances are connected in —" **parallel**, so that each gets 220 V and can be switched on or off independently. "A fuse is connected in —" **series** with the live wire.

### Worked example 2 — series and parallel

Two resistors of 6 Ω and 3 Ω are connected to a 12 V battery.
- **Series:** R = 6 + 3 = 9 Ω; I = 12 ÷ 9 ≈ **1.33 A**
- **Parallel:** 1/R = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 → R = **2 Ω**; I = 12 ÷ 2 = **6 A**

## Electric power, energy and your electricity bill

- **Power P = V × I = I²R = V²/R**. Unit: **watt**.
- **Heating effect (Joule's law)**: heat produced H = I²Rt. Used in heaters, geysers, electric irons, bulbs and **fuses**.
- **Electrical energy = power × time**. The commercial unit is the **kilowatt-hour (kWh)** — **"1 unit"** on your bill. 1 kWh = 1,000 W × 3,600 s = **3.6 × 10⁶ J**.

### Worked example 3 — electricity bill

A house in Bhagalpur uses: 4 LED bulbs of 10 W for 6 h a day, 2 fans of 60 W for 10 h a day and one 1,000 W iron for 30 minutes a day. Find units used in 30 days and the cost at ₹7 per unit.
- Bulbs: 4 × 10 × 6 = 240 Wh/day
- Fans: 2 × 60 × 10 = 1,200 Wh/day
- Iron: 1,000 × 0.5 = 500 Wh/day
- Total = 1,940 Wh/day = 1.94 kWh/day
- 30 days → 1.94 × 30 = **58.2 units**
- Cost = 58.2 × 7 ≈ **₹407**

(Real tariffs differ by slab and subsidy — this is only for practice.)

### Worked example 4 — current drawn and fuse rating

A 2,200 W geyser runs on 220 V. What current does it draw, and which fuse should be used?
- I = P ÷ V = 2,200 ÷ 220 = **10 A** → use a fuse/MCB rated slightly above, e.g. **15 A** (power circuit).

### LED vs CFL vs filament bulb

**LEDs** (light-emitting diodes) convert most of the electricity into light and use roughly a tenth of the energy of a filament bulb for the same light. India's **UJALA scheme (2015)** distributed crores of LED bulbs at low prices, cutting household electricity use. CFLs contain a little **mercury**, so they must be disposed of carefully.

## Household electricity and safety

- In India, household supply is **alternating current (AC)** at **220–230 V and 50 Hz** (the USA uses about 110–120 V, 60 Hz).
- **AC** changes direction periodically; **DC** (from cells, batteries, solar panels) flows in one direction. AC is used for transmission because its voltage can be easily raised or lowered by transformers. **Nikola Tesla** promoted AC; **Thomas Edison** promoted DC (the "war of currents").
- House wiring has three wires: **live (phase)** — red/brown insulation; **neutral** — black/blue; **earth** — green (or green-yellow).
- **Fuse**: a thin wire of **low melting point** alloy (tin–lead) in series with the live wire; it melts when too much current flows (overloading or short circuit). Modern houses use **MCBs (miniature circuit breakers)**, which trip and can be reset.
- **Earthing**: the metal body of appliances (fridge, iron, washing machine) is connected to the earth wire so that a leaking current goes to the ground instead of through a person.
- **Short circuit**: live and neutral wires touch directly; resistance becomes almost zero, current becomes huge, causing sparks and fire.
- **Overloading**: too many appliances on one socket.
- Birds can sit on a single high-voltage wire safely because there is **no potential difference** across their body (they do not touch a second wire or the ground).
- Electric shock is more dangerous with **wet hands** (wet skin has lower resistance).

## Magnetism

- A **magnet** has two poles — **north and south**. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract. Poles always come in pairs; breaking a magnet gives two smaller magnets.
- A freely suspended magnet points **north–south**. This is the principle of the **magnetic compass** (used by Chinese navigators early on).
- The Earth behaves like a giant magnet. The Earth's **geographic north pole is near its magnetic south pole** (that is why the compass's north end points there). The angle between geographic and magnetic north is **declination**; the angle a freely hung needle makes with the horizontal is **dip** (90° at magnetic poles, 0° at the magnetic equator).
- Magnetic materials: **iron, nickel, cobalt** (ferromagnetic). Soft iron is used for temporary electromagnets; **steel** and alloys like **alnico** and **neodymium** magnets are permanent magnets.
- Magnetism is destroyed by **heating** (above the **Curie temperature**), hammering or dropping.
- **Aurora borealis/australis** (northern and southern lights) occur when charged particles from the Sun are guided by Earth's magnetic field to the poles.

## Electromagnetism

### Magnetic effect of current

**Hans Christian Oersted (1820)** found that a current-carrying wire deflects a compass needle — **electricity produces magnetism**. A coil of wire carrying current (a **solenoid**) with a soft iron core becomes an **electromagnet**, which can be switched on and off.

Uses of electromagnets: **electric bell**, cranes lifting scrap iron, relays, loudspeakers, **MRI scanners**, **maglev trains**, separating iron from waste.

**Fleming's left-hand rule** gives the direction of force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field (used for **motors**). **Fleming's right-hand rule** gives the direction of induced current (used for **generators**).

### Electromagnetic induction

**Michael Faraday (1831)** discovered that a **changing magnetic field produces an electric current** in a coil. This is **electromagnetic induction** — the basis of generators, transformers and induction cooktops. **Lenz's law** gives the direction of the induced current.

### Motors, generators and transformers

| Device | Principle | Energy change | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Electric motor** | Force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field | Electrical → mechanical | Fans, pumps, mixers, washing machines, electric vehicles, trains |
| **Generator / dynamo** | Electromagnetic induction | Mechanical → electrical | Power stations, bicycle dynamo, diesel gensets |
| **Transformer** | Mutual induction; **works only on AC** | Changes AC voltage (step-up or step-down) | Power transmission, phone chargers, TV |
| Galvanometer | Coil in a magnetic field | Detects small currents | Converted into ammeter (low shunt) or voltmeter (high series resistance) |
| Induction cooktop | Eddy currents induced in a steel/iron pan | Electrical → heat | Kitchens (needs iron-based utensils) |

**Why transmit at high voltage?** Power loss in wires = I²R. Raising the voltage (with **step-up transformers** at power stations, e.g. to 220 kV or 400 kV) lowers the current, so less energy is wasted as heat. **Step-down transformers** at substations bring it down to 11 kV and then 230 V for homes.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar's big thermal power stations — **NTPC Barh, Kahalgaon, Nabinagar** and **Kanti (Muzaffarpur)** — all use the same principle: coal heats water → steam turns a turbine → turbine turns a generator (electromagnetic induction). Bihar now has near-universal household electrification under **"Har Ghar Bijli"** (one of the Saat Nischay resolves). Transmission and distribution losses are a recurring theme in the state's power-sector discussion.

## Electronics and semiconductors

- **Semiconductors** like **silicon** and **germanium** have conductivity between conductors and insulators. Adding tiny impurities (**doping**) creates **n-type** (extra electrons; doped with phosphorus/arsenic) or **p-type** (extra "holes"; doped with boron/gallium) semiconductors.
- **p–n junction diode**: allows current in one direction only — used as a **rectifier** (AC → DC) in chargers.
- **LED** (light-emitting diode): a diode that emits light. The **blue LED** won the **Nobel Prize in Physics 2014** (Akasaki, Amano, Nakamura) and made white LEDs possible.
- **Transistor** (invented 1947 at Bell Labs by **Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley**): acts as an amplifier and a switch — the building block of all computer chips.
- **Integrated circuit (IC)**: many transistors on one silicon chip (Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce). A modern processor holds billions of transistors.
- **Solar cells** are large p–n junctions.

The Indian Semiconductor Mission and chip fabs are covered in [ICT, AI and Biotechnology](/interview/bpsc-2027/science/ict-ai-and-biotechnology).

### Radioactivity

**Radioactivity** is the spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable nuclei. Discovered by **Henri Becquerel (1896)** in uranium salts; **Marie and Pierre Curie** discovered **polonium** and **radium**. Marie Curie is the **only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences** (Physics 1903, Chemistry 1911).

| Radiation | Nature | Charge | Penetrating power | Stopped by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Alpha (α)** | Helium nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons) | +2 | **Least** | A sheet of paper / skin |
| **Beta (β)** | Fast electrons | −1 | Medium | A few mm of aluminium |
| **Gamma (γ)** | Electromagnetic wave (photons) | None | **Most** | Thick lead or concrete |

Ionising power is the reverse — alpha is the most ionising.

**Half-life**: time in which half of a radioactive sample decays. It cannot be changed by temperature or pressure.

### Worked example 5 — half-life

A sample has 80 g of a radioactive isotope with a half-life of 5 years. How much remains after 15 years?
- 15 ÷ 5 = 3 half-lives → 80 → 40 → 20 → **10 g**

### Uses of radioisotopes

| Isotope | Use |
|---|---|
| **Carbon-14** | **Radiocarbon dating** of fossils, wood, archaeological remains (half-life ≈ 5,730 years); developed by **Willard Libby** |
| **Uranium-238 / potassium-argon** | Dating rocks and very old fossils |
| **Cobalt-60** | Cancer radiotherapy; sterilising medical equipment and food irradiation |
| **Iodine-131** | Diagnosis and treatment of **thyroid** disorders |
| **Technetium-99m** | Medical imaging (most used medical isotope) |
| Sodium-24 | Detecting blood clots and leaks in pipes |
| Phosphorus-32 | Study of plant nutrition; some blood disorders |
| Uranium-235, Plutonium-239 | Nuclear reactor fuel and nuclear weapons |

Radiation dose is measured in **sievert (Sv)** (biological effect) and **gray (Gy)** (absorbed energy). A **Geiger–Müller counter** detects radiation.

### Nuclear fission and fusion

| Feature | Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | A **heavy nucleus splits** (e.g. U-235 hit by a neutron → barium + krypton + 2–3 neutrons + energy) | **Light nuclei join** (hydrogen isotopes → helium + energy) |
| Discovered | **Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann (1938)** | — |
| Where | **Nuclear reactors**, atom bomb | **Sun and stars**, hydrogen bomb |
| Conditions | Critical mass, controlled chain reaction | Extremely high temperature (millions of °C) |
| Waste | Long-lived radioactive waste | Very little |
| Status | Used commercially | Experimental (e.g. **ITER** in France — India is a member) |

Energy comes from the conversion of a little mass into energy by **Einstein's equation E = mc²**.

### Parts of a nuclear reactor

| Part | Material | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Fuel** | Uranium-235 (natural or enriched uranium), plutonium-239 | Undergoes fission |
| **Moderator** | **Heavy water (D₂O)**, graphite, ordinary water | **Slows down neutrons** so they cause more fission |
| **Control rods** | **Cadmium or boron** | **Absorb extra neutrons** to control the chain reaction |
| **Coolant** | Water, heavy water, liquid sodium, CO₂ | Carries heat to make steam |
| Shielding | Thick concrete and lead | Stops radiation escaping |

> **Memory trick:** "**M**oderator **S**lows, **C**ontrol rods **C**atch" — moderator slows neutrons, control rods (cadmium) catch them.

India's three-stage nuclear power programme (Homi Bhabha), thorium, and nuclear plants are covered in [Energy and Environment Technology](/interview/bpsc-2027/science/energy-and-environment-technology).

### Other modern physics topics

- **X-rays**: discovered by **Wilhelm Röntgen (1895)**; pass through soft tissue but are stopped by bones. **CT scan** uses X-rays from many angles.
- **MRI** (magnetic resonance imaging) uses strong magnets and radio waves — **no ionising radiation**.
- **Photoelectric effect**: light falling on a metal ejects electrons; explained by **Albert Einstein (1905)** using photons — his **Nobel Prize (1921)** was for this, not for relativity. Used in **solar cells**, automatic doors and light meters.
- **Theory of relativity**: Einstein's special (1905) and general (1915) relativity. General relativity predicted **gravitational waves**, first detected by **LIGO in 2015** (Nobel 2017). India is building **LIGO-India** at **Hingoli, Maharashtra** (status — check latest).
- **LASER** = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Laser light is **monochromatic** (one colour), **coherent** and highly **directional**. Uses: eye surgery (LASIK), barcode readers, optical fibre communication, cutting metals, laser printers, distance measurement, LiDAR.
- **Black hole**: region where gravity is so strong that not even light escapes. **S. Chandrasekhar** gave the **Chandrasekhar limit** (≈ 1.4 times the Sun's mass) above which a dying star cannot remain a white dwarf.
- **Quantum technology**: computers and communication based on quantum effects — see the National Quantum Mission in [ICT, AI and Biotechnology](/interview/bpsc-2027/science/ict-ai-and-biotechnology).

| Fission / fusion | Splitting heavy nuclei / joining light nuclei |
| Moderator | Substance that slows neutrons in a reactor |
| Isotope | Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons |

### Mains questions

**1. "Universal electrification is only the first step; reliable and efficient power is the real goal." Discuss with reference to Bihar.** (about 300 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Bihar achieved near-universal household connections under Har Ghar Bijli (Saat Nischay) and Saubhagya; per-capita consumption is still among the lowest in India (check latest figures).
- **Science basis:** generation by electromagnetic induction; transmission at high voltage to cut I²R losses; transformers and substations.
- **Issues:** high aggregate technical and commercial (AT&C) losses, overloaded rural transformers, dependence on central thermal stations (Barh, Kahalgaon, Nabinagar, Kanti), little own hydro.
- **Technology solutions:** smart prepaid meters (Bihar has been an early large-scale adopter — check latest), feeder separation for agriculture, rooftop solar (PM Surya Ghar), solar pumps (PM-KUSUM), LED use (UJALA), underground cabling in cities.
- **Development impact:** irrigation, small industry, study hours for students, health centres, digital services.
- **Conclusion:** efficiency + renewables + metering reform are needed to turn connections into reliable, affordable power.

**2. Explain peaceful uses of radioactivity and nuclear technology in India, and the safety concerns involved.** (about 250 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** radioactivity (Becquerel, Curies) and fission (Hahn–Strassmann); India's programme under Bhabha.
- **Energy:** nuclear power reactors; low-carbon base-load power.
- **Medicine:** Co-60 and other radiotherapy, I-131 for thyroid, Tc-99m imaging; Tata Memorial and the cancer hospital network (Homi Bhabha Cancer Hospital & Research Centre at **Muzaffarpur** in Bihar).
- **Agriculture and food:** radiation-bred crop varieties by BARC, food irradiation to cut post-harvest losses (useful for litchi and mango).
- **Industry and research:** radiography of welds, tracers, carbon dating in archaeology (e.g. dating finds from excavated sites).
- **Safety:** AERB regulation, waste management, lessons from Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima (2011), public consent.
- **Conclusion:** strict regulation lets India gain from nuclear science while managing risks.
