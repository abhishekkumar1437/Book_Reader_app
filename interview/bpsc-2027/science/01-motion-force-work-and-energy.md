This chapter covers mechanics at NCERT class 6–10 level: units and measurement, motion, force and Newton's laws, gravitation, pressure and floating, and work, power and energy. BPSC Prelims usually asks General Science as **direct fact questions** ("SI unit of …", "Who gave …") and **"why does this happen?" questions** about daily life (why a passenger falls forward when a bus brakes, why a needle floats). Small numerical questions (speed, work, power) also appear. You need the idea, the formula and the unit — nothing more advanced.

> **In simple words:** Motion is change of position. A force is a push or a pull that changes motion. Work is done when a force moves something. Energy is the ability to do work, and it is never destroyed — it only changes form. If you understand these four sentences, every question in this chapter becomes easy.

## Physical quantities and SI units

A **physical quantity** is anything we can measure: length, mass, time, temperature. Each needs a **unit**. The world uses the **SI system** (Système International d'Unités), adopted in **1960** by the General Conference on Weights and Measures. Since **20 May 2019** (World Metrology Day) all seven base units are defined using fixed constants of nature (for example, the kilogram is now defined using the **Planck constant**, not a metal cylinder kept in Paris).

### Scalars and vectors

- **Scalar**: has only size (magnitude). Examples: mass, speed, distance, time, energy, work, temperature, pressure.
- **Vector**: has size **and direction**. Examples: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, weight.

> **Common mistake:** Pressure feels like it has a direction, but it is a **scalar**. Work is also a scalar, even though force and displacement are vectors.

## Motion: distance, displacement, speed, velocity, acceleration

- **Distance**: total path length travelled (scalar).
- **Displacement**: shortest straight-line distance from start to end, with direction (vector). If you walk round a circular park and come back to the gate, distance = the circumference, displacement = **zero**.
- **Speed** = distance ÷ time (m/s). **Velocity** = displacement ÷ time.
- **Acceleration** = change in velocity ÷ time (m/s²). If velocity decreases, the acceleration is negative, called **retardation** or **deceleration**.
- **Uniform circular motion**: speed is constant but direction changes all the time, so velocity changes and the body **is accelerating**. The force towards the centre is the **centripetal force**.

### Worked example 1 — average speed

A bus goes from Patna to Gaya (about 100 km) in 2 hours and returns in 3 hours by a jam-filled road. What is the average speed for the whole trip?

- Total distance = 100 + 100 = 200 km
- Total time = 2 + 3 = 5 h
- Average speed = 200 ÷ 5 = **40 km/h** (not the average of 50 and 33.3).
- Average velocity = total displacement ÷ time = 0 ÷ 5 = **0**, because the bus came back to Patna.

To convert km/h to m/s, multiply by **5/18**. So 72 km/h = 72 × 5/18 = **20 m/s**. For more speed problems, see [Time, Speed and Distance](/interview/quant-aptitude/time-speed-and-distance).

### Equations of motion (uniform acceleration)

<div class="formula">

| Equation | Use when |
|---|---|
| v = u + at | you know time |
| s = ut + ½at² | you need distance with time |
| v² = u² + 2as | time is not given |

</div>

Here u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = acceleration, t = time, s = displacement.

### Worked example 2 — braking

A car moving at 20 m/s brakes and stops in 4 s. Find the retardation and the distance covered while braking.

- a = (v − u) ÷ t = (0 − 20) ÷ 4 = **−5 m/s²** (retardation 5 m/s²)
- s = ut + ½at² = 20 × 4 + ½ × (−5) × 16 = 80 − 40 = **40 m**

### Free fall

Near the Earth, every object falls with the same acceleration **g ≈ 9.8 m/s²** if air resistance is ignored. **Galileo Galilei** showed that heavy and light bodies fall together (the famous, possibly legendary, Leaning Tower of Pisa experiment). In a vacuum, a feather and a coin fall together. In air, the feather is slower only because of air resistance.

## Force and Newton's laws of motion

A **force** is a push or pull. It can start motion, stop it, change its speed or direction, or change the shape of a body. Unit: **newton (N)**. **Isaac Newton** gave the three laws of motion in his book **Principia Mathematica (1687)**.

### First law — inertia

A body stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, **unless an external force acts on it**. This tendency is called **inertia**. **Mass is the measure of inertia** — a heavier body has more inertia.

Everyday examples (favourite Prelims questions):
- Passengers fall **forward** when a moving bus brakes suddenly (inertia of motion — the body wants to keep moving).
- Passengers fall **backward** when a stopped bus starts suddenly (inertia of rest).
- Dust comes out of a carpet when it is beaten (the carpet moves, the dust stays at rest).
- A coin on a card over a glass drops into the glass when the card is flicked.
- Leaves fall when a branch is shaken.
- Seat belts are worn to stop the forward motion of the body in a crash.

### Second law — F = ma

The rate of change of momentum is proportional to the force. In simple form: **Force = mass × acceleration (F = ma)**.

**Momentum (p) = mass × velocity**, unit kg·m/s. A loaded truck is harder to stop than a bicycle at the same speed because it has more momentum.

**Impulse** = force × time = change in momentum. If you increase the time of impact, the force becomes smaller. That is why:
- A cricketer **pulls his hands back** while catching a fast ball.
- Athletes doing high jump land on **foam or sand**.
- Cars have **crumple zones** and **airbags**.
- Glass items are packed in **straw, thermocol or paper**.

### Worked example 3 — F = ma

A force acts on a 1,000 kg car and accelerates it from rest to 10 m/s in 5 s. Find the force.
- a = (10 − 0) ÷ 5 = 2 m/s²
- F = ma = 1,000 × 2 = **2,000 N**

### Third law — action and reaction

**To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.** Action and reaction act on **different bodies**, so they do not cancel.
- A **rocket** moves up because hot gases are pushed out downward (rockets work in space too — they do not need air to push on).
- A **gun recoils** (moves back) when a bullet is fired.
- We **walk** by pushing the ground backward; the ground pushes us forward.
- A swimmer pushes water back to move forward.
- A boat moves back when you jump from it to the shore.

### Conservation of momentum

When no external force acts, the **total momentum of a system stays constant**. This explains gun recoil and rocket propulsion.

### Friction

**Friction** is the force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact. Static friction > sliding (kinetic) friction > rolling friction. That is why **wheels and ball bearings** make movement easier. Friction is needed for walking and for brakes; we reduce it with lubricants (oil, grease), ball bearings and streamlined shapes. Walking on ice is hard because friction is very low.

### Centripetal force and banking

Anything moving in a circle needs a force towards the centre (**centripetal force**). For the Moon it is gravity; for a stone on a string it is the tension in the string. **Centrifugal force** is the apparent outward force felt in the rotating frame. Uses: **cream separator**, washing-machine dryer, centrifuge in labs. Roads and railway tracks on curves are **banked** (outer edge raised) to provide centripetal force. A cyclist **leans inward** while turning for the same reason.

## Gravitation

**Newton's law of universal gravitation**: every two bodies attract each other with a force that is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

F = G × m₁ × m₂ ÷ r², where G = **6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²** (universal gravitational constant — the same everywhere). If you double the distance, the force becomes one-fourth.

### Mass and weight

| Mass | Weight |
|---|---|
| Amount of matter in a body | Force with which the Earth pulls the body (W = mg) |
| Scalar, unit kg | Vector, unit newton |
| Same everywhere — on Earth, Moon or in space | Changes with g |
| Measured with a beam balance | Measured with a spring balance |

### How g changes

- g is **greatest at the poles** and **least at the equator**, because the Earth bulges at the equator (larger radius) and because of Earth's rotation.
- g **decreases with height** above the surface and also **decreases with depth** below the surface. At the **centre of the Earth, g = 0**, so your weight there would be zero (mass stays the same).
- On the **Moon, g is about 1/6** of Earth's. A 60 kg person weighs about 600 N on Earth and about 100 N on the Moon. Their mass is still 60 kg.
- If the Earth stopped rotating, g at the equator would increase slightly (weight would increase); at the poles nothing would change.

### Weight in a lift

| Lift is … | You feel |
|---|---|
| At rest or moving at constant speed | Normal weight |
| Accelerating upward | Heavier |
| Accelerating downward | Lighter |
| Falling freely (cable cut) | **Weightless** (apparent weight zero) |

Astronauts in an orbiting spacecraft feel weightless because they and the spacecraft are **falling freely around the Earth together** — not because there is no gravity there.

### Escape velocity and satellites

- **Escape velocity** on Earth ≈ **11.2 km/s** — the minimum speed needed to leave Earth's gravity without further push. On the Moon it is only about 2.4 km/s, which is why the Moon **cannot hold an atmosphere**.
- **Orbital velocity** near the Earth's surface ≈ **7.9 km/s**. Orbital velocity does **not** depend on the satellite's mass.
- **Geostationary satellite**: orbits above the equator at about **35,786 km** height, period **24 hours**, moves west-to-east with the Earth, so it looks fixed over one place. Used for communication, TV and weather (e.g. INSAT/GSAT). **Arthur C. Clarke** first proposed the idea.
- **Polar / Sun-synchronous satellites**: low orbits (about 500–800 km) passing over the poles; used for remote sensing and mapping (e.g. ISRO's IRS, Cartosat, Oceansat series).

### Kepler's laws (planets)

1. Planets move in **elliptical orbits** with the Sun at one focus.
2. A line joining a planet and the Sun sweeps **equal areas in equal times** — so a planet moves **fastest when nearest to the Sun** (perihelion, around 3 January for Earth).
3. The square of a planet's period is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun (T² ∝ r³). Farther planets take longer to go round.

> **Prelims angle:** Questions like "Where is the weight of a body maximum?" (poles), "Weight at the centre of the Earth?" (zero), "Why do astronauts float?" (free fall), "Escape velocity on Earth?" (11.2 km/s), "Height of geostationary orbit?" (about 36,000 km) appear again and again.

## Pressure in solids, liquids and gases

**Pressure = force ÷ area** (unit pascal). The same force on a smaller area gives **more pressure**.
- A sharp knife cuts better than a blunt one (small area).
- Nails and needles have pointed tips.
- Wide straps on school bags, broad tyres of trucks and tractors, and wide foundations of buildings **reduce** pressure.
- Camels and snow shoes have wide feet so they do not sink.

### Pressure in liquids

Liquid pressure = **h × ρ × g** (depth × density × g). It increases with depth, so **dams are made thicker at the bottom**. Deep-sea divers wear special suits. Water spurts farther from a hole lower down in a tank.

**Pascal's law**: pressure applied to an enclosed liquid is transmitted **equally in all directions**. Applications: **hydraulic lift, hydraulic press, hydraulic brakes** of vehicles, hydraulic jack.

### Atmospheric pressure

Air has weight and presses on us with about **1.013 × 10⁵ Pa** (about 76 cm of mercury) at sea level. **Torricelli** invented the mercury **barometer** (1643). Pressure **decreases with height**, so:
- Nosebleeds can occur at high altitude.
- Fountain pens may leak in aeroplanes.
- Cooking is difficult on mountains (water boils below 100 °C — see the next chapter).
- A sudden fall in barometer reading indicates a **storm**; a steady rise indicates fair weather.

Drinking with a straw, filling an ink dropper and the working of a syringe all use atmospheric pressure.

### Bernoulli's principle

Where a fluid moves **faster**, its pressure is **lower**. Applications: **lift on an aeroplane wing**, roofs blown off in storms (fast wind above, normal pressure below), the **atomiser/spray gun**, a spinning ball swinging in cricket, and why you should not stand close to the edge of a platform when a fast train passes (low pressure pulls you towards the train).

## Floating, density and Archimedes' principle

**Density** = mass ÷ volume (kg/m³). Water: **1,000 kg/m³ (1 g/cm³)**. **Relative density** (specific gravity) = density of substance ÷ density of water (no unit). Milk purity is checked with a **lactometer**; the density of liquids is checked with a **hydrometer**.

**Archimedes' principle**: a body immersed in a fluid loses weight equal to the **weight of the fluid it displaces**. This upward force is called **buoyant force** or **upthrust**.

**Law of floatation**: a body floats if its weight equals the weight of liquid it displaces. Put simply — if the body's average density is less than the liquid's, it floats.

| Observation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Iron nail sinks, iron ship floats | The ship's hollow shape displaces a large volume of water |
| Swimming is easier in sea water | Sea water is denser, so upthrust is greater |
| Easier to float in the **Dead Sea** | Extremely salty, very dense water |
| Ice floats on water, ~9/10 of an iceberg is under water | Ice density ≈ 0.92 g/cm³ |
| A ship sinks a little deeper when it moves from sea into a river | River (fresh) water is less dense |
| A loaded ship has a **Plimsoll line** | Safe loading mark painted on the hull |
| Submarines dive and rise | They fill or empty ballast tanks with water |
| Balloon filled with hydrogen/helium rises | The gas is lighter than the air it displaces |

> **Memory trick:** "Eureka!" — Archimedes reportedly shouted it when he found out how to test whether King Hiero's crown was pure gold, while getting into his bath.

### Surface tension, capillarity and viscosity

- **Surface tension**: the surface of a liquid behaves like a stretched skin. That is why **raindrops are spherical**, a **needle or razor blade can float**, and insects such as water striders walk on water. **Soap and detergents reduce surface tension**, so they spread and clean better. Hot water has lower surface tension, which also helps washing. Oil poured on the sea calms waves by reducing surface tension.
- **Capillarity**: rise of a liquid in a thin tube. Examples: kerosene rising in a lamp wick, ink in blotting paper, water rising in plants (partly), a towel soaking water. **Mercury falls** in a glass capillary (it does not wet glass).
- **Viscosity**: internal friction of a fluid (its "thickness"). Honey and glycerine are highly viscous; viscosity of liquids **decreases with heat**, while that of gases **increases**.

## Work, power and energy

### Work

**Work = force × displacement in the direction of force (W = F × s)**. Unit: **joule (J)**. One joule is the work done when a force of 1 N moves a body by 1 m.

- If there is no displacement, **no work is done** (in the physics sense). A person pushing a wall, or a coolie standing still with a load on his head, does **zero work** even though he gets tired.
- If the force is perpendicular to the motion, work is zero. A coolie carrying a load and walking on a **level** road does no work against gravity. The Moon moving round the Earth: gravity does no work (it is at right angles to the motion).

### Worked example 4 — work

A farmer in Nalanda lifts a 50 kg sack of grain onto a cart 1.2 m high. How much work does he do? (g = 10 m/s²)
- Force needed = weight = mg = 50 × 10 = 500 N
- Work = F × s = 500 × 1.2 = **600 J**

### Power

**Power = work ÷ time (P = W ÷ t)**. Unit: **watt (W)**; 1 kW = 1,000 W; **1 hp ≈ 746 W**. Power tells how *fast* work is done.

### Worked example 5 — power

A motor pump lifts 600 kg of water to a tank 10 m high in 1 minute. Find its power (g = 10 m/s²).
- Work = mgh = 600 × 10 × 10 = 60,000 J
- Time = 60 s
- Power = 60,000 ÷ 60 = **1,000 W = 1 kW** (about 1.34 hp)

### Energy and its forms

**Energy** is the capacity to do work, measured in joules.

- **Kinetic energy (KE)** — energy of motion: **KE = ½mv²**. Doubling the speed makes KE **four times**. This is why speeding is so dangerous on highways.
- **Potential energy (PE)** — stored energy due to position or shape: **PE = mgh**. Water stored in a dam, a stretched bow, a wound watch spring, a stretched rubber band.
- Other forms: heat, light, sound, chemical (food, fuel, batteries), electrical, nuclear.

### Worked example 6 — kinetic energy

A 50 kg boy runs at 4 m/s. Find his KE. If he doubles his speed, what is the new KE?
- KE = ½ × 50 × 4² = ½ × 50 × 16 = **400 J**
- At 8 m/s: ½ × 50 × 64 = **1,600 J** (4 times)

### Law of conservation of energy

**Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it only changes from one form to another.** The total energy of an isolated system stays constant.

## Simple machines

A **machine** makes work easier by changing the size or direction of a force. **Mechanical advantage = load ÷ effort**.

**Levers** — three classes, decided by what is in the middle:

| Class | In the middle | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| First class | **Fulcrum** | See-saw, scissors, pliers, crowbar, beam balance, hand pump handle |
| Second class | **Load** | Nutcracker, wheelbarrow, bottle opener, lemon squeezer |
| Third class | **Effort** | Fire tongs, forceps, broom, fishing rod, **human forearm** (lifting a weight) |

> **Memory trick:** "**F-L-E: 1-2-3**" — Fulcrum, Load, Effort in the middle for classes 1, 2, 3.

Other simple machines: **pulley** (well rope — changes direction of force), **inclined plane** (ramp, winding mountain roads), **wedge** (axe, knife), **screw**, **wheel and axle** (steering wheel, doorknob).

### Mains questions

These topics are basic for Mains, but GS Paper II section 3 can ask how simple science supports development. Practise linking science to daily life and policy.

**1. "Road accidents are a physics problem as much as a traffic problem." Explain with reference to inertia, momentum and kinetic energy, and suggest safety measures for Bihar's highways.** (about 300 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** India records lakhs of road accidents a year; Bihar's expanding highway network (expressways, four-lane NHs) raises speeds. Physics explains why speed kills.
- **Inertia:** occupants keep moving when a vehicle stops — hence seat belts, head rests, child seats.
- **Kinetic energy ½mv²:** doubling speed quadruples energy to be absorbed in a crash; overloaded trucks (higher m) are more dangerous.
- **Momentum and impulse:** increasing collision time reduces force — airbags, crumple zones, crash barriers, helmets with foam.
- **Friction and stopping distance:** wet roads and worn tyres reduce friction; braking distance rises with v².
- **Centripetal force:** banking of curves, speed limits on bends, avoiding sharp turns on rural roads.
- **Measures:** speed cameras, helmet and seat-belt enforcement, road-safety education in schools, trauma centres on highways, black-spot correction.
- **Conclusion:** applying basic physics in road design and enforcement can save lives cheaply.

**2. Explain the principles of pressure and buoyancy and their applications in daily life and engineering.** (about 250 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** definition of pressure (F/A) and buoyancy.
- **Solids:** sharp tools, broad tyres for tractors, foundations.
- **Liquids:** pressure = hρg; dam design (relevant to barrages like the Kosi barrage), Pascal's law in hydraulic machines.
- **Air:** atmospheric pressure, barometer and weather forecasting, Bernoulli and aircraft lift.
- **Buoyancy:** ships, submarines, lactometer for milk quality (dairy co-operatives like COMFED/Sudha in Bihar), life jackets used in flood rescue.
- **Conclusion:** simple principles underpin large public works and safety.
