This is the chemistry chapter that gives the most marks per hour of study. BPSC loves "common name ↔ chemical name" pairs (baking soda, plaster of Paris, bleaching powder), "ore of which metal", "composition of brass/bronze", "pH of blood", "why is LPG given a smell", "which metal causes Minamata disease". Learn the tables well — then revise them again in Phase 4.

> **In simple words:** Acids taste sour and turn blue litmus red; bases taste bitter, feel soapy and turn red litmus blue. When they meet, they cancel each other and form salt and water. Metals are shiny, conduct electricity and are mostly taken out of rocks called ores. Many things in your kitchen and bathroom are simple chemicals with long names.

## Acids, bases and indicators

| Property | Acid | Base |
|---|---|---|
| Taste | Sour | Bitter |
| Touch | — | Soapy, slippery |
| Litmus | **Blue → red** | **Red → blue** |
| Phenolphthalein | Colourless | **Pink** |
| Methyl orange | Red | Yellow |
| Turmeric | Stays yellow | Turns **red-brown** |
| In water gives | H⁺ (H₃O⁺) ions | OH⁻ ions |
| With metals | Hydrogen gas (burns with a pop) | (some, like NaOH with Zn, also give H₂) |
| With carbonates | **CO₂** (fizz; turns lime water milky) | — |

- Bases that dissolve in water are called **alkalis** (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, NH₄OH). All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.
- **Litmus** is a natural dye from **lichens**. Other natural indicators: turmeric, red cabbage, hibiscus (China rose) petals. **Olfactory indicators** (onion, vanilla, clove oil) change smell.
- Strong acids: hydrochloric (HCl), sulphuric (H₂SO₄), nitric (HNO₃). Weak acids: acetic, citric, carbonic. Strong bases: NaOH, KOH. Weak base: ammonium hydroxide.
- **Always add acid to water, never water to acid** — the reaction is very exothermic and can splash.

## The pH scale

**pH** (introduced by **S. P. L. Sørensen**, 1909) measures how acidic or basic a solution is: **pH = −log[H⁺]**. Scale 0–14. **pH 7 = neutral**, below 7 = acidic, above 7 = basic. Each step is a 10-times change: pH 3 is **10 times** more acidic than pH 4 and **100 times** more acidic than pH 5.

| Substance | Approximate pH |
|---|---|
| Gastric juice | 1.5–2 (strongly acidic) |
| Lemon juice | ≈ 2.4 |
| Vinegar | ≈ 3 |
| Tomato juice | ≈ 4 |
| Coffee | ≈ 5 |
| Normal rain | ≈ 5.6 (CO₂ dissolves) |
| Urine | ≈ 6 (range 4.5–8) |
| Milk | ≈ 6.5 |
| Saliva | ≈ 6.5–7.5 |
| Pure water | **7** |
| **Human blood** | **7.35–7.45 (≈ 7.4)** |
| Sea water | ≈ 8 |
| Baking soda solution | ≈ 8.3 |
| Milk of magnesia | ≈ 10 |
| Lime water | ≈ 12 |
| Sodium hydroxide | ≈ 14 |

Applications:
- **Tooth decay** starts when the pH in the mouth falls **below about 5.5** (bacteria produce acids from sugar). Toothpastes are **basic** to neutralise them.
- **Acidity/indigestion**: antacids like **milk of magnesia (Mg(OH)₂)** and baking soda neutralise extra stomach acid.
- **Soil pH**: crops grow best near neutral (6–7.5). **Acidic soil is treated with lime** (quicklime, slaked lime or chalk); **alkaline (saline-sodic, "usar") soil is treated with gypsum**, organic matter or press mud.
- **Acid rain** (pH < 5.6) comes from **SO₂ and NOx** from coal power plants and vehicles; it damages crops, aquatic life and monuments — the **Taj Mahal's marble is yellowing** ("marble cancer") partly from such pollution (Mathura refinery debate).
- Insect stings: neutralise acidic stings with mild base (baking soda paste), alkaline wasp stings with mild acid (vinegar).

## Salts

A **salt** forms when an acid reacts with a base (neutralisation): **acid + base → salt + water**. Example: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O.

- **Water of crystallisation**: water molecules inside salt crystals, e.g. blue copper sulphate (CuSO₄·5H₂O) turns **white** when heated (loses water) and blue again when water is added.
- **Common salt (NaCl)** is the raw material for caustic soda, baking soda, washing soda and bleaching powder. Rock salt (**sendha namak**) is mined; sea salt is from evaporation (Gujarat is the top producer).
- **Iodised salt** prevents **goitre** and iodine deficiency disorders (potassium iodate is added).
- **Chlor-alkali process**: electrolysis of brine (salt water) → **NaOH** (at cathode side), **chlorine** (anode) and **hydrogen**.

### Worked example 1 — why does a cake rise?

On heating, baking soda decomposes: **2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂↑**. The CO₂ gas makes the dough puff up. Sodium carbonate tastes bitter, so **baking powder** adds a mild edible acid (tartaric acid) that reacts with baking soda to give CO₂ without the bitter taste.

## Hard water and soft water

- **Hard water** does not form lather easily with soap and forms **scum**. It contains **calcium and magnesium** salts.
- **Temporary hardness**: bicarbonates of Ca and Mg → removed by **boiling** or by adding lime (**Clark's method**).
- **Permanent hardness**: chlorides and sulphates of Ca and Mg → removed by **washing soda**, **ion-exchange (zeolite/permutit)** or **reverse osmosis (RO)**.
- Hard water causes **scaling in boilers and kettles**.
- **Detergents** work well even in hard water; **soaps** do not.

## Soaps and detergents

| Soap | Detergent |
|---|---|
| Sodium or potassium salts of **long-chain fatty acids** | Sodium salts of **sulphonic acids** (synthetic) |
| Made from oils/fats + NaOH by **saponification** (by-product **glycerol**) | Made from petrochemicals |
| Does not work well in **hard water** (forms scum) | **Works in hard water** |
| Biodegradable | Some are not biodegradable; phosphates cause **eutrophication** of water bodies |

**How soap cleans**: a soap molecule has a water-loving (**hydrophilic**) head and an oil-loving (**hydrophobic**) tail. The tails grab grease and form ball-like **micelles** that are washed away by water.

### Reactivity series (most → least reactive)

**K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au > Pt**

> **Memory trick:** "**P**lease **S**top **C**alling **M**e **A** **Z**ebra, **I** **L**ike **H**er **C**alling **M**e **S**weet **G**oat **P**lease" → Potassium, Sodium, Calcium, Magnesium, Aluminium, Zinc, Iron, Lead, Hydrogen, Copper, Mercury, Silver, Gold, Platinum.

- Metals **above hydrogen** displace hydrogen from acids. Copper, silver and gold do not — so they are used for coins and jewellery (**noble metals**).
- Gold, platinum are found **free (native)** in nature; reactive metals are found as compounds.

## Fuels

A good fuel has a **high calorific value** (energy released per kg), burns easily, is cheap, and produces little smoke.

| Fuel | Approx. calorific value (kJ/g) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cow-dung cake | 6–8 | Smoky; indoor pollution |
| Wood | 17–22 | — |
| Coal | 25–33 | Types by carbon content: peat < lignite < bituminous < **anthracite** (best) |
| Petrol | ~45 | — |
| Kerosene, diesel | ~45 | — |
| LPG | ~55 | Butane + propane |
| CNG / methane | ~50 | Cleaner vehicle fuel |
| Biogas | ~35–40 | Methane (~55–75%) + CO₂ |
| **Hydrogen** | **~150** | **Highest**; product is only water; hard to store |

- **LPG** is odourless; **ethyl mercaptan** (a sulphur compound) is added so that leaks can be smelled.
- **CNG** is mainly methane; it burns cleanly. Delhi's switch of buses and autos to CNG (Supreme Court order, around 2001–02) is a famous pollution policy.
- **Octane number** measures a petrol's resistance to **knocking** (premature burning in engines). **Cetane number** is the corresponding measure for diesel. Earlier, **tetraethyl lead** was added as an anti-knock agent; **leaded petrol was phased out in India by 2000** because lead is toxic.
- **Coal gas** (H₂ + CH₄ + CO), **water gas** (CO + H₂), **producer gas** (CO + N₂) are industrial fuels.
- **Ethanol blending**: India reached about **20% ethanol blending in petrol (E20)** in 2025 (check latest). Ethanol comes from sugarcane molasses, grains (maize, broken rice) — Bihar has new grain-based ethanol plants. Details in [Energy and Environment Technology](/interview/bpsc-2027/science/energy-and-environment-technology).
- **Fire triangle**: fuel + oxygen (air) + heat (ignition temperature). Remove any one to put out a fire. **Water should not be used on oil or electrical fires**; use CO₂, foam or dry-powder extinguishers (sand for small oil fires).

## Cement, glass and ceramics

- **Portland cement** (Joseph Aspdin, 1824) is made by heating **limestone + clay** and adding **gypsum** (to slow setting). Main constituents: calcium silicates and aluminates. **Concrete** = cement + sand + gravel + water; **reinforced cement concrete (RCC)** has steel bars. Cement plants are usually built near **limestone** deposits — in Bihar these are in **Rohtas and Kaimur** districts.
- **Glass** is a super-cooled liquid made by melting **sand (silica) + soda ash + limestone**. Coloured glass: **cobalt oxide → blue**, **chromium oxide → green**, ferrous → green, **gold chloride → ruby red**, manganese dioxide → purple. **Borosilicate (Pyrex)** glass resists heat; **lead (crystal) glass** sparkles; **safety glass** is layered; photochromic glass darkens in sunlight (silver halide).
- **Ceramics**: clay heated at high temperature (pottery, porcelain, insulators, tiles).

## Chemistry in agriculture

| Fertiliser | Nutrient | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Urea** CO(NH₂)₂ | Nitrogen (**46% N** — highest among solid N-fertilisers) | Most used in India; **neem-coated urea** (100% from 2015) reduces misuse and slows release |
| Ammonium sulphate | Nitrogen + sulphur | — |
| **DAP** (di-ammonium phosphate) | Nitrogen + **phosphorus** (18:46) | Second most used |
| **MOP** (muriate of potash) | **Potassium** (KCl) | India imports all its potash |
| Superphosphate | Phosphorus | — |
| NPK | All three | — |
| **Nano urea / nano DAP** | Liquid, sprayed on leaves | Developed by **IFFCO**; nano urea launched 2021 (effectiveness still debated — check latest) |
| Zinc sulphate | Zinc | Treats khaira disease of rice |
| Biofertilisers | *Rhizobium*, *Azotobacter*, **blue-green algae** (*Anabaena*, *Nostoc*), *Azolla* | Fix nitrogen naturally |

**Primary nutrients**: N, P, K. **Secondary**: Ca, Mg, S. **Micronutrients**: Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni. The ideal N:P:K use ratio usually cited for India is **4:2:1**; actual use is heavily tilted to nitrogen.

**Pesticides**: insecticides, fungicides (**Bordeaux mixture**), herbicides (weedicides), rodenticides (**zinc phosphide**). **DDT** (insecticide; still used in limited cases for disease-vector control, e.g. kala-azar control spraying was historically DDT and later shifted to synthetic pyrethroids). Organophosphates are toxic — the **2013 Saran (Dharmasati Gandaman) midday meal tragedy** in Bihar, where 23 children died, was caused by **monocrotophos**, an organophosphate pesticide, in cooking oil.

## Chemistry in medicine

| Drug type | Use | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Analgesic** | Pain relief | **Aspirin** (acetylsalicylic acid; also anti-clotting), paracetamol, ibuprofen; narcotic analgesics — morphine |
| **Antipyretic** | Reduces fever | **Paracetamol**, aspirin |
| **Antibiotic** | Kills or stops bacteria | **Penicillin** (discovered by **Alexander Fleming, 1928**, from the fungus *Penicillium notatum*), streptomycin (first drug for TB — Waksman), tetracycline, chloramphenicol (typhoid) |
| **Antiseptic** | Applied on living tissue | Dettol (chloroxylenol + terpineol), **tincture of iodine**, boric acid, hydrogen peroxide, Savlon; **Joseph Lister** pioneered antiseptic surgery |
| **Disinfectant** | Used on non-living things | Phenol (1%), bleaching powder, chlorine; 0.2% phenol is an antiseptic |
| **Antacid** | Neutralises excess stomach acid | Milk of magnesia, baking soda, aluminium hydroxide; ranitidine/omeprazole reduce acid secretion |
| **Antihistamine** | Allergy | Cetirizine |
| **Tranquilliser** | Anxiety, sleep | Diazepam (Valium), barbiturates |
| **Anaesthetic** | Removes sensation | **General**: chloroform, ether, **nitrous oxide**; **local**: lidocaine, procaine |
| Antimalarial | Malaria | **Quinine** (from cinchona bark), chloroquine, **artemisinin** (Tu Youyou, Nobel 2015) |
| Sulpha drugs | Early antibacterials | Prontosil |
| Oral rehydration solution (ORS) | Diarrhoea dehydration | Salt + sugar (glucose) + water (WHO formula) |

**Antibiotic resistance (AMR)** grows when antibiotics are misused (incomplete courses, use in poultry). India's **Red Line campaign** marks prescription-only antibiotics with a red vertical line.

| Chemical | Disease / effect |
|---|---|
| **Mercury** | **Minamata disease** (Japan, 1950s — mercury in fish) |

| **Arsenic** | **Arsenicosis**, skin lesions, "**black foot disease**", cancers — a major problem in groundwater of the **Ganga plains of Bihar, West Bengal and UP** |
| **Fluoride** (excess) | **Fluorosis** — mottled teeth, bone deformities (skeletal fluorosis) |
| **Nitrate** in drinking water | **Blue baby syndrome** (methaemoglobinaemia) |
| Asbestos | **Asbestosis**, lung cancer |

| Coal dust | Black lung (pneumoconiosis) |
| Cotton dust | Byssinosis |
| Sulphur dioxide | Acid rain, respiratory problems |
| Nitrogen oxides | Acid rain, smog, ozone formation |
| Carbon monoxide | Reduces oxygen-carrying capacity of blood |
| CFCs | Ozone depletion |
| PM2.5 / PM10 | Fine particles causing lung and heart disease (Patna, Muzaffarpur and Gaya often appear in pollution reports — check latest) |
| **Methyl isocyanate** | **Bhopal gas tragedy (1984)** |

> **Bihar connection:** **Arsenic contamination** of groundwater affects many districts along the Ganga (e.g. **Bhojpur, Buxar, Patna, Vaishali, Saran, Begusarai, Bhagalpur** — first reported widely around 2002 in **Semariya Ojha Patti, Bhojpur**). **Fluoride** is a problem in south Bihar districts such as **Gaya, Nawada, Aurangabad, Jamui, Banka, Munger**; **iron** excess in north-eastern districts. The **Har Ghar Nal Ka Jal** programme (Saat Nischay) and water treatment plants aim to supply safe piped water. This is a perfect Mains GS II S&T and Bihar example.

### Mains questions

**1. Groundwater contamination by arsenic and fluoride is a silent public-health crisis in Bihar. Discuss the causes and technological solutions.** (about 300 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** much of rural Bihar depends on hand pumps and tube wells; arsenic in Ganga-belt districts, fluoride in south Bihar, iron in the north-east.
- **Causes:** arsenic is geogenic (released from sediments under reducing conditions), worsened by heavy groundwater pumping; fluoride from rocks in the south Bihar plateau fringe.
- **Health impact:** arsenicosis, skin lesions, cancers; dental and skeletal fluorosis; economic loss and stigma.
- **Technological solutions:** testing kits and labs, community arsenic/fluoride removal plants, activated alumina and iron-oxide filters, deeper safe aquifers, surface water-based multi-village schemes (Ganga water), rainwater harvesting, Har Ghar Nal Ka Jal and Jal Jeevan Mission.
- **Research and monitoring:** role of IITs, Mahavir Cancer Sansthan (Patna) studies, CGWB mapping, GIS maps.
- **Community:** awareness, women's groups (Jeevika), panchayat water committees.
- **Conclusion:** safe water needs science + infrastructure + regular testing; it is central to SDG 6.

**2. Examine the problem of food adulteration in India. How can science and technology help ensure food safety?** (about 250 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** adulteration examples — milk, spices, oil, sweets during festivals; mass tragedies (e.g. argemone oil and epidemic dropsy).
- **Health effects:** lathyrism, dropsy, cancers from dyes, carbide ripening.
- **Legal framework:** FSS Act 2006, FSSAI standards, state food safety commissioners.
- **Technology:** mobile food testing labs ("Food Safety on Wheels"), rapid test kits, DART book (Detect Adulteration with Rapid Test) by FSSAI, blockchain traceability, sensors.
- **Bihar angle:** milk co-operatives (COMFED) quality testing; Saran midday-meal tragedy (2013) lesson for school food safety.
- **Conclusion:** testing capacity + awareness + strict penalties.
