This chapter builds the chemistry base: what matter is made of, how atoms are built, how the periodic table is arranged, how atoms join (bonds), and what kinds of chemical reactions happen around us — rusting, burning, curdling of milk, digestion. BPSC Prelims asks scientist–discovery pairs, "which separation method is used for…", properties of elements and simple reaction types. The next chapter covers acids, metals and everyday chemicals.

> **In simple words:** Everything is made of tiny atoms. Atoms have a small heavy centre (the nucleus, with protons and neutrons) and light electrons around it. Atoms join by sharing or giving away electrons. A chemical reaction is just atoms changing partners — nothing is lost, only rearranged.

## Matter and its states

**Matter** is anything that has mass and occupies space. Indian philosopher **Maharshi Kanad** (around 6th–2nd century BCE; dates vary) proposed that matter is made of tiny indivisible particles called **"parmanu"** — an early atomic idea. Modern atomic theory came from **John Dalton (1808)**.

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Fixed | Takes the container's shape | Fills the whole container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Not fixed |
| Inter-particle force | Strongest | Medium | Weakest |
| Compressibility | Almost none | Very little | **Highly compressible** (that is why LPG/CNG are stored in cylinders) |
| Diffusion | Very slow | Faster | **Fastest** (smell of perfume or incense spreads quickly) |

Other states: **Plasma** — hot ionised gas found in the **Sun, stars, lightning**, neon signs (the "fourth state", and the most common state in the visible universe). **Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC)** — formed at temperatures near absolute zero; predicted by **S. N. Bose and Einstein** in 1924–25, first made in 1995 (Nobel 2001).

### Changes of state

| Change | Name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Solid → liquid | Melting (fusion) | Ice melting |
| Liquid → solid | Freezing | Water to ice |
| Liquid → gas | Evaporation / boiling | Drying clothes, boiling water |
| Gas → liquid | Condensation | Dew; water drops on a cold glass |
| **Solid → gas directly** | **Sublimation** | **Camphor, naphthalene balls, iodine, dry ice (solid CO₂), ammonium chloride** |
| Gas → solid directly | Deposition | Frost formation |

> **Prelims angle:** "Which of these sublimes?" — camphor, naphthalene, iodine, ammonium chloride, dry ice. Common salt and sugar do **not** sublime.

### Physical and chemical changes

| Physical change | Chemical change |
|---|---|
| No new substance forms; usually reversible | **New substance forms**; usually irreversible |
| Melting ice, dissolving sugar, tearing paper, magnetising iron, glowing of a bulb filament | **Rusting** of iron, **burning** of paper/wood, **curdling of milk**, cooking food, **ripening of fruit**, digestion, photosynthesis, souring of milk, rotting of eggs |

## Elements, compounds and mixtures

- **Element**: pure substance made of only one kind of atom (118 known; about 94 occur naturally). Examples: hydrogen, carbon, iron, gold.
- **Compound**: two or more elements **chemically combined in a fixed ratio**; properties differ from the elements. Example: water (H₂O) — hydrogen burns, oxygen helps burning, but water puts out fire!
- **Mixture**: substances physically mixed in **any ratio**; each keeps its properties; can be separated by physical methods. Examples: air, sea water, soil, milk, brass, salt solution.

| Mixture type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Homogeneous (solution) | Same throughout | Salt water, sugar solution, air, **alloys like brass and bronze** |
| Heterogeneous | Not uniform | Sand in water, oil and water, soil |

### Solutions, colloids and suspensions

| Type | Particle size | Light scattering (Tyndall effect) | Settles? | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| True solution | Smallest (< 1 nm) | No | No | Salt or sugar in water |
| **Colloid** | 1–1,000 nm | **Yes** | No | **Milk, blood, fog, smoke, butter, jelly, ink, shaving cream** |
| Suspension | Largest | Yes | **Yes** | Muddy water, chalk in water |

The **Tyndall effect** — a light beam becomes visible when it passes through a colloid (sunlight entering a dark room through a gap; headlights in fog; sunbeams in a forest).

Colloid types: **emulsion** (liquid in liquid — milk, mayonnaise), **aerosol** (liquid or solid in gas — fog, mist, smoke), **foam** (gas in liquid — shaving cream, soap lather), **gel** (liquid in solid — jelly, cheese).

### Separation methods

| Method | Principle | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Filtration** | Particle size | Tea leaves from tea, sand from water |
| **Evaporation** | One component evaporates | Salt from sea water (salt pans) |
| **Distillation** | Different boiling points | Pure water from salty water; distilled water for batteries |
| **Fractional distillation** | Small differences in boiling points | **Crude oil (petroleum) refining**; separating gases of air (oxygen, nitrogen, argon) |
| **Crystallisation** | Solubility | Pure salt, sugar, alum, copper sulphate crystals |
| **Chromatography** | Different speeds of movement on paper/column | Separating colours in ink, dyes; detecting drugs in blood |
| **Centrifugation** | Spinning — denser part moves outward | **Cream from milk** (dairies), blood tests, washing-machine dryers |
| **Sublimation** | One component sublimes | Ammonium chloride or camphor from salt |
| **Separating funnel** | Immiscible liquids settle in layers | Oil from water, kerosene from water |
| **Magnetic separation** | Magnetism | Iron filings from sand |
| Sedimentation & decantation | Heavy particles settle | Rice washing, muddy water |
| Winnowing, threshing, sieving, hand-picking | Weight and size | Farms and kitchens |
| Loading / coagulation with **alum (phitkari)** | Alum makes tiny particles clump | **Purifying muddy water** in villages |

> **Bihar connection:** **Barauni refinery** (Begusarai; IOCL; commissioned 1964 with Soviet help) uses **fractional distillation** to separate crude oil into LPG, petrol, kerosene, diesel and bitumen. A Mains or Prelims question on "Bihar's only oil refinery" is common.

## Atomic structure

### The fundamental particles

| Particle | Charge | Relative mass | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | +1 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | Nucleus |
| Electron | −1 | ~1/1,836 | Shells (orbits) around the nucleus |

- **Atomic number (Z)** = number of **protons** (= number of electrons in a neutral atom). It **identifies the element**.
- **Mass number (A)** = protons + neutrons.
- Number of neutrons = A − Z.

### Atomic models — the story

| Model | Scientist | Year | Key idea | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atomic theory | **John Dalton** | 1808 | Atoms are indivisible; atoms of an element are identical | Atoms are divisible |
| **"Plum pudding" / watermelon model** | **J. J. Thomson** | 1904 | Electrons embedded in a sphere of positive charge | Could not explain α-scattering |
| **Nuclear model** | **Ernest Rutherford** | 1911 | **Gold-foil experiment**: most α-particles passed through, a few bounced back → atom is mostly empty space with a tiny dense positive nucleus | Orbiting electrons should lose energy and fall into the nucleus |
| **Bohr model** | **Niels Bohr** | 1913 | Electrons move in fixed **shells/energy levels (K, L, M, N)** without losing energy; jump between levels by absorbing/emitting energy | Works only for hydrogen-like atoms |
| Quantum mechanical model | Schrödinger, Heisenberg | 1920s | Electrons in **orbitals** (probability clouds); Heisenberg's uncertainty principle | Current model |

### Electronic configuration

The maximum number of electrons in a shell is **2n²**: K (n=1) = 2, L (n=2) = 8, M (n=3) = 18, N (n=4) = 32. The outermost shell does not hold more than 8 electrons. Electrons in the outermost shell are **valence electrons**; they decide chemical behaviour.

| Element | Z | Configuration | Valency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Helium | 2 | 2 | 0 (stable) |
| Carbon | 6 | 2, 4 | 4 |
| Nitrogen | 7 | 2, 5 | 3 |
| Oxygen | 8 | 2, 6 | 2 |
| Neon | 10 | 2, 8 | 0 (stable) |
| Sodium | 11 | 2, 8, 1 | 1 |
| Magnesium | 12 | 2, 8, 2 | 2 |
| Chlorine | 17 | 2, 8, 7 | 1 |
| Argon | 18 | 2, 8, 8 | 0 (stable) |
| Calcium | 20 | 2, 8, 8, 2 | 2 |

**Octet rule**: atoms tend to gain, lose or share electrons to get **8 electrons** in the outer shell (like noble gases).

### Isotopes, isobars and isotones

| Term | Same | Different | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Isotopes** | Atomic number (protons) | Mass number (neutrons) | **Protium (¹H), deuterium (²H, D), tritium (³H, radioactive)**; C-12, C-14; U-235, U-238 |
| **Isobars** | Mass number | Atomic number | Argon-40 and calcium-40 |
| **Isotones** | Number of neutrons | Protons | C-14 and N-15 |

Isotopes have the **same chemical properties** but different physical properties. **Heavy water (D₂O)** contains deuterium; it is used as a **moderator in nuclear reactors**. India makes it at Heavy Water Board plants.

### Mole concept (only the basics)

- **1 mole** = **6.022 × 10²³** particles (**Avogadro's number**).
- **Molar mass** = mass of 1 mole in grams = molecular mass in g.

### Worked example 1 — molar mass

Find the molecular mass of water (H₂O) and of carbon dioxide (CO₂). (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16)
- H₂O = 2 × 1 + 16 = **18 g/mol**
- CO₂ = 12 + 2 × 16 = **44 g/mol**
- So 36 g of water = 36 ÷ 18 = **2 moles** = 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 1.2 × 10²⁴ molecules.

### Laws of chemical combination

| Law | Given by | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Conservation of mass** | **Antoine Lavoisier** (father of modern chemistry) | Mass is neither created nor destroyed in a reaction |
| Constant (definite) proportions | Joseph Proust | A compound always has elements in the same mass ratio |
| Multiple proportions | Dalton | — |
| Avogadro's law | Amedeo Avogadro | Equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure have equal numbers of molecules |

## The periodic table

| Step | Scientist | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Triads | Johann Döbereiner (1817–29) | Groups of three similar elements |
| Law of Octaves | John Newlands (1865) | Every 8th element similar (like musical notes) |
| **Periodic law (atomic mass)** | **Dmitri Mendeleev (1869)** | Properties are periodic functions of **atomic mass**; left gaps for undiscovered elements (eka-aluminium = gallium, eka-silicon = germanium) — "father of the periodic table" |
| **Modern periodic law** | **Henry Moseley (1913)** | Properties are periodic functions of **atomic number** |

The **modern periodic table** has **18 groups** (vertical columns) and **7 periods** (horizontal rows). There are **118 elements** (last added in 2016: nihonium, moscovium, tennessine, oganesson).

### Trends (left → right across a period; top → bottom down a group)

| Property | Across a period → | Down a group ↓ |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic size | Decreases | Increases |
| Metallic character | Decreases | Increases |
| Non-metallic character | Increases | Decreases |
| Electronegativity | Increases (fluorine is highest) | Decreases |

### Important groups

| Group | Name | Members | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Alkali metals** | Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr | Very reactive; **sodium and potassium are kept in kerosene**; soft (cut with a knife) |
| 2 | Alkaline earth metals | Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra | Calcium in bones; Mg in chlorophyll |
| 17 | **Halogens** ("salt formers") | F, Cl, Br, I, At | **Fluorine** most reactive non-metal and most electronegative; **bromine** is a liquid; **iodine** a solid that sublimes |
| 18 | **Noble (inert) gases** | He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn | Full outer shell, very unreactive; **radon** is radioactive |
| 3–12 | Transition metals | Fe, Cu, Zn, Ag, Au, Pt… | Coloured compounds, catalysts |
| Bottom rows | Lanthanides & actinides | Rare earths; uranium, thorium, plutonium | **Rare earth elements** used in magnets, electronics; India has large **monazite (thorium)** sands in Kerala |

### Allotropes of carbon

**Allotropes** are different forms of the same element.

| Allotrope | Structure | Properties / uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Diamond** | Each C bonded to 4 others in 3D | Hardest; bad conductor; cutting glass, jewellery |
| **Graphite** | Layers of hexagons, each C bonded to 3 | Soft, slippery; **conducts electricity**; pencil "lead", lubricant, electrodes, moderator |
| **Fullerene (C₆₀, buckyball)** | Football-shaped | Discovered 1985 (Nobel 1996) |
| **Graphene** | Single layer of graphite | Strongest known material, very conductive (Nobel Physics 2010) |
| Carbon nanotubes | Rolled graphene | Light, strong materials |

Other examples: oxygen (O₂) and **ozone (O₃)**; white and red phosphorus; rhombic and monoclinic sulphur.

## Chemical bonding

| Bond | How formed | Properties of compounds | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ionic (electrovalent)** | **Transfer** of electrons from metal to non-metal → ions attract | High melting/boiling points; dissolve in water; **conduct electricity when molten or dissolved** | NaCl, MgCl₂, CaO |
| **Covalent** | **Sharing** of electron pairs between non-metals | Low melting points; mostly poor conductors | H₂, O₂ (double bond), N₂ (triple bond), H₂O, CH₄, CO₂ |
| Metallic | Sea of free electrons around metal ions | Conduct heat and electricity; shiny, malleable | Copper, iron |
| Hydrogen bond | Weak attraction involving H with F, O or N | Explains water's high boiling point and why ice floats | Water, DNA's two strands |

### Carbon and organic chemistry (basics)

Carbon has valency 4 and can join with itself to form long chains and rings (**catenation**). This is why there are millions of carbon compounds — the subject of **organic chemistry**.

| Series | General formula | First members | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alkanes** (single bonds, saturated) | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | Methane CH₄, ethane, propane, butane | Methane = **marsh gas**, main part of **CNG** and **biogas**; propane + butane = **LPG** |
| **Alkenes** (double bond) | CₙH₂ₙ | **Ethylene (ethene)** C₂H₄ | Ethylene ripens fruit; makes polythene |
| **Alkynes** (triple bond) | CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ | **Acetylene (ethyne)** C₂H₂ | Oxy-acetylene flame for **welding**; **calcium carbide** releases acetylene (illegally used to ripen mangoes — harmful) |

Functional groups: **alcohols** (ethanol — drinks, sanitiser, biofuel), **aldehydes** (formaldehyde/formalin preserves biological specimens), **carboxylic acids** (acetic acid — **vinegar**, 5–8%), **esters** (sweet smell — used in perfumes and flavours).

## Chemical reactions and equations

A **chemical equation** shows reactants → products. It must be **balanced** (same number of each atom on both sides) because mass is conserved.

### Worked example 2 — balancing

Balance: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
- Oxygen: 2 on left, 1 on right → put 2 before H₂O: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
- Hydrogen: now 4 on right → put 2 before H₂: **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O** ✓

Balance: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (burning of methane/CNG)
- C: 1 = 1. H: 4 on left → 2H₂O. O on right: 2 + 2 = 4 → 2O₂.
- **CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat** ✓

### Types of chemical reactions

| Type | Pattern | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| **Combination** | A + B → AB | **Quicklime + water → slaked lime** (whitewashing; very hot): CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂; burning of coal C + O₂ → CO₂ |
| **Decomposition** | AB → A + B | **Thermal**: limestone → quicklime + CO₂ (lime kilns, cement). **Electrolytic**: water → H₂ + O₂. **Photolytic** (by light): silver chloride/bromide → silver (used in **black-and-white photography**) — this is why such chemicals are kept in **dark bottles** |
| **Displacement** | A + BC → AC + B (more reactive displaces less reactive) | Iron nail in copper sulphate solution turns brown; blue colour fades: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu |
| **Double displacement** | AB + CD → AD + CB | Silver nitrate + sodium chloride → white precipitate of AgCl |
| **Precipitation** | Insoluble solid forms | Barium chloride + sodium sulphate → white BaSO₄ |
| **Neutralisation** | Acid + base → salt + water | Antacid for acidity; lime for acidic soil |
| **Combustion** | Fuel + oxygen → CO₂ + H₂O + heat + light | Burning LPG, wood, petrol |
| **Exothermic** | Releases heat | Burning, respiration, quicklime + water, dissolving NaOH in water |
| **Endothermic** | Absorbs heat | **Photosynthesis**, dissolving ammonium chloride (feels cold), cooking, decomposition of limestone |

### Oxidation and reduction (redox)

| Oxidation | Reduction |
|---|---|
| Gain of oxygen / loss of hydrogen | Loss of oxygen / gain of hydrogen |
| **Loss of electrons** | **Gain of electrons** |

> **Memory trick:** "**OIL RIG**" — **O**xidation **I**s **L**oss, **R**eduction **I**s **G**ain (of electrons).

Effects of oxidation in daily life:
- **Corrosion / rusting**: iron + oxygen + water → **hydrated iron oxide (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O)**. Rusting needs **both air and moisture**; it is faster in coastal areas (salt). Prevention: painting, oiling, greasing, **galvanisation** (zinc coating), chrome plating, making **stainless steel** (iron + chromium + nickel), alloying, **cathodic protection** (sacrificial magnesium/zinc blocks on ships and pipelines).
- Silver turns **black** (silver sulphide); copper gets a **green** coating (basic copper carbonate) — the Statue of Liberty is green for this reason.
- **Rancidity**: oils and fats get oxidised and smell bad. Prevention: **antioxidants**, airtight containers, **flushing chip packets with nitrogen**, refrigeration.
- **Delhi's Iron Pillar** (Mehrauli, Gupta period, associated with Chandragupta II) has not rusted for about 1,600 years because of high phosphorus content and a protective "misawite" film — an ancient Indian metallurgy marvel.

### Rate of reaction and catalysts

Rate of a reaction increases with **temperature**, **concentration**, **surface area** (powdered sugar dissolves faster) and with a **catalyst**. A **catalyst** speeds up a reaction without being used up.

| Process | Catalyst |
|---|---|
| **Haber process** — ammonia (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃) for fertilisers | **Iron** (with molybdenum promoter) |
| **Contact process** — sulphuric acid | **Vanadium pentoxide (V₂O₅)** |
| **Hydrogenation of oils** → vanaspati ghee | **Nickel** |
| Ostwald process — nitric acid | Platinum |
| Catalytic converters in vehicles | Platinum, palladium, rhodium (turn CO and NOx into CO₂, N₂) |
| Biological reactions | **Enzymes** (biological catalysts), e.g. amylase, pepsin, zymase in fermentation |

## Gases and gas laws (basic)

| Law | Statement | Daily example |
|---|---|---|
| **Boyle's law** | At constant temperature, **pressure × volume = constant** (P ∝ 1/V) | Bubbles grow as they rise in water; syringes |
| **Charles's law** | At constant pressure, **volume ∝ absolute temperature** | Tyres expand in the hot sun; hot-air balloons |
| Gay-Lussac's law | At constant volume, pressure ∝ temperature | Tyres can burst in summer; aerosol cans explode in fire |
| Avogadro's law | Equal volumes, same T and P → same number of molecules | — |
| Graham's law | Lighter gases diffuse faster | Hydrogen balloons go flat faster |

**STP** (standard temperature and pressure): 0 °C (273 K) and 1 atm. One mole of any gas at STP occupies about **22.4 L**.

### Important gases — properties and uses

| Gas | Key facts |
|---|---|
| Oxygen | Supports burning; discovered by **Priestley** and **Scheele**; named by Lavoisier; medical oxygen cylinders |
| Nitrogen | Inert; used in food packaging (chips), liquid nitrogen preserves semen and biological samples, in tyres |

| Carbon dioxide | Fire extinguishers (does not support burning, heavier than air), soda water, **dry ice**; greenhouse gas |
| Carbon monoxide | Colourless, odourless, poisonous; binds haemoglobin (forms carboxyhaemoglobin) ~200+ times more strongly than O₂; danger from **angithi/coal stoves in closed rooms** in winter |
| Chlorine | Disinfects drinking water; bleaching |
| Ozone | Protects us from UV in the stratosphere; pollutant near the ground |
| Methane | Marsh gas; CNG; biogas; greenhouse gas from paddy fields and cattle |
| Ammonia | Fertiliser (urea), refrigerant; pungent smell |
| Nitrous oxide (N₂O) | **Laughing gas**; anaesthetic; also a greenhouse gas |
| Sulphur dioxide | Causes acid rain; bleaching; preservative |
| Helium | Balloons; breathing mix for deep-sea divers (with O₂) to avoid "the bends" |
| Neon | Glow signs |
| Argon | Bulbs, welding |
| Xenon | Flash bulbs, car headlamps |
| Radon | Radioactive; used in cancer treatment; natural indoor pollutant |
| Phosgene, mustard gas | Poisonous war gases (World War I) |
| Methyl isocyanate (MIC) | **Bhopal gas tragedy, 2–3 December 1984** (Union Carbide) |

### Mains questions

**1. Explain the causes of corrosion and its economic impact. Suggest methods to prevent it, with examples from Indian infrastructure.** (about 250 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** corrosion is oxidation of metals by air, moisture and chemicals; rusting of iron is the commonest form.
- **Causes:** moisture and oxygen together, salts (coastal areas), acid rain, industrial gases.
- **Impact:** weakening of bridges, rail tracks, pipelines, ships, electricity towers; studies estimate losses of a few per cent of GDP globally (use qualitatively); safety risks (bridge collapses).
- **Prevention:** painting, galvanisation, stainless steel, alloying, cathodic protection, corrosion-resistant reinforcement bars, regular inspection.
- **Indian examples:** Delhi Iron Pillar as a heritage example; protection of Ganga bridges in Bihar (e.g. Mahatma Gandhi Setu rehabilitation), rail bridges.
- **Conclusion:** materials science and maintenance budgets save money and lives.

**2. "Chemistry is behind the food on our plate." Discuss the role of chemical processes in Indian agriculture and food security.** (about 300 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Green Revolution relied on chemistry — fertilisers, pesticides.
- **Fertilisers:** Haber process (ammonia) → urea; Barauni fertiliser plant (HURL, revived and restarted around 2021–22 — check latest) in Bihar; nano urea by IFFCO.
- **Soil chemistry:** pH testing, lime for acidic soils, gypsum for alkaline soils; Soil Health Cards.
- **Food processing:** preservation by salt, sugar, vinegar, refrigeration; nitrogen flushing; food irradiation.
- **Concerns:** overuse of urea, soil degradation, pesticide residues, calcium carbide ripening; shift to balanced fertilisation and organic/natural farming (Bihar's organic corridor along the Ganga — check latest).
- **Conclusion:** smart, balanced use of chemistry is key to sustainable food security.
