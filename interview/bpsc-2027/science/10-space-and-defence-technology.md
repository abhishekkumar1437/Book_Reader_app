Space and defence are the two "technology in the news" areas that BPSC asks most: "Chandrayaan-3 landed near which pole?", "Aditya-L1 is placed at which point?", "BrahMos is a joint venture with which country?", "Mission Shakti tested what?". In Mains GS Paper II, section 3 asks how science and technology (including space) helps India's and Bihar's development. This chapter gives the stable background plus recent missions. **Space missions move fast — every recent item here is "as of 2026 — check the latest" and you must update it from current affairs** (see [Defence, Space and Technology in News](/interview/bpsc-2027/current-affairs/defence-space-and-technology-in-news)).

> **In simple words:** India's space programme began with small rockets launched from a church in Thumba in 1963 and now lands on the Moon and studies the Sun. It was built for development — weather, communication, mapping, disaster warning — not for prestige alone. Defence technology aims at self-reliance (Atmanirbharta): designing missiles, aircraft and ships in India instead of buying them.

- **Polar / Sun-synchronous orbit (SSO)**: passes over the poles and crosses each place at the **same local time**, giving uniform lighting for images — used for **remote sensing** (Cartosat, Resourcesat, Oceansat, RISAT, EOS series). **PSLV** is the specialist for SSO.
- **Geostationary orbit (GEO)**: ~35,786 km above the equator; satellite moves with Earth's rotation and seems fixed — **communication and weather** (INSAT, GSAT, CMS). Launched first into **Geosynchronous Transfer Orbit (GTO)** by **GSLV / LVM3**, then raised by the satellite's own motor.
- **Lagrange points** (L1–L5): points where the gravity of two bodies (Sun and Earth) balance so a spacecraft can stay with little fuel. **L1** is ~**1.5 million km** from Earth towards the Sun — **Aditya-L1** is in a **halo orbit** around it (so it can watch the Sun without eclipses).

## Launch vehicles

| Rocket | Stages / fuel | Capacity & role | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SLV-3** | 4 solid stages | ~40 kg to LEO | First Indian launcher (Rohini, 1980); Kalam |
| ASLV | 5 solid stages | ~150 kg | Augmented SLV, 1987–94 |
| **PSLV** (Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle) | 4 stages: **solid–liquid (Vikas)–solid–liquid** | ~1,750 kg to SSO; ~1,400 kg to GTO | **"Workhorse of ISRO"**; launched Chandrayaan-1, Mangalyaan, Aditya-L1, 104-satellite record; versions PSLV-G, -CA, -XL, -DL, -QL |
| **GSLV** (Mk II) | 3 stages incl. **indigenous cryogenic upper stage** (liquid hydrogen + liquid oxygen) | ~2,250 kg to GTO | Cryogenic tech was denied under MTCR pressure in 1990s; India built its own (first successful flight **GSLV-D5, January 2014**) |
| **LVM3** (formerly GSLV Mk III) | 2 solid strap-ons (S200) + liquid core (L110) + cryogenic (C25) | **~4,000 kg to GTO**, ~8,000–10,000 kg to LEO | India's **heaviest rocket**; launched **Chandrayaan-2, Chandrayaan-3**, OneWeb constellations; **human-rated version** for Gaganyaan; nicknamed "Bahubali" |
| **SSLV** (Small Satellite Launch Vehicle) | 3 solid stages + velocity trimming module | ~500 kg to LEO | Low-cost, launch-on-demand; first success **SSLV-D2 (Feb 2023)**, D3 (Aug 2024, EOS-08); technology transferred to industry (HAL won the bid in 2025 — check latest) |
| **RLV-TD** | Winged reusable vehicle | Technology demonstrator | Autonomous **landing experiments (RLV-LEX)** succeeded 2023–24 at Chitradurga, Karnataka ("Pushpak") |
| **NGLV** (Next Generation Launch Vehicle) | Reusable first stage, semi-cryogenic | Up to ~30,000 kg to LEO | Approved **September 2024**; for Bharatiya Antariksh Station (as of 2026 — check latest) |

> **Prelims angle:** **Cryogenic engine** = uses fuels at very low temperatures (**liquid hydrogen at −253 °C** and **liquid oxygen at −183 °C**). **Semi-cryogenic engine** = refined kerosene + liquid oxygen (ISRO's SE2000 under development). The **Vikas engine** (liquid, named after Vikram Sarabhai) powers PSLV's second stage and GSLV/LVM3 stages. **Scramjet** (supersonic combustion ramjet) uses oxygen from the air — ISRO tested one in **2016**.

### How space helps development (Mains content)

- **Agriculture**: crop acreage and yield estimation (**FASAL** programme), drought assessment, soil moisture, crop insurance (**PMFBY** uses satellite data), **CHAMAN** (horticulture mapping).
- **Disaster management**: cyclone warnings (Odisha's near-zero deaths in recent cyclones show the value), **flood inundation maps** (NRSC and **Bhuvan**) — very relevant to **Bihar's Kosi and Gandak floods**, landslide and forest-fire alerts, **Disaster Management Support Programme**.
- **Fisheries**: **Potential Fishing Zone (PFZ)** advisories from INCOIS using ocean colour data.
- **Governance**: geotagging of rural-employment assets (Bhuvan; done under MGNREGA, which was replaced by the **VB-G RAM G Act** in December 2025 — as of 2026, check latest), **PMAY-G**, **SVAMITVA** (drone + satellite-based property cards for villages, launched 24 April 2020), land records, water bodies (Bhuvan-based monitoring of Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali assets).
- **Communication and education**: DTH, VSAT, telemedicine to remote areas, **SWAYAM PRABHA** educational TV channels, **disaster communication** when ground networks fail.
- **Navigation**: NavIC, GAGAN; train tracking (**RTIS**).
- **Climate and environment**: monitoring glaciers, forest cover (**India State of Forest Report** uses satellite data), air quality, wetlands.
- **Urban planning**: AMRUT GIS master plans, Smart Cities.

> **Bihar connection:** Space data supports **Bihar's flood forecasting and mapping** (Flood Management Improvement Support Centre — FMISC, Water Resources Department), the **Bihar Remote Sensing Application Centre** at Patna, crop assessment for kharif rice and rabi maize, **embankment monitoring** along the Kosi, and Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali asset mapping. ## India's major space missions

### Chandrayaan-1 (2008)

- Launched **22 October 2008** by **PSLV-C11** from Sriharikota; India's **first lunar mission**.
- Carried 11 instruments (5 Indian, 6 foreign). Its **Moon Impact Probe** hit the surface near the south pole (with the Indian tricolour) on 14 November 2008 — the impact point was named **Jawahar Sthal**.
- **Discovered evidence of water molecules (H₂O/OH) on the Moon** (NASA's **Moon Mineralogy Mapper, M³**, and ISRO's data) — a historic finding.
- Mission ended in August 2009 when contact was lost.

### Mars Orbiter Mission — Mangalyaan (2013)

- Launched **5 November 2013** by **PSLV-C25**; entered **Mars orbit on 24 September 2014**.
- India became the **first country to reach Mars orbit in its first attempt** and the **first Asian nation** to reach Mars orbit; ISRO became the fourth space agency to reach Mars (after the Soviet space programme, NASA and ESA).
- Very **low cost** (about ₹450 crore) — often compared with a Hollywood film budget.
- Designed for 6 months, it worked for about **8 years** (contact lost in 2022). A second Mars mission is planned (check latest).

### Chandrayaan-2 (2019)

- Launched **22 July 2019** by **GSLV Mk III-M1 (LVM3)**; orbiter + lander **Vikram** + rover **Pragyan**.
- The **Vikram lander crash-landed** on 6–7 September 2019 due to a guidance/software problem in the final descent. The crash site was later named **"Tiranga Point"**.
- The **orbiter is still working** and supported Chandrayaan-3 (it has a very high-resolution camera, OHRC).

### Chandrayaan-3 (2023)

- Launched **14 July 2023** by **LVM3-M4**; lander **Vikram** and rover **Pragyan**, with a propulsion module (no new orbiter).
- **Soft-landed on 23 August 2023** near the **lunar south pole** (about **69° S**) — **India became the 4th country to soft-land on the Moon** (after the USSR, USA and China) and the **first to land near the south polar region**.
- The landing point was named **"Shiv Shakti Point"**; **23 August is now National Space Day** (first observed in 2024).
- Pragyan's **LIBS** instrument confirmed **sulphur** (and detected aluminium, calcium, iron, chromium, titanium, manganese, silicon, oxygen) near the south pole; **ChaSTE** measured the temperature profile of the lunar soil. Vikram also performed a **"hop" experiment** (lifted and re-landed). The propulsion module was later brought back to Earth orbit — practice for sample-return missions.
- Why the south pole? Permanently shadowed craters may hold **water ice**, useful for future bases (drinking water, oxygen, rocket fuel).

### Aditya-L1 (2023)

- India's **first space-based solar observatory**, launched **2 September 2023** by **PSLV-C57**; reached a **halo orbit around Sun–Earth L1** (~1.5 million km from Earth) on **6 January 2024**.
- Carries **7 payloads**, including **VELC** (Visible Emission Line Coronagraph — studies the **corona** and **coronal mass ejections**) and **SUIT** (Solar Ultraviolet Imaging Telescope). Studies **solar wind**, **solar flares** and **space weather** that can damage satellites and power grids.

### Human spaceflight — Gaganyaan

- **Gaganyaan** is India's **first human spaceflight programme** (announced 15 August 2018). Goal: send a crew of **2–3 astronauts** to a ~**400 km** low Earth orbit for up to 3 days and bring them back safely by **sea splashdown** (Bay of Bengal/Arabian Sea).
- Rocket: **human-rated LVM3 (HLVM3)**. Crew module + service module. **Crew Escape System** tested in **TV-D1 (21 October 2023)**. Parachute drop tests and the Integrated Air Drop Test (2025) followed.
- Uncrewed flights first (**G1** with the humanoid robot **"Vyommitra"**), then the crewed flight (H1). Dates have shifted several times — **as of 2026, check the latest schedule**.
- **Astronaut-designates** (announced **27 February 2024**): Group Captain **Prasanth Balakrishnan Nair**, Group Captain **Ajit Krishnan**, Group Captain **Angad Pratap**, Wing Commander (now Group Captain) **Shubhanshu Shukla** — all Indian Air Force test pilots, trained partly in **Russia**.
- **Axiom-4 (2025)**: **Shubhanshu Shukla** flew as **pilot** of the private **Axiom Mission 4** (SpaceX Dragon, launched **25 June 2025**) and spent about **18 days** aboard the **International Space Station** — the **first Indian on the ISS** and the **second Indian in space after Rakesh Sharma (1984)**. He carried out Indian microgravity experiments (e.g. on seeds, microalgae and muscle).
- **Indian-origin astronauts** (not Indian citizens when they flew): **Kalpana Chawla** (first Indian-born woman in space, 1997; died in the **Columbia** disaster, 2003), **Sunita Williams** (long ISS stays; returned in March 2025 after an extended mission).

## Space reforms and the private sector

| Reform | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Antrix Corporation** (1992) | ISRO's first commercial arm |
| **NSIL** (NewSpace India Limited, **March 2019**) | Public-sector company under DoS; commercialises ISRO technology, builds and owns "demand-driven" satellites, sells launch services |
| **IN-SPACe** (Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre, **June 2020**) | Single-window **regulator and promoter** for private space activities; HQ **Ahmedabad** |
| **Indian Space Policy 2023** (April 2023) | Defines roles: ISRO focuses on R&D and advanced missions; private sector can do end-to-end activities (launch, satellites, ground stations) |
| **FDI liberalisation** (February 2024) | Up to **100%** FDI allowed in parts of the space sector (e.g. satellite components); **74%** automatic for satellite manufacturing and operations, **49%** automatic for launch vehicles (higher with approval) |
| **Space vision** | Indian space economy targeted to grow manyfold (e.g. ~$44 billion by 2033 — check latest); India's share of global space economy is still small (~2%) |
| **Private milestones** | **Skyroot Aerospace — Vikram-S**, India's **first privately built rocket**, sub-orbital flight on **18 November 2022** ("Mission Prarambh"); **Agnikul Cosmos — Agnibaan SOrTeD** (30 May 2024) from India's first private launch pad at Sriharikota, using the world's first **single-piece 3D-printed** semi-cryogenic engine; **Pixxel** (hyperspectral imaging satellites), **Dhruva Space**, **Digantara** (space situational awareness); OneWeb/Starlink-type satellite internet licences (check latest) |

### International space law and cooperation

- **Outer Space Treaty (1967)** — space for peaceful purposes, no nuclear weapons in orbit, no national claims on celestial bodies. India is a party.
- **Artemis Accords** (US-led principles for Moon exploration) — **India signed in June 2023**.
- **UNOOSA** — UN Office for Outer Space Affairs (Vienna).
- **Indian Space Situational Assessment** reports and **NETRA** project track space debris; Mission Shakti raised debris debates.

## Defence technology

### Institutions

- **DRDO** (Defence Research and Development Organisation) — formed in **1958**; HQ **New Delhi**; about 50 laboratories. Motto "Balasya Mulam Vigyanam" (the source of strength is science).
- **Defence PSUs**: **HAL** (aircraft, helicopters, Bengaluru), **BEL** (electronics, radars), **BDL** (missiles, Hyderabad), **Mazagon Dock** (submarines, destroyers, Mumbai), **Cochin Shipyard** (INS Vikrant), **GRSE** (Kolkata), **BEML**. The old **Ordnance Factory Board** was split into **7 new defence PSUs** in **October 2021** (e.g. Munitions India Ltd, AWEIL, AVNL).
- **Chief of Defence Staff (CDS)** — post created **1 January 2020** (first CDS **Gen Bipin Rawat**); heads the **Department of Military Affairs** (current CDS — as of 2026, check latest).

> **Bihar connection:** **Ordnance Factory Nalanda** at **Rajgir** (now under **Munitions India Ltd**) makes **Bi-Modular Charge Systems (BMCS)** — propellant charges for artillery guns. The **Bihar Regiment** (Regimental Centre at **Danapur**, Patna) is among the Army's most decorated — its **16 Bihar** battalion fought at **Galwan (June 2020)**. These are good Bihar links in a defence answer.

### Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme (IGMDP)

Launched in **1983** under **Dr A. P. J. Abdul Kalam**; formally completed in **2008**. It produced five missile systems:

| Missile | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Prithvi** | Surface-to-surface, short-range ballistic (SRBM) | **First missile** of IGMDP (1988); ~150–350 km; **Dhanush** is the naval version |
| **Agni** | Surface-to-surface, intermediate to intercontinental-range ballistic | Agni-I (~700 km) to **Agni-V (5,000+ km, ICBM-class; first test 2012)**; **Agni-Prime** (new-generation, canisterised); **Mission Divyastra (11 March 2024)** — Agni-V tested with **MIRV** (Multiple Independently-targetable Re-entry Vehicles — one missile, many warheads) |
| **Trishul** | Short-range surface-to-air | Technology later used elsewhere; programme closed |
| **Akash** | Medium-range **surface-to-air** missile (SAM) | ~25–30 km (Akash-NG longer); inducted by Army and IAF; **exported** (Armenia); performed in air defence in 2025 (as reported — check) |
| **Nag** | **Anti-tank**, "**fire-and-forget**" | Third-generation; **NAMICA** carrier; **Helina/Dhruvastra** (helicopter-launched) |

> **Memory trick:** "**PATNA**" — **P**rithvi, **A**gni, **T**rishul, **N**ag, **A**kash (and Patna is Bihar's capital — easy to remember!).

| **Ballistic Missile Defence (BMD)** | Two-tier (PAD, AAD; phase-II AD-1, AD-2) | Shields cities against incoming missiles |

**Test site**: **Dr A. P. J. Abdul Kalam Island** (earlier **Wheeler Island**) off **Odisha** and the **Integrated Test Range, Chandipur (Balasore)**.

**Ballistic vs cruise missiles**: a **ballistic missile** is rocket-powered only at the start and then follows a high arc (like a thrown stone) — e.g. Agni, Prithvi. A **cruise missile** flies low, powered throughout, like a pilotless aircraft — e.g. BrahMos, Nirbhay.

### Nuclear capability and doctrine

| Event | Facts |
|---|---|
| **Pokhran-I** | **18 May 1974**, "**Smiling Buddha**" — called a "peaceful nuclear explosion"; led by **Raja Ramanna** |
| **Pokhran-II** | **11 and 13 May 1998**, "**Operation Shakti**" — five tests; India declared itself a nuclear weapon state; **11 May = National Technology Day** |
| **Nuclear doctrine (2003)** | **No First Use (NFU)**; **credible minimum deterrence**; **massive retaliation** if attacked; no use against non-nuclear states; civilian political control through the **Nuclear Command Authority** (Political Council headed by the PM; Executive Council by the NSA); Strategic Forces Command |
| **Nuclear triad** | Ability to deliver nuclear weapons from **land (Agni), air (fighter aircraft) and sea (SSBN submarines)**; completed when **INS Arihant** completed its first **deterrence patrol (November 2018)** |
| Treaties | India has **not signed the NPT** (1968) or the **CTBT** (1996) — calls them discriminatory; has a **civil nuclear deal with the USA (2008)** and an NSG waiver (2008); member of **MTCR (2016)**, **Wassenaar Arrangement (2017)**, **Australia Group (2018)**; NSG membership not yet obtained |

### Mains questions

**1. "India's space programme is a programme for development, not prestige." Examine with examples, and explain how space technology can help Bihar.** (38 marks / about 500 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Sarabhai's vision — use space to solve the real problems of man and society; from Thumba to Chandrayaan-3.
- **Communication:** INSAT/GSAT — DTH, telemedicine, tele-education, banking connectivity in remote areas.
- **Earth observation:** crop estimation (FASAL), crop insurance, groundwater prospect maps, forest and wetland monitoring.
- **Disaster management:** cyclone and flood warnings, inundation maps, post-disaster damage assessment.
- **Navigation:** NavIC for transport, fishing, emergency alerts.
- **Governance:** Bhuvan geotagging (rural employment works, PMAY), SVAMITVA property cards, urban planning.
- **Bihar:** Kosi–Gandak flood mapping and forecasting with FMISC; embankment monitoring; rabi maize and kharif paddy estimation; Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali asset mapping; lightning forecasting with satellite and radar data; tele-education in schools; land-record digitisation.
- **Prestige also matters:** Chandrayaan-3, Aditya-L1 inspire students, strengthen soft power and the space economy (start-ups, jobs).
- **Challenges:** low budget share, need for private investment, skill gaps, data access for states.
- **Conclusion:** space is a development tool; Bihar should build state-level capacity (remote sensing centre, trained district staff) to use it fully.

**2. Discuss the significance of Chandrayaan-3 and Aditya-L1 for India's scientific and strategic capabilities.** (about 300 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** 2023 — soft landing near the lunar south pole and India's first solar observatory.
- **Chandrayaan-3:** 4th nation to soft-land; first near south pole; in-situ science (sulphur, temperature profile); proven landing technology for future sample return (Chandrayaan-4) and crewed missions; low-cost engineering.
- **Aditya-L1:** continuous Sun observation from L1; space weather understanding to protect satellites, power grids and communication; scientific data for researchers.
- **Strategic:** technology demonstration, international partnerships (Artemis Accords, LUPEX with Japan), commercial credibility for launches.
- **Social:** inspiration (National Space Day), STEM interest.
- **Conclusion:** these missions show maturity; sustained funding and private participation will decide the next decade.

**3. "Self-reliance in defence production is both a security need and an economic opportunity." Discuss.** (about 300 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** India has been among the world's largest arms importers; dependence creates risks in crises (sanctions, supply delays).
- **Security need:** assured supply, technology control, customisation; lessons from recent conflicts.
- **Measures:** DAP 2020 (IDDM), positive indigenisation lists, corridors (UP, TN), iDEX, 74% FDI, corporatisation of OFB, private players (Tata C-295 line).
- **Achievements:** Tejas, INS Vikrant, Akash, BrahMos exports, Pinaka, rising exports.
- **Economic opportunity:** MSME supply chains, jobs, exports, spin-off civilian technologies.
- **Challenges:** low R&D spending, engine technology gap (aero-engines), delays, testing infrastructure, skilled manpower.
- **Bihar angle:** Ordnance Factory Nalanda; potential for MSMEs, IIT Patna research, skill centres to join defence supply chains.
- **Conclusion:** self-reliance is a long-term project needing steady policy, R&D funding and private participation.
