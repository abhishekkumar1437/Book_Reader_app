Heat, light and sound give BPSC some of its most predictable science questions: "Why is the sky blue?", "Which lens corrects short-sightedness?", "At what temperature are Celsius and Fahrenheit equal?", "Why does sound travel faster in summer?". This chapter explains each idea once, simply, and then lists the applications that examiners like to ask.

> **In simple words:** Heat is energy that flows from a hotter body to a colder one. Light is a wave that travels in straight lines, bends when it enters a new medium, and splits into colours. Sound is a vibration that needs a medium (air, water, solid) to travel — it cannot pass through empty space.

## Heat and temperature

**Heat** is a form of energy (unit: **joule**; older unit **calorie**, 1 cal ≈ 4.18 J). **Temperature** tells how hot or cold a body is — it measures the average kinetic energy of its molecules (SI unit: **kelvin**). Heat flows from higher temperature to lower temperature until both are equal (**thermal equilibrium**).

### Worked example 1 — conversion

Convert 104 °F (high fever) into Celsius.
- C = (F − 32) × 5/9 = (104 − 32) × 5/9 = 72 × 5/9 = **40 °C**

### Thermometers

| Thermometer | Use |
|---|---|
| Clinical thermometer | Body temperature, range about 35–42 °C; has a **kink** (constriction) so the mercury does not fall back. Digital thermometers are now replacing mercury ones because mercury is toxic. |
| Laboratory thermometer | −10 to 110 °C |
| Pyrometer | Very high temperatures (furnaces, the Sun) by measuring radiation |
| Thermocouple | Uses the electric voltage produced at the junction of two metals |
| Six's maximum–minimum thermometer | Records the day's highest and lowest temperatures (weather stations) |

Mercury is used because it is a good conductor, expands uniformly, is visible (shiny) and does not stick to glass. **Alcohol** thermometers are used for very cold places because alcohol freezes at about −115 °C (mercury freezes at about −39 °C).

### Thermal expansion

Most substances **expand on heating** and contract on cooling.
- **Gaps are left between railway rails** and in bridges (expansion joints).
- Electric and telephone wires **sag in summer** and become tight in winter.
- A tight metal lid on a glass bottle opens easily after dipping it in hot water (metal expands more than glass).
- Thick glass tumblers can crack when hot water is poured in (uneven expansion). **Pyrex/borosilicate** glass expands very little, so it is used in labs and ovens.
- **Bimetallic strips** (two metals joined) bend on heating — used in **thermostats** of irons, fridges and fire alarms.

### Anomalous expansion of water

Water behaves strangely: when cooled from 4 °C to 0 °C it **expands instead of contracting**. So water has its **maximum density at 4 °C**.
- In winter, ponds and lakes freeze from the **top**. The ice floats and acts as an insulator, while water at 4 °C stays at the bottom — **fish and aquatic life survive**.
- Water pipes **burst** in very cold regions because water expands on freezing.
- Ice is lighter than water and floats.

### Conduction

Heat passes from molecule to molecule without the molecules moving from their place. **Metals are good conductors** (silver is the best, then copper, then aluminium). **Bad conductors (insulators)**: wood, plastic, air, wool, glass, water.
- Cooking pans have **wooden or plastic handles**.
- **Woollen clothes keep us warm** because they trap air, a bad conductor.
- Two thin blankets are warmer than one thick blanket (air layer between them).
- **Igloos** (made of snow) keep Inuit people warm — snow and trapped air are bad conductors.
- On a cold morning, a metal chair feels colder than a wooden one at the same temperature, because metal conducts heat away from your hand faster.

### Convection

Heat is carried by the actual movement of the heated fluid (liquid or gas). Hot fluid rises, cold fluid sinks, forming **convection currents**.
- **Land and sea breezes** — by day the land heats faster, air above it rises, cool sea breeze blows in; at night the opposite (land breeze).
- **Monsoon winds** are convection on a giant scale.
- Ventilators are placed **near the ceiling**; room heaters are placed low and ACs high.
- Chimneys work by convection.

### Radiation

Heat travels as **electromagnetic waves (infrared)** and needs no medium. It is the fastest mode (speed of light).
- **Black and dull surfaces absorb and emit** radiation best; **white and shiny surfaces reflect** it. That is why we wear **white clothes in summer** and dark clothes in winter, and the bottoms of cooking pots are blackened.
- **Greenhouse effect**: sunlight enters through glass/atmosphere, but the infrared heat radiated back is trapped.

### Thermos flask (vacuum flask)

Invented by **Sir James Dewar** (1892). It has double glass walls with a **vacuum** between them (stops conduction and convection), the walls are **silvered** (stops radiation) and it has a cork/plastic stopper.

## Specific heat and latent heat

**Specific heat capacity**: heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K). **Water has a very high specific heat** (about **4,186 J/kg·K**, i.e. 1 cal/g·°C).
- Water is used in **car radiators** and hot-water bottles.
- Coastal places (Mumbai, Chennai) have a **moderate (equable) climate**, while inland places like Patna have hot summers and cold winters (**continental climate**) — the sea heats and cools slowly.
- Sand at a beach is hot by day, but sea water stays cool.

**Latent heat**: heat absorbed or released when a substance **changes state without any change in temperature**.
- **Latent heat of fusion of ice** ≈ 336 J/g (80 cal/g).
- **Latent heat of vaporisation of water** ≈ 2,260 J/g (540 cal/g).
- So **burns from steam at 100 °C are more severe than from boiling water at 100 °C** — steam gives out its extra latent heat when it condenses on the skin.
- Ice at 0 °C cools a drink better than water at 0 °C (it absorbs latent heat while melting).

### Boiling, melting and pressure

- **Boiling point falls when pressure falls.** On high mountains, water boils below 100 °C, so food cooks slowly.
- **Boiling point rises when pressure rises.** A **pressure cooker** raises the pressure, so water boils at about **120 °C** and food cooks faster.
- **Impurities** (like salt) **raise the boiling point** and **lower the freezing point** of water. Salt is sprinkled on icy roads; ice + salt is used in old-style ice-cream making (freezing mixture).
- **Melting point of ice falls when pressure increases** — this is called **regelation** (ice melts under the pressure of a wire passing through it and refreezes above it). Ice skating is easier partly because of a thin melted layer.

### Evaporation and cooling

**Evaporation** happens at all temperatures, only at the surface, and **causes cooling** (fast molecules escape, the average energy of those left falls).
- Water in an **earthen pot (matka/surahi)** stays cool — water seeps through tiny pores and evaporates.
- We **sweat** to cool down; we feel cooler under a fan because it speeds up evaporation of sweat.
- A **desert cooler** works better in dry heat than in humid weather (evaporation is slower in humid air).
- Wet clothes dry faster on a windy, sunny, dry day.
- Rate of evaporation increases with temperature, surface area and wind speed; it decreases with humidity.

### Humidity

**Relative humidity** = actual water vapour in air ÷ maximum the air can hold at that temperature × 100%. Measured with a **hygrometer**. High humidity (as in Bihar's monsoon months) makes us feel sticky because sweat does not evaporate. **Dew** forms when air cools at night below its **dew point**. Humid summer days in the Gangetic plains feel hotter than the thermometer shows — this is captured by the **heat index**.

### Refrigerator and AC

A fridge works by **evaporation of a refrigerant** inside the coils (absorbing heat from inside) and its compression and condensation outside (releasing heat at the back). Earlier refrigerants were **CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons)**, which damage the **ozone layer**; they were phased out under the **Montreal Protocol (1987)**. Their replacements (HFCs) are greenhouse gases, being phased down under the **Kigali Amendment (2016)**. India's **India Cooling Action Plan (2019)** was among the first such national plans.

## Light: nature and propagation

Light is an **electromagnetic wave** that needs no medium. Its speed in vacuum is **c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s** (about 3 lakh km per second). It slows down in denser media: in water it is about 2.25 × 10⁸ m/s and in glass about 2 × 10⁸ m/s. Sunlight takes about **8 minutes 20 seconds** (≈ 500 s) to reach the Earth; moonlight takes about 1.3 s.

- **Newton** proposed the corpuscular (particle) theory; **Huygens** the wave theory; **Maxwell** showed light is electromagnetic; **Einstein** (photoelectric effect) and **Planck** (quantum) showed light also comes in packets called **photons**. Today we say light has a **dual nature** — wave and particle.
- Light travels in **straight lines** (rectilinear propagation). This explains **shadows**, **eclipses** and the **pinhole camera** (which forms an inverted image).

### The electromagnetic spectrum

| Wave (from longest to shortest wavelength) | Main uses |
|---|---|
| Radio waves | Radio, TV broadcasting, mobile phones (microwave/radio bands) |
| Microwaves | Microwave ovens, radar, satellite communication |
| Infrared | TV remote, night vision, heat lamps, thermal imaging |
| Visible light (VIBGYOR) | Seeing; violet ≈ 400 nm to red ≈ 700 nm |
| Ultraviolet (UV) | Sterilising water and surgical tools, forming vitamin D in skin; mostly blocked by ozone |
| X-rays | Medical imaging of bones, airport baggage checks (discovered by **Röntgen**, 1895) |
| Gamma rays | Cancer therapy (radiotherapy), sterilising food; come from radioactive nuclei |

> **Memory trick:** "**R**aja **M**ahendra **I**n **V**aranasi **U**ses **X**-ray **G**uns" → Radio, Micro, Infrared, Visible, UV, X-ray, Gamma — wavelength decreasing, energy and frequency increasing.

All these waves travel at the same speed c in vacuum.

## Reflection of light

When light bounces back from a surface, it is **reflected**. **Laws**: (1) angle of incidence = angle of reflection; (2) the incident ray, reflected ray and the normal lie in the same plane.

**Plane mirror**: image is virtual, erect, same size, as far behind the mirror as the object is in front, and **laterally inverted** (left appears right). That is why "AMBULANCE" is written in mirror letters on the front of ambulances. To see your full height, the mirror must be at least **half your height**. Two mirrors at angle θ form (360/θ − 1) images; at 60° that is **5 images** (the kaleidoscope uses this). Parallel mirrors give infinite images.

### Spherical mirrors

| Mirror | Image | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Concave** (converging) | Real & inverted (object far) or virtual, erect & **magnified** (object very close) | **Shaving and make-up mirrors**, **dentist's mirror**, **headlights and torches** (bulb at focus gives parallel beam), solar cookers and solar furnaces, reflecting telescopes |
| **Convex** (diverging) | Always virtual, erect and **diminished** | **Rear-view mirrors** of vehicles (wide field of view), street-light reflectors, security mirrors in shops and at blind turns |

Focal length f = R/2 (half the radius of curvature). Mirror formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u.

## Refraction of light

When light passes from one medium to another, it **bends**. This is **refraction**. It bends **towards the normal** when going into a denser medium (air → water) and **away from the normal** when going into a rarer medium. **Refractive index (n)** = speed of light in vacuum ÷ speed in the medium. Water ≈ 1.33, glass ≈ 1.5, **diamond ≈ 2.42** (very high).

Daily-life effects of refraction:
- A pool or tank of water looks **shallower** than it really is.
- A stick or pencil half-dipped in water looks **bent**.
- **Stars twinkle** — starlight is refracted by changing layers of the atmosphere. **Planets do not twinkle** much because they are closer and appear as small discs, not points.
- The Sun is visible about **2 minutes before actual sunrise and after actual sunset** (atmospheric refraction).
- Letters under a glass slab appear raised.

### Total internal reflection (TIR)

When light goes from a denser to a rarer medium at an angle larger than the **critical angle**, it is reflected back completely. Examples:
- **Sparkling of diamond** (very small critical angle, about 24°).
- **Mirage** in deserts and on hot roads — hot air near the ground is rarer; light from the sky bends and reflects, looking like water.
- **Optical fibres** — light travels through thin glass fibres by repeated TIR; used in internet cables and **endoscopy**. **Narinder Singh Kapany** (Indian-born) is called the "father of fibre optics".
- An empty test tube in water looks silvery.

## Lenses

A **lens** is a piece of transparent material with curved surfaces.

### Power of a lens

**Power P = 1/f**, where f is in **metres**. Unit: **dioptre (D)**. Convex lens: positive power; concave lens: negative power.

### Worked example 2 — power of a lens

An optician prescribes a lens of **−2.0 D**. What kind of lens is it and what is its focal length?
- f = 1/P = 1/(−2) = **−0.5 m = −50 cm**
- Negative power → **concave lens** → the person has **myopia**.

## The human eye and its defects

The eye works like a camera. The **cornea** and the **lens** focus light on the **retina**, which has two kinds of cells: **rods** (dim light, black-and-white vision) and **cones** (bright light and **colour** vision). The **iris** (coloured part) controls the size of the **pupil**. The **ciliary muscles** change the shape of the lens to focus near or far objects — this is called **accommodation**. The image on the retina is **real and inverted**; the brain makes it erect. The **optic nerve** carries signals to the brain; the point where it leaves has no rods or cones — the **blind spot**.

- **Least distance of distinct vision** (near point) for a normal eye: **25 cm**. Far point: infinity.
- **Persistence of vision**: an image stays on the retina for about **1/16 second**. This is why movies (24 or more frames per second) look continuous.

| Defect | Problem | Cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Myopia** (short-sightedness) | Cannot see **far** objects clearly | Eyeball too long / lens too thick; image forms **in front of** retina | **Concave lens** |
| **Hypermetropia** (long-sightedness) | Cannot see **near** objects clearly | Eyeball too short; image forms **behind** retina | **Convex lens** |
| **Presbyopia** | Difficulty with near objects in old age | Ciliary muscles weaken | **Bifocal** or progressive lenses (Benjamin Franklin invented bifocals) |
| **Astigmatism** | Blurred vision in some directions | Uneven curvature of cornea | **Cylindrical lens** |
| **Cataract** | Cloudy vision | Lens becomes opaque (often with age) | Surgery — replacement with an artificial **intraocular lens** |
| Night blindness | Poor vision in dim light | Lack of **vitamin A** | Vitamin A-rich diet |
| Colour blindness | Cannot tell red from green (common type) | Genetic, sex-linked; more common in males | No cure |

> **Memory trick:** "**M**yopia → **C**oncave" (MC like "Mic Check"); "Hyper → convex" (the other one).

**Eye donation**: it is the **cornea** that is transplanted. Corneal blindness can be cured by donated eyes.

## Dispersion and scattering of light

### Dispersion

**Dispersion** is the splitting of white light into its seven colours (**VIBGYOR**: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red) when it passes through a prism. **Newton** first showed this. Red bends the least (longest wavelength), violet the most.

**Rainbow** forms when sunlight is dispersed, refracted and internally reflected by raindrops. You see a rainbow only with the **Sun behind you**, usually in the morning or late afternoon after rain. Red is on the outer side of the primary rainbow.

### Scattering

Tiny particles in the air scatter light. **Shorter wavelengths (blue, violet) are scattered much more** than longer ones (red) — this is **Rayleigh scattering**.
- **Sky is blue** because blue light is scattered most (our eyes are more sensitive to blue than violet).
- **Sun looks red/orange at sunrise and sunset** — light travels a longer path through the atmosphere and most blue is scattered away, leaving red.
- **Danger signals and brake lights are red** — red is scattered least, so it can be seen from far away even in fog.
- **Sky looks black to astronauts** in space — no atmosphere to scatter light.
- **Clouds look white** — water droplets are large and scatter all colours equally.
- The sea looks blue partly due to scattering and reflection of the sky.

### The Raman effect

**Sir C. V. Raman** discovered that when light passes through a transparent substance, a small part of the scattered light changes its wavelength. This **Raman effect** was announced on **28 February 1928**. Raman won the **Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930** — the **first Asian and first Indian** to win a Nobel in science. India celebrates **28 February as National Science Day**. Raman spectroscopy is used to identify chemicals, test drugs and study materials.

> **Prelims angle:** "National Science Day is celebrated on —" (28 February, Raman effect). Do not confuse with **National Technology Day (11 May**, Pokhran-II nuclear tests 1998) or **National Space Day (23 August**, Chandrayaan-3 landing, 2023).

### Colours

- **Primary colours of light**: **red, green, blue (RGB)** — used in TV and mobile screens. Red + green + blue = white; red + green = yellow; blue + green = cyan; red + blue = magenta.
- An object looks a certain colour because it **reflects** that colour and absorbs others. A red rose under green light looks **black**.
- **Primary colours of pigments (paints)** are different: cyan, magenta, yellow (CMY, plus black K in printers).

### Optical instruments

| Instrument | Uses / fact |
|---|---|
| Simple microscope | One convex lens (magnifying glass) |
| Compound microscope | Two convex lenses (objective + eyepiece); **Robert Hooke** saw cells (1665); **Leeuwenhoek** saw bacteria |
| Electron microscope | Uses electrons instead of light; much higher magnification (Knoll & Ruska, 1931) |
| Telescope | **Galileo** first used it for astronomy (1609); reflecting telescope by **Newton** |
| Periscope | Two plane mirrors (or prisms) at 45°; used in submarines and trenches |
| Kaleidoscope | Mirrors at angles making repeated patterns |
| Binoculars | Uses prisms with total internal reflection |
| Spectroscope | Splits light to study its spectrum |

## Sound

**Sound** is produced by **vibrating bodies** and travels as a **longitudinal wave** (particles vibrate back and forth in the direction of travel, forming compressions and rarefactions). It **needs a medium** — sound cannot travel in vacuum, so astronauts on the Moon talk by radio.

### Speed of sound

| Medium | Approximate speed |
|---|---|
| Air at 0 °C | 332 m/s |
| Air at 20 °C | 343 m/s |
| Water | about 1,500 m/s |
| Iron / steel | about 5,000–6,000 m/s |

- Sound is **fastest in solids**, slower in liquids and slowest in gases. (Opposite of light, which is fastest in vacuum.)
- Speed in air **increases with temperature** (about 0.6 m/s per °C) and with **humidity** (moist air is less dense) — so sound travels faster on humid days and in summer.
- Speed of sound does **not** change with pressure (at constant temperature).
- We see **lightning before hearing thunder** because light is far faster than sound.

### Worked example 3 — lightning distance

You hear thunder 3 seconds after seeing a lightning flash. How far is the lightning? (speed of sound = 340 m/s)
- Distance = speed × time = 340 × 3 = **1,020 m ≈ 1 km**

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar is among the states most affected by **lightning deaths** in India, especially in the pre-monsoon and monsoon months. The Bihar government's **"Indra Vajra" mobile app** sends lightning warnings to users in an area before a strike. The "30-second rule" (if thunder follows within 30 s, lightning is within about 10 km — go indoors) is a simple physics safety tip. This links science to disaster management for Mains.

### Characteristics of sound

| Characteristic | Depends on | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Loudness** | **Amplitude** | Measured in **decibels (dB)**. Normal talk ≈ 60 dB; noise above ~85 dB for long periods can damage hearing; 120 dB+ causes pain |
| **Pitch** (shrillness) | **Frequency** | Women's and children's voices have higher pitch than men's; a mosquito's buzz has high pitch, a lion's roar low pitch but high loudness |
| **Quality / timbre** | Waveform (overtones) | Lets us recognise a friend's voice or tell a sitar from a flute playing the same note |

### Range of frequencies

| Range | Frequency | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Infrasonic** | Below 20 Hz | Produced by earthquakes, volcanoes, whales, elephants; some animals sense it before earthquakes |
| **Audible** | 20 Hz – 20,000 Hz (20 kHz) | Normal human hearing |
| **Ultrasonic** | Above 20 kHz | **Bats** (echolocation), dolphins, dog whistles |

**Uses of ultrasound**: **SONAR** (Sound Navigation and Ranging) to find the depth of the sea, submarines and fish shoals; **ultrasonography** to see babies in the womb and internal organs (safe, no radiation); breaking kidney stones (lithotripsy); cleaning delicate parts; detecting cracks in metals. **Echocardiography** uses ultrasound to image the heart.

> **Common mistake:** Ultrasound is used for checking pregnancy, but **sex-determination tests are illegal in India** under the **PCPNDT Act, 1994** (Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act). A link worth using in a Mains answer on technology and society.

### Echo and reverberation

- An **echo** is a reflected sound heard separately. The sensation of sound stays in our ear for about **0.1 s**, so to hear a distinct echo the reflecting surface must be at least about **17 m** away (sound covers 34 m in 0.1 s — going and returning).
- **Reverberation** is the persistence of sound due to many reflections. Cinema halls and auditoriums use **sound-absorbing materials** (curtains, fibreboard, carpets) to reduce it.

### Doppler effect

The pitch (frequency) of sound seems to **rise when the source comes towards you** and **fall when it goes away** — e.g. a train whistle or ambulance siren. The same effect with light is used by astronomers (**red shift** shows galaxies moving away — evidence of the expanding universe) and with radio waves by **police speed guns** and **Doppler weather radars**.

### Musical instruments and resonance

- **Resonance**: when a body is made to vibrate at its natural frequency, it vibrates with large amplitude. Soldiers **break step while crossing a bridge** so their marching rhythm does not cause dangerous resonance. Tuning a radio is also resonance.
- Instrument types: **string** (sitar, veena, violin, guitar — pitch rises if the string is tighter or shorter), **wind** (flute, shehnai — **Ustad Bismillah Khan**, born in **Dumraon, Buxar, Bihar**, made the shehnai world-famous), **percussion** (tabla, drum, dholak).

### Mains questions

**1. Lightning has become one of the deadliest natural hazards in Bihar. Explain the science of lightning and suggest technology-based measures to reduce deaths.** (about 300 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Bihar is among the top states for lightning deaths; most victims are farmers and labourers in open fields during pre-monsoon and monsoon months.
- **Science:** charge separation in cumulonimbus clouds; discharge to the ground; light is seen before thunder (speed difference); tall isolated objects attract strikes.
- **Early warning:** Bihar's Indra Vajra app; IMD and IITM lightning-location networks; SMS alerts; "30-30 rule".
- **Infrastructure:** lightning arresters (conductors) on schools, panchayat buildings, health centres; lightning shelters in fields.
- **Awareness:** crouch position, avoid trees and water bodies, jeevika/self-help groups and school campaigns.
- **Response:** ex-gratia compensation, trained first responders (CPR), declaring lightning a state-specific disaster.
- **Conclusion:** cheap physics-based measures plus mobile technology can cut deaths sharply; integrate with the State Disaster Management Plan.

**2. Explain the role of optics and acoustics (light and sound technologies) in modern medicine and communication.** (about 250 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** light and sound waves are the base of many everyday technologies.
- **Medicine:** X-ray, CT, ultrasonography and echocardiography, endoscopy via optical fibres, laser eye surgery (LASIK), cataract lens implants, lithotripsy.
- **Communication:** optical fibre internet (BharatNet to gram panchayats), satellite microwaves, mobile networks.
- **Society:** PCPNDT Act to prevent misuse of ultrasound; cost and access in rural Bihar; tele-radiology.
- **Conclusion:** affordable diagnostics and fibre connectivity are key for health equity.
