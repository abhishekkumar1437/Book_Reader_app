This chapter opens the biology part. It covers how living things are classified, the cell (the unit of life), how cells divide, how traits pass from parents to children (genetics), and how life evolved. BPSC asks "powerhouse of the cell", "who discovered the cell / nucleus / DNA structure", "Mendel worked on which plant", "which disorder is sex-linked" and evolution theory names. These ideas also support the biotechnology chapter later.

> **In simple words:** Every living thing is made of cells — tiny rooms with walls, a control centre (nucleus) and small machines (organelles). Instructions for building the body are written in DNA, in a four-letter code. Children get half their DNA from each parent, which is why they resemble both. Over millions of years, small changes in DNA plus natural selection created all the variety of life.

## Classification of living things

**Carolus Linnaeus** (Sweden, 18th century) is the **father of taxonomy**. He gave **binomial nomenclature** — two-part Latin names: **genus** (capital letter) + **species** (small letter), printed in italics. Example: humans — *Homo sapiens*; tiger — *Panthera tigris*; mango — *Mangifera indica*; rice — *Oryza sativa*; wheat — *Triticum aestivum*; frog — *Rana tigrina*; house fly — *Musca domestica*; peepal — *Ficus religiosa*; litchi — *Litchi chinensis*; makhana — *Euryale ferox*.

**Hierarchy (big → small)**: Kingdom → Phylum (Division in plants) → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.

> **Memory trick:** "**K**ing **P**hilip **C**ame **O**ver **F**or **G**ood **S**oup."

### Five-kingdom classification (R. H. Whittaker, 1969)

| Kingdom | Cell type | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monera** | **Prokaryotic** (no true nucleus) | Single-celled | **Bacteria**, **cyanobacteria (blue-green algae)** |
| **Protista** | Eukaryotic | Mostly single-celled | **Amoeba**, **Paramecium**, **Euglena**, *Plasmodium* (malaria), diatoms |
| **Fungi** | Eukaryotic | No chlorophyll; cell wall of **chitin**; absorb food (saprophytes) | Mushroom, yeast, *Penicillium*, bread mould (*Rhizopus*) |
| **Plantae** | Eukaryotic | Multicellular, chlorophyll, cellulose cell wall | Mosses, ferns, pine, flowering plants |
| **Animalia** | Eukaryotic | Multicellular, no cell wall, mostly move | Sponges to mammals |

- **Viruses** are **not** in any kingdom. They are **non-cellular**, have DNA *or* RNA inside a protein coat, and **reproduce only inside living cells** (so they are on the border of living and non-living). Discovered through work on **tobacco mosaic virus** (Dmitri Ivanovsky, 1892). **Viroids** (only RNA, no coat) and **prions** (only protein — cause mad cow disease) are even simpler.
- **Carl Woese** later proposed the **three-domain system**: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
- **Lichens** are a partnership (**symbiosis**) of **algae and fungi**; they are **indicators of air pollution** (they do not grow in SO₂-polluted air).

## The cell — basic unit of life

| Discovery | Scientist | Year |
|---|---|---|
| **Cell** (in cork; dead cells) | **Robert Hooke** (book *Micrographia*) | **1665** |
| Living cells (bacteria, protozoa) | **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** | 1670s |
| **Nucleus** | **Robert Brown** | 1831 |
| **Cell theory** — all living things are made of cells | **Schleiden** (plants, 1838) and **Schwann** (animals, 1839) | 1838–39 |
| "All cells arise from pre-existing cells" (*omnis cellula e cellula*) | **Rudolf Virchow** | 1855 |
| Protoplasm named | Purkinje | 1839 |

- The **smallest cell**: **Mycoplasma** (PPLO). The **largest cell**: **ostrich egg** (yolk). The **longest cell** in the human body: **nerve cell (neuron)**. Smallest human cell: often given as **sperm** (by volume; some sources say RBC/cerebellar granule cell); largest human cell: **ovum** (egg cell).
- **Unicellular** organisms: Amoeba, Paramecium, bacteria, yeast. **Multicellular**: plants and animals.

### Prokaryotic vs eukaryotic cells

| Feature | Prokaryotic | Eukaryotic |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | No true nucleus (DNA in a "nucleoid") | True nucleus with nuclear membrane |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Present (mitochondria, ER, Golgi…) |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 80S (70S inside mitochondria and chloroplasts) |
| Size | Small (1–10 µm) | Larger (10–100 µm) |
| Examples | Bacteria, cyanobacteria | Plants, animals, fungi, protists |

> **Prelims angle:** "Which organelle is called the powerhouse?" (mitochondria), "suicide bag?" (lysosome), "Which organelles have their own DNA?" (mitochondria and chloroplasts), "Which is absent in animal cells?" (cell wall, chloroplast, large vacuole). Also: "Which cell organelle is absent in a mature human RBC?" — **nucleus** (and mitochondria).

### Osmosis and diffusion

- **Diffusion**: movement of molecules from **high to low concentration** (smell of perfume, oxygen entering blood in lungs).
- **Osmosis**: movement of **water** through a **semi-permeable membrane** from a dilute solution to a concentrated one.
  - Raisins (kishmish) **swell** in water (endosmosis).
  - Salting pickles or sprinkling salt on leeches draws water out (exosmosis).
  - **Plasmolysis**: a plant cell shrinks when kept in strong salt solution — why excess fertiliser "burns" plants.
  - Wilted vegetables become fresh when kept in water.
- **Reverse osmosis (RO)**: applying pressure to push water from salty side to pure side — used in **RO water purifiers** and desalination plants.

| Biomolecule | Units | Role |

## Cell division

| Feature | **Mitosis** | **Meiosis** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | **Body (somatic) cells** — growth, repair | **Reproductive organs** — to make **gametes** (sperm, egg) |
| Number of divisions | One | Two (Meiosis I and II) |
| Daughter cells | **2**, identical to parent | **4**, genetically different |
| Chromosome number | Stays the same (**diploid, 2n**; 46 in humans) | **Halved** (**haploid, n**; 23 in humans) |
| Crossing over | No | **Yes** — creates variation |
| Called | Equational division | Reduction division |

Mitosis stages: **Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase** (then cytokinesis). Memory trick: "**PMAT**". The resting stage between divisions is **interphase** (DNA copies itself).

**Cancer** is uncontrolled mitosis — cells divide without stopping and may spread (**metastasis**). Benign tumours stay in one place; malignant tumours spread.

## DNA, RNA and genes

### DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)

- DNA is the **genetic material** in almost all organisms (in some viruses it is RNA).
- **Double helix** structure proposed by **James Watson and Francis Crick (1953)**, using the **X-ray photographs of Rosalind Franklin** (and Maurice Wilkins). Watson, Crick and Wilkins got the **Nobel Prize in 1962** (Franklin had died in 1958).
- Each strand is a chain of **nucleotides**: sugar (**deoxyribose**) + phosphate + a **nitrogen base**.
- Four bases: **Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Guanine (G), Cytosine (C)**. **A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds); G pairs with C (3 hydrogen bonds)** — **Chargaff's rule**: A = T and G = C.
- **Purines**: A, G (two rings). **Pyrimidines**: C, T, U (one ring).
- DNA was first isolated by **Friedrich Miescher (1869)**, who called it "nuclein".
- Human DNA in one cell, stretched out, is about **2 metres** long.
- **DNA fingerprinting** (Alec Jeffreys, 1984) identifies people from DNA patterns — used in crime investigation and paternity disputes. In India, **Dr Lalji Singh** (born in Jaunpur, UP) pioneered DNA fingerprinting at CCMB Hyderabad and is called the **"father of DNA fingerprinting in India"**. **CDFD** (Centre for DNA Fingerprinting and Diagnostics) is in Hyderabad.

### RNA (ribonucleic acid)

| DNA | RNA |
|---|---|
| Double-stranded | Usually single-stranded |
| Sugar: deoxyribose | Sugar: ribose |
| Bases A, T, G, C | Bases A, **U (uracil)**, G, C — **uracil replaces thymine** |
| Stores genetic information | Helps make proteins (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA) |
| Mostly in nucleus | Nucleus and cytoplasm |

**Central dogma** (Francis Crick): **DNA → (transcription) → RNA → (translation) → protein**. Some viruses (**retroviruses** like **HIV**) use the enzyme **reverse transcriptase** to make DNA from RNA.

**Genetic code**: three bases (a **codon**) code for one amino acid. **Har Gobind Khorana** (born in Raipur, Punjab — now in Pakistan) shared the **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1968** with Nirenberg and Holley for **deciphering the genetic code**; he also made the **first artificial gene** (1970s).

### Genes and chromosomes

- A **gene** is a segment of DNA that carries the code for one trait/protein. The word "gene" was coined by **Wilhelm Johannsen** (1909); **Mendel** called them "factors".
- **Chromosomes** are thread-like structures of DNA wound around proteins (histones), seen during cell division. Word coined by **Waldeyer**. **T. H. Morgan** (fruit-fly experiments) showed genes are on chromosomes.
- **Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs)**: **22 pairs of autosomes + 1 pair of sex chromosomes**. **Female: XX; male: XY**.
- **Human Genome Project** (1990–2003) mapped the human genome: about **3 billion base pairs** and roughly **20,000–25,000 genes**. India's **Genome India Project** (DBT, 2020) sequenced **10,000 genomes** of Indians from diverse groups (completed and data released around 2024–25 — check latest).

## Mendel and the laws of inheritance

**Gregor Johann Mendel** (Austrian monk, **father of genetics**) worked on the **garden pea (*Pisum sativum*)** and published his results in **1865–66**. His work was ignored until it was **rediscovered in 1900** by **de Vries, Correns and Tschermak**. He chose pea because it has clear contrasting traits, short life cycle and self-pollinates. He studied **7 pairs of contrasting traits** (tall/dwarf stem, round/wrinkled seed, yellow/green seed, etc.).

### Key words

- **Dominant**: trait that shows even if only one copy is present (T for tall).
- **Recessive**: trait that shows only when both copies are recessive (t for dwarf).
- **Genotype**: the genetic make-up (TT, Tt, tt). **Phenotype**: the visible trait (tall or dwarf).
- **Homozygous**: TT or tt (same). **Heterozygous**: Tt (different).
- **Allele**: alternative forms of a gene (T and t).

- **Monohybrid** F2 ratio: **3 : 1** (phenotype), **1 : 2 : 1** (genotype).
- **Dihybrid** cross (two traits, e.g. round-yellow × wrinkled-green) F2 ratio: **9 : 3 : 3 : 1**.
- **Test cross**: crossing with the recessive parent (tt) to find out if a tall plant is TT or Tt.

### Mendel's laws

1. **Law of dominance**: in a heterozygote, one allele (dominant) hides the other (recessive).
2. **Law of segregation** (law of purity of gametes): the two alleles separate during gamete formation; each gamete gets only one.
3. **Law of independent assortment**: genes for different traits are inherited independently (seen in the dihybrid cross; true for genes on different chromosomes).

Exceptions found later: **incomplete dominance** (red × white **snapdragon/four o'clock (Mirabilis)** → **pink**, ratio 1:2:1), **co-dominance** (both expressed — **AB blood group**), **multiple alleles** (ABO blood groups have three alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i), **linkage** (genes close together on a chromosome are inherited together — Morgan).

## Sex determination

- In humans the **mother always gives an X** chromosome; the **father gives either X or Y**. **XX → girl; XY → boy**. So the **father's sperm decides the sex of the child** — a fact that should be used to fight the social stigma against mothers for having daughters.
- The chance of a boy or a girl in each birth is **50 : 50**.
- In birds, the female decides (ZW female, ZZ male). In some reptiles (turtles, crocodiles), the **temperature of incubation** decides sex. In honeybees, males (drones) come from unfertilised eggs (haploid).

> **Mains angle:** In answers on the **declining child sex ratio** and **Beti Bachao Beti Padhao**, a line like "Science shows the father's Y chromosome decides a child's sex, yet women are blamed" shows awareness. Also mention the **PCPNDT Act, 1994** banning sex-selection.

## Genetic disorders

| Disorder | Type | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Haemophilia** | **X-linked recessive** | Blood does not clot ("bleeder's disease"); "**royal disease**" — spread through Queen Victoria's descendants; mostly males affected, females usually carriers |
| **Colour blindness** (red–green) | **X-linked recessive** | More common in males (~8% of men vs <1% of women) |
| **Sickle-cell anaemia** | Autosomal recessive | Abnormal haemoglobin; RBCs become sickle-shaped; carriers resist **malaria**; common in tribal communities of central India — **National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission** launched **July 2023** (target 2047) |
| **Thalassaemia** | Autosomal recessive | Low or defective haemoglobin; needs regular blood transfusion |
| Phenylketonuria (PKU) | Autosomal recessive | Cannot break down phenylalanine; mental retardation if untreated |
| Albinism | Autosomal recessive | No melanin pigment |
| Cystic fibrosis | Autosomal recessive | Thick mucus in lungs |
| Huntington's disease | Autosomal dominant | Brain degeneration |
| **Down syndrome** | **Trisomy 21** (an extra chromosome 21; total **47**) | Mental retardation, distinct facial features; risk rises with mother's age |
| **Turner syndrome** | **XO** (45 chromosomes) | Females, sterile, short stature |
| **Klinefelter syndrome** | **XXY** (47) | Males, sterile, some female characteristics |
| Patau syndrome | Trisomy 13 | Severe |
| Edwards syndrome | Trisomy 18 | Severe |

**Y-linked** traits pass only from father to son (e.g. **hypertrichosis of the ear** — hairy ears).

> **Memory trick:** "**Hemo-C**olour" — **H**aemophilia and **C**olour blindness are the two famous **X-linked** disorders; they affect **men** more because men have only one X.

## Origin of life

- **Spontaneous generation** (life from non-living matter like mud) was disproved by **Francesco Redi** (meat and flies) and finally by **Louis Pasteur** (swan-neck flask experiment, 1860s).
- **Oparin–Haldane hypothesis** (1920s): life arose from simple chemicals in the **primitive atmosphere** (no free oxygen; methane, ammonia, hydrogen, water vapour) by "chemical evolution" in a "**primordial soup**".
- **Miller–Urey experiment (1953)**: passing electric sparks through CH₄, NH₃, H₂ and water vapour produced **amino acids** — support for chemical evolution.
- The Earth is about **4.5–4.6 billion years** old; the earliest life (bacteria-like) appeared about **3.5–3.8 billion years ago**. **Cyanobacteria** produced the first oxygen in the atmosphere.

## Theories of evolution

**Evolution** is the gradual change in living things over many generations, producing new species.

| Theory | Scientist | Main idea | Example used |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inheritance of acquired characters (use and disuse)** | **Jean-Baptiste Lamarck** (book *Philosophie Zoologique*, 1809) | Organs used more grow stronger, unused ones weaken, and these acquired changes are passed on | **Giraffe's long neck** from stretching for leaves; disproved by **August Weismann** (cut mouse tails for many generations — babies still had tails) |
| **Natural selection** ("survival of the fittest") | **Charles Darwin** (*On the Origin of Species*, **1859**); **Alfred Russel Wallace** reached the same idea independently | More offspring are born than can survive; there is variation; individuals with useful variations survive and reproduce more | **Darwin's finches** of the **Galápagos Islands** (voyage on **HMS Beagle**, 1831–36); "survival of the fittest" phrase was coined by **Herbert Spencer** |
| **Mutation theory** | **Hugo de Vries** | New species arise by sudden large changes (**mutations**) | Evening primrose (*Oenothera*) |
| **Modern synthetic theory** | Dobzhansky, Huxley, others | Combines Darwin + Mendel's genetics + mutation + population genetics | — |

**Industrial melanism** — the classic modern example of natural selection: in England, the **peppered moth** became mostly **dark-coloured** after the Industrial Revolution blackened tree trunks with soot (dark moths were hidden from birds); after pollution control, light moths came back.

Another modern example: **antibiotic-resistant bacteria** and **pesticide-resistant insects** — the few resistant ones survive and multiply.

### Evidences of evolution

| Evidence | Example |
|---|---|
| **Homologous organs** — same basic structure, **different function** (divergent evolution) | Human arm, whale flipper, bat wing, cat's foreleg |
| **Analogous organs** — different structure, **same function** (convergent evolution) | Wings of a bird, bat and insect; eye of octopus and human |
| **Vestigial organs** — reduced, no clear use in humans | **Appendix (vermiform)**, **wisdom teeth**, **coccyx (tail bone)**, nictitating membrane (plica semilunaris), ear muscles, body hair |
| **Fossils** | ***Archaeopteryx*** — a "**connecting link**" between **reptiles and birds** (had feathers and teeth) |
| Connecting links | ***Peripatus*** (annelids–arthropods), **duck-billed platypus** (egg-laying mammal — reptiles and mammals), lungfish (fish–amphibians) |
| Embryology | Early embryos of fish, bird and human look similar (gill slits) |
| Molecular biology | Similar DNA and proteins (humans and chimps share about 98–99% of DNA) |

**Fossils** are dated using **radioactive carbon (C-14)** for younger remains and uranium/potassium methods for older ones. The study of fossils is **palaeontology**. India has dinosaur fossil sites in Gujarat (Balasinor), MP (Dhar) and Tamil Nadu (Ariyalur).

### Human evolution (simplified sequence)

*Dryopithecus* and *Ramapithecus* (ape-like, ~15 million years ago; *Ramapithecus* fossils were found in the **Shivalik hills**) → ***Australopithecus*** (Africa; "Lucy", ~3.2 million years ago; walked upright) → ***Homo habilis*** ("handy man" — first stone tools) → ***Homo erectus*** (upright, used fire; Java man, Peking man) → ***Homo neanderthalensis*** (Neanderthals; buried their dead) → ***Homo sapiens*** (modern humans; arose in **Africa** about **3 lakh (300,000) years ago**). **Cro-Magnon** were early modern humans in Europe (cave paintings).

> **Remember:** **Svante Pääbo** won the **Nobel Prize in Medicine 2022** for sequencing the **Neanderthal genome**, showing that modern non-African humans carry some Neanderthal DNA.

### Mains questions

**1. "Genetics can be used to fight both disease and social prejudice." Discuss with reference to sickle-cell anaemia, thalassaemia and sex determination.** (about 300 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** advances in genetics since Mendel and the DNA double helix let us understand inherited disease and bust myths.
- **Inherited diseases:** sickle-cell (National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission 2023 — screening of crores of people in tribal areas), thalassaemia (premarital and antenatal screening, transfusion and bone-marrow transplant support).
- **Tools:** genetic counselling, newborn screening, carrier testing, gene therapy (CRISPR-based therapies approved abroad — check latest), Genome India data.
- **Social prejudice:** the father's chromosome decides a child's sex — evidence against blaming mothers; link to child sex ratio, Beti Bachao Beti Padhao, PCPNDT Act enforcement.
- **Ethical issues:** genetic privacy, discrimination, misuse for sex selection.
- **Bihar angle:** need for thalassaemia day-care centres in district hospitals, awareness via ASHA workers.
- **Conclusion:** science-backed public health and awareness can reduce both disease burden and social bias.

**2. Explain Darwin's theory of natural selection. How does it help us understand the rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR)?** (about 250 words)

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Darwin (1859); variation, overproduction, struggle, survival of the fittest.
- **Evidence:** finches, peppered moth, homologous organs, fossils.
- **AMR as evolution in action:** antibiotics kill sensitive bacteria; resistant mutants survive and multiply; misuse (incomplete courses, over-the-counter sales, use in poultry) speeds this.
- **Indian response:** National Action Plan on AMR (2017), Red Line campaign, Kerala's KARSAP as a state model; surveillance labs.
- **Conclusion:** understanding evolution is vital to protect the effectiveness of medicines.
