This chapter is the "map" of the Constitution. You learn what the Preamble says, what makes our Constitution special, where its ideas came from, and how it is arranged in Parts and Schedules. You also cover Articles 1–11 (the Union, new states, citizenship). BPSC loves questions like "Which Schedule deals with anti-defection?", "From which country was the concept of DPSP borrowed?" and "Which words were added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment?".

> **In simple words:** The Preamble is the introduction — it tells us who made the Constitution (the people), what kind of state India is (sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic), and what it wants for citizens (justice, liberty, equality, fraternity). The rest of the Constitution is divided into Parts (chapters) and Schedules (lists and tables at the end).

## The Preamble

### Text (as amended)

> WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a **SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC** and to secure to all its citizens:
> **JUSTICE**, social, economic and political;
> **LIBERTY** of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship;
> **EQUALITY** of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all
> **FRATERNITY** assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;
> IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.

### Four parts of the Preamble

| Component | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| **Source of authority** | "We, the people of India" — power comes from the people |
| **Nature of the Indian state** | Sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic, republic |
| **Objectives** | Justice, liberty, equality, fraternity |
| **Date of adoption** | 26 November 1949 |

### Key words explained

- **Sovereign** — India is free from outside control; it can make its own decisions inside and outside. Membership of the Commonwealth or the UN does not reduce sovereignty.
- **Socialist** — added by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**. Indian socialism is **democratic socialism** (mixed economy, public and private sectors together), not communist state ownership. The Supreme Court has called it a blend of Marxism and Gandhism leaning towards Gandhian socialism.
- **Secular** — added by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**. Indian secularism is **positive secularism**: the state has no religion of its own, treats all religions equally, and can reform religious practices. Secularism was declared part of the **basic structure** in **S.R. Bommai (1994)**.
- **Democratic** — power with the people through elections; India follows **representative, parliamentary** democracy. Includes political, social and economic democracy.
- **Republic** — the head of state (President) is **elected**, not hereditary. Every public office is open to every citizen.
- **Justice** — **social, economic and political** — this idea is taken from the **Russian Revolution (1917)**.
- **Liberty, equality and fraternity** — taken from the **French Revolution (1789)**.
- **Fraternity** — a sense of brotherhood. The words "**unity and integrity of the Nation**" — "**integrity**" was added by the 42nd Amendment.

> **Memory trick:** The 42nd Amendment added three words: "**S**ocialist, **S**ecular, **I**ntegrity" — "**SSI**". It is the **only time the Preamble has been amended** (so far).

### Is the Preamble a part of the Constitution?

| Case | Year | Held |
|---|---|---|
| **Berubari Union case** (a Presidential reference) | 1960 | Preamble is **not** a part of the Constitution; but it is a key to the minds of the makers |
| **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala** | 1973 | Preamble **is a part** of the Constitution; it can be amended, but the **basic structure** in it cannot be destroyed |
| **LIC of India case** | 1995 | Again held the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution |
| **Dr Balram Singh v Union of India** | 2024 (25 Nov) | Dismissed petitions challenging the words "socialist" and "secular" added in 1976; Parliament's amending power extends to the Preamble |

Other points:
- The Preamble is **not enforceable in a court** (non-justiciable).
- It is **neither a source of power** to the legislature **nor a limit** on its powers.
- It was based on the **Objectives Resolution** of Nehru. It was enacted **last**, after the rest of the Constitution, so that it matched the final text.
- N.A. Palkhivala called it the "**identity card of the Constitution**". K.M. Munshi called it the "horoscope of our sovereign democratic republic". Thakurdas Bhargava called it "the soul of the Constitution". Ernest Barker praised it as the "key-note" of the Constitution.

> **Prelims angle:** Traps: (1) Preamble is **amendable** (Kesavananda). (2) It was amended **only once** (42nd, 1976). (3) The word "**fraternity**" is in the Preamble; "**federal**" is not. (4) "Justice" comes from the Russian Revolution; "liberty, equality, fraternity" from the French Revolution; the idea of a preamble itself from the **USA**. (5) The order is "Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic".

## Salient features of the Constitution

### 1. Lengthiest written constitution

- Originally **395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules**. Today about **470 Articles in 25 Parts with 12 Schedules** (Article numbers stay at 395 because new articles are inserted as 21A, 243A etc.).
- Reasons for length: geography and diversity; the influence of the GoI Act 1935 (itself bulky); a single constitution for the Centre and the states (except J&K earlier); domination of legal experts; detailed provisions on administration, SC/ST, official language etc.

### 2. Drawn from various sources

<div class="formula">

| Source | Features borrowed |
|---|---|
| **Government of India Act 1935** | Federal scheme, office of Governor, judiciary, Public Service Commissions, emergency provisions, administrative details |
| **British Constitution** | Parliamentary government, rule of law, legislative procedure, single citizenship, cabinet system, prerogative writs, parliamentary privileges, bicameralism |
| **US Constitution** | Fundamental Rights, independence of judiciary, judicial review, impeachment of the President, removal of SC and HC judges, post of Vice-President |
| **Irish Constitution** | Directive Principles of State Policy, nomination of members to Rajya Sabha, method of election of the President |
| **Canadian Constitution** | Federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with the Centre, appointment of state Governors by the Centre, advisory jurisdiction of the Supreme Court |
| **Australian Constitution** | Concurrent List, freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse, joint sitting of the two Houses |
| **Weimar Constitution (Germany)** | Suspension of Fundamental Rights during emergency |
| **Soviet (USSR) Constitution** | Fundamental Duties, ideals of justice (social, economic, political) in the Preamble |
| **French Constitution** | Republic; ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity in the Preamble |
| **South African Constitution** | Procedure for amendment of the Constitution, election of members of Rajya Sabha |
| **Japanese Constitution** | "Procedure established by law" (Article 21) |

</div>

> **Memory trick:** **I**reland gives **D**irections (DPSP) — "**I D**irect". **USSR** gives **D**uties — "Soviet Duties". **US** gives **R**ights — "US Rights". **C**anada gives **C**entre (strong Centre). **A**ustralia gives **C**oncurrent (A-C). **G**ermany **S**uspends (emergency). **S**outh **A**frica **A**mends.

### 3. Blend of rigidity and flexibility

- **Article 368** provides three ways to change the Constitution:
  1. **Simple majority** of Parliament (outside Article 368) — e.g. creation of new states (Article 4), abolition or creation of Legislative Councils (Article 169), citizenship, quorum, salaries.
  2. **Special majority** of Parliament — a majority of the **total membership** of each House **and** two-thirds of members **present and voting**. Used for most provisions, e.g. Fundamental Rights, DPSP.
  3. **Special majority + ratification by half of the state legislatures** (by simple majority) — for federal provisions: election of President, extent of executive power of Union and states, Supreme Court and High Courts, distribution of legislative powers, GST Council, representation of states in Parliament, Article 368 itself.

### 4. Federal system with unitary bias

- **Federal features:** two governments (Union and states), division of powers (Seventh Schedule), written constitution, supremacy of the Constitution, rigid constitution (partly), independent judiciary, bicameral Parliament.
- **Unitary (non-federal) features:** strong Centre, single constitution, single citizenship, flexibility, integrated judiciary, all-India services, emergency provisions, Governors appointed by the Centre, Parliament can change state boundaries (Article 3), Rajya Sabha not equal representation of states.
- **Article 1** calls India a "**Union of States**", not a "federation". Ambedkar explained: the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among states, and states have no right to secede.
- Scholars' descriptions: **K.C. Wheare** — "**quasi-federal**"; **Granville Austin** — "**cooperative federalism**"; **Ivor Jennings** — "a federation with a strong centralising tendency"; **Morris Jones** — "bargaining federalism".

### 5. Parliamentary form of government

- Taken from the British (**Westminster**) model. Features: nominal head (President) and real executive (Council of Ministers), majority party rule, **collective responsibility** of the ministers to the Lok Sabha, membership of ministers in the legislature, leadership of the PM, dissolution of the lower house.
- Difference from Britain: India has a **written constitution** and **judicial review**, so the Indian Parliament is **not sovereign** like the British Parliament. India's system is a **synthesis of parliamentary sovereignty and judicial supremacy**.

### 6. Other features

- **Integrated and independent judiciary** — one Supreme Court at the top, High Courts below, subordinate courts below them; one system enforces both central and state laws.
- **Fundamental Rights** (Part III), **Directive Principles** (Part IV) and **Fundamental Duties** (Part IVA).
- **Secular state**, **universal adult franchise** (voting age 21, lowered to **18 by the 61st Amendment, 1988**, effective 1989).
- **Single citizenship** (unlike the USA's dual citizenship).
- **Independent bodies** — Election Commission, CAG, UPSC, SPSCs.
- **Emergency provisions** (Part XVIII).
- **Three-tier government** — Union, state, and local (73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992).
- **Cooperative societies** — Part IXB (97th Amendment, 2011).

### The 22 languages of the Eighth Schedule

Assamese, Bengali, **Bodo**, **Dogri**, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, **Konkani**, **Maithili**, Malayalam, **Manipuri**, Marathi, **Nepali**, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, **Santhali**, **Sindhi**, Tamil, Telugu, Urdu.

| Amendment | Languages added |
|---|---|
| 21st (1967) | **Sindhi** |
| 71st (1992) | **Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali** |
| 92nd (2003) | **Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali** |

- **Oriya** was renamed **Odia** by the **96th Amendment (2011)**.
- English is **not** in the Eighth Schedule.

> **Bihar connection:** **Maithili**, the language of Mithila (north Bihar — Darbhanga, Madhubani, Sitamarhi, Saharsa), was added to the Eighth Schedule by the **92nd Amendment (2003)**. **Bhojpuri, Magahi, Angika and Bajjika** are **not** in the Eighth Schedule; there are long-standing demands to include Bhojpuri. **Santhali**, added in 2003, is spoken by Santhal tribals of Bihar and Jharkhand. Hindi is the official language of Bihar; **Urdu** is the **second official language of Bihar** (since 1981).

## Part I — The Union and its territory (Articles 1–4)

| Article | Content |
|---|---|
| **1** | "**India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States**." Territory of India = states + Union Territories + territories that may be acquired |
| **2** | Parliament may **admit or establish new states** that are **not** part of India (e.g. Sikkim, 1975 — via 35th and 36th Amendments) |
| **3** | Parliament may by law **form new states, alter areas, boundaries or names** of existing states |
| **4** | Laws under Articles 2 and 3 are **not** amendments under Article 368 (simple majority is enough) |

**Procedure under Article 3:**
- A bill can be introduced only with the **prior recommendation of the President**.
- The President must refer it to the concerned **state legislature** for its views within a time limit.
- The President (and Parliament) is **not bound** by the views of the state legislature.
- The bill is passed by **simple majority**.

> **Bihar connection:** **Jharkhand** was created from Bihar by the **Bihar Reorganisation Act, 2000** (passed under Article 3), with effect from **15 November 2000** (the birth anniversary of Birsa Munda). Chhattisgarh (1 November 2000) and Uttarakhand (9 November 2000) were created the same year. After separation, Bihar kept **38 districts** today and **243** assembly seats; it lost most of its mineral and industrial base to Jharkhand — a theme for Mains questions on Bihar's development.

## Part II — Citizenship (Articles 5–11)

- The Constitution only identified who became a citizen on **26 January 1950** (Articles 5–8). It left later matters to Parliament (**Article 11**).
- **Article 5** — citizenship at the commencement (domicile + birth/parent born/residence 5 years).
- **Article 6** — persons who migrated from Pakistan.
- **Article 7** — persons who migrated to Pakistan and later returned.
- **Article 8** — persons of Indian origin living abroad.
- **Article 9** — a person who voluntarily acquires foreign citizenship is not an Indian citizen (no dual citizenship).
- **Article 10** — continuance of rights of citizenship.
- **Article 11** — Parliament can regulate citizenship by law.

**Citizenship Act, 1955** — five ways to acquire: **birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, incorporation of territory**. Three ways to lose: **renunciation, termination** (on voluntarily acquiring another citizenship), **deprivation** (by government, e.g. fraud).

- **Overseas Citizen of India (OCI)** — introduced in 2005 (it is **not** dual citizenship; no voting rights, cannot hold constitutional posts).
- **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 (CAA)** — Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from **Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan** who entered India on or before **31 December 2014** can get citizenship faster (naturalisation period reduced to 5 years). Rules notified in **March 2024**.
- Rights available **only to citizens**: Articles **15, 16, 19, 29, 30**; right to vote; right to become President, VP, judge of SC/HC, Governor, AG, MP, MLA.

## The basic structure doctrine

- Born in **Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973)** — a **13-judge bench**, decided **7:6**. Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution under Article 368, including Fundamental Rights, but **cannot alter its "basic structure"**.
- The court did not give a closed list. Elements recognised over time:

| Element of basic structure | Case where recognised |
|---|---|
| Supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secular character, separation of powers, federal character | Kesavananda Bharati (1973) |
| Free and fair elections, rule of law | Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975) |
| Limited amending power, harmony between FR and DPSP, judicial review | Minerva Mills (1980) |
| Secularism, federalism | S.R. Bommai (1994) |
| Judicial review (of tribunals' decisions by HCs) | L. Chandra Kumar (1997) |
| Rule of equality, Articles 14, 19, 21 ("golden triangle") | I.R. Coelho (2007) |
| Independence of judiciary | Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record (NJAC case, 2015) |
| Welfare state, sovereignty, unity and integrity, dignity of the individual | Various cases |

> **Mains angle:** The basic structure doctrine is the judiciary's guard against majority governments changing the Constitution's identity. Use it in answers on "parliamentary sovereignty vs judicial supremacy", "amendment procedure", and "Kesavananda Bharati at 50 (2023)". Note the critique: it is judge-made, not written in the text, and gives courts the final word on amendments.

### Mains questions

**1. "The Preamble is the soul of the Indian Constitution." Discuss with reference to its key words and judicial interpretation. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** The Preamble, based on Nehru's Objectives Resolution, states the source, nature and goals of the Constitution.
- **Body:**
  - Source of authority: "We, the people".
  - Nature: sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic, republic — meaning of each.
  - Objectives: justice (social, economic, political), liberty, equality, fraternity, dignity, unity and integrity.
  - Judicial view: Berubari (1960) → Kesavananda (1973) → LIC (1995) → Balram Singh (2024).
  - Use in interpretation of laws; basis of the basic structure.
  - Bihar example: social justice goals behind reservation in panchayats and the 2023 caste-based survey.
- **Conclusion:** Non-justiciable but the guiding light — the "identity card" of the Constitution.

**2. "The Indian Constitution is federal in form but unitary in spirit." Examine. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Article 1 calls India a "Union of States"; scholars call it quasi-federal.
- **Body:** Federal features (dual polity, Seventh Schedule, written constitution, independent judiciary, bicameralism) → Unitary features (strong Centre, Article 3, single citizenship, all-India services, emergency, Governor, Article 249, integrated judiciary, Rajya Sabha not equal) → Trends: cooperative federalism (GST Council, NITI Aayog), competitive federalism; Bihar's demands (special category status, more devolution from Finance Commissions).
- **Conclusion:** A flexible federation — "a federation with a strong centralising tendency" — designed for unity in diversity.

**3. Explain the basic structure doctrine and its significance for Indian democracy. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Kesavananda Bharati (1973) — limit on Parliament's amending power.
- **Body:** Background (Shankari Prasad, Sajjan Singh, Golaknath, 24th Amendment); the judgment; elements added later (Indira Gandhi 1975, Minerva Mills 1980, Bommai 1994, Coelho 2007, NJAC 2015); significance (protects rights, federalism, democracy); criticism (vague, judge-made).
- **Conclusion:** It keeps the Constitution's identity safe while allowing change.
