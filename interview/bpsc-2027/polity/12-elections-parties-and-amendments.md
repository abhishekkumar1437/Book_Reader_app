This chapter brings together three linked topics: **how elections work**, **how political parties are recognised and kept from defecting**, and **how the Constitution itself is amended**. BPSC asks factual questions ("Which amendment lowered the voting age?", "What is the anti-defection Schedule?", "Which amendment introduced GST?") and Mains questions on electoral reforms, criminalisation of politics and "One Nation, One Election". The amendments table here is your one-stop revision sheet.

> **In simple words:** Every adult citizen (18+) can vote. The Election Commission conducts elections under laws made by Parliament (the Representation of the People Acts). Parties are recognised as national or state parties based on votes and seats. The anti-defection law stops legislators from switching parties for gain. The Constitution can be changed by Parliament under Article 368 — so far 106 amendment Acts have been passed (as of 2026).

## The electoral system (Part XV, Articles 324–329)

| Article | Provision |
|---|---|
| **324** | Election Commission — superintendence, direction and control of elections |
| **325** | **One general electoral roll** for each constituency; no one excluded on grounds of religion, race, caste or sex (no separate electorates) |
| **326** | Elections on the basis of **adult suffrage** — every citizen aged **18** or more (lowered from 21 by the **61st Amendment, 1988**, effective 1989) |
| **327** | Parliament can make laws on elections to Parliament and state legislatures (delimitation, rolls, etc.) |
| **328** | State legislatures can make laws on their elections (if Parliament has not) |
| **329** | **Bar on interference by courts** in electoral matters (delimitation, allotment of seats); an election can be challenged **only through an election petition** (to the **High Court**, with appeal to the SC) |

### Election laws

- **Representation of the People Act, 1950** — allocation of seats, delimitation, **qualifications of voters**, preparation of **electoral rolls**.
- **Representation of the People Act, 1951** — **conduct of elections**, qualifications and **disqualifications** of members, **corrupt practices**, election disputes, registration of political parties (Section 29A), election expenses.
- **Delimitation Commission Acts** — commissions in **1952, 1963, 1973 and 2002**. The last (chaired by **Justice Kuldip Singh**) used the **2001 Census** for boundaries (2008); the **number of seats per state** stays frozen on the **1971 Census** until the first census after **2026** (84th Amendment). Delimitation orders **cannot be challenged** in court.

### Key features and facts

- **First general elections:** **1951–52**, conducted by CEC **Sukumar Sen**; about 17 crore voters.
- **First-past-the-post (FPTP)** system for Lok Sabha and Assemblies; **proportional representation (single transferable vote)** for the Rajya Sabha, Legislative Councils, President and Vice-President.
- **EVMs** were first used in **1982** in the **Parur (Kerala)** by-election; used in all Lok Sabha constituencies from **2004**. **VVPAT** (Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail) was first used in **2013** in the **Noksen (Nagaland)** by-election; in all constituencies from **2019**. The SC (*Association for Democratic Reforms, April 2024*) rejected a return to paper ballots and 100% VVPAT counting.
- **NOTA (None of the Above)** — introduced after ***PUCL v Union of India (2013)***; first used in the **2013** Assembly elections. NOTA votes do not change the result even if they are the highest.
- **EPIC (Electors Photo Identity Card)** — introduced in **1993** under CEC **T.N. Seshan**, who is also remembered for strict enforcement of the **Model Code of Conduct**.
- **Election expenditure limit** (2022): up to **₹95 lakh** for a Lok Sabha seat and **₹40 lakh** for an Assembly seat in bigger states like Bihar (check the latest revision).
- **Security deposit:** **₹25,000** for Lok Sabha and **₹10,000** for Assembly (half for SC/ST candidates). It is **forfeited** if the candidate gets less than **one-sixth** of valid votes.
- A candidate can contest from at most **two constituencies** (1996 amendment of the RPA).
- **Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021** — voluntary linking of the voter list with **Aadhaar**; **four qualifying dates** a year (1 January, 1 April, 1 July, 1 October) for new voters; gender-neutral term "spouse" for service voters.
- **Postal ballot** extended to voters aged **85+** and persons with disabilities (home voting).
- **Electoral bonds** (introduced 2018) were **struck down** by a Constitution Bench in ***ADR v Union of India (15 February 2024)*** as violating voters' **right to information** (Article 19(1)(a)); the SBI was ordered to disclose bond data.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar has seen high voter interest and, notably, **women voters turning out in higher proportion than men** in recent Assembly and Lok Sabha elections — a result linked to schemes for women and the prohibition policy. In 2025 the ECI did a **Special Intensive Revision (SIR)** of the rolls in Bihar before the November 2025 Assembly elections (two phases, results on **14 November 2025**). Bihar's early election history also includes notorious **booth capturing** — reportedly first reported at **Rachiyari village (Begusarai) in 1957** — which made central forces, EVMs and webcasting key reforms.

### Criminalisation of politics and judicial reforms

| Case | Year | Held |
|---|---|---|
| ***Union of India v ADR*** | **2002** | Voters have the right to know candidates' **criminal antecedents, assets and education** — affidavits made compulsory |
| ***PUCL v Union of India*** | 2003 / **2013** | Upheld disclosure; NOTA introduced (2013) |
| ***Lily Thomas v Union of India*** | **2013** | Struck down Section 8(4) of the RPA; a legislator convicted with **2+ years** sentence is **disqualified immediately** |
| ***Public Interest Foundation v Union of India*** | **2018** | Candidates and parties must **publicise criminal records** in the media |
| ***Rambabu Singh Thakur v Sunil Arora*** | **2020** | Parties must publish **reasons** for choosing candidates with criminal cases on their websites |
| ***Anoop Baranwal*** | **2023** | On the method of appointing election commissioners |
| ***ADR v Union of India*** (Electoral Bonds) | **2024** | Electoral bond scheme unconstitutional |

### Electoral reform committees

**Tarkunde Committee (1974–75)**, **Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)** — state funding, EVMs; **Vohra Committee (1993)** — criminal-politician nexus; **Indrajit Gupta Committee (1998)** — **state funding** of elections; **Law Commission 170th report (1999)** and **255th report (2015)**; **Ram Nath Kovind High-Level Committee (2023–24)** on **simultaneous elections**.

### One Nation, One Election

- Simultaneous elections were held for Lok Sabha and Assemblies in **1951–52, 1957, 1962 and 1967**; the cycle broke after early dissolutions.
- The **High-Level Committee chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind** (formed **September 2023**, report **March 2024**) recommended simultaneous Lok Sabha and Assembly elections first, and local body elections within 100 days after that.
- The **Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024** and the **Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2024** were introduced in **December 2024** and referred to a **Joint Parliamentary Committee** (status as of 2026 — check the latest).
- Arguments for: lower cost, less policy paralysis due to the Model Code, better governance focus. Against: federalism concerns, national issues overshadowing state issues, constitutional difficulties when a government falls mid-term.

## Political parties

- Parties are **not** mentioned in the original Constitution; the word "**political party**" came in through the **Tenth Schedule (52nd Amendment, 1985)**.
- **Registration** by the ECI under **Section 29A of the RPA 1951**; **recognition** and symbols under the **Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968**.

### Criteria for recognition (Symbols Order, as amended)

**National party** — any **one** of:
1. Secures **6% of valid votes** in **four or more states** in a Lok Sabha or Assembly election **and** wins **4 Lok Sabha seats** from any state(s); or
2. Wins **2% of Lok Sabha seats** (at least **11** seats) from **at least three states**; or
3. Is recognised as a **state party in four or more states**.

**State party** — any **one** of:
1. **6% of valid votes** in the state Assembly election **and 2 Assembly seats**; or
2. **6% of valid votes** in the state in the Lok Sabha election **and 1 Lok Sabha seat**; or
3. **3% of Assembly seats** or **3 seats**, whichever is more; or
4. **1 Lok Sabha seat for every 25** seats allotted to the state; or
5. **8% of total valid votes** in the state in the last Lok Sabha or Assembly election (liberalised criterion).

- **National parties (as of 2026 — check the latest):** **BJP, Indian National Congress, BSP, CPI(M), Aam Aadmi Party** (national since **April 2023**) and **National People's Party (NPP)**. In April 2023 the ECI withdrew national status from the **TMC, NCP and CPI**.
- Benefits of recognition: reserved symbol, free airtime on public broadcasters, 40 star campaigners (national/state parties), copies of electoral rolls, one proposer for nomination.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar's major parties (as of 2026 — check the latest status) include the **BJP**, **Janata Dal (United)** (founded 2003; led for long by Nitish Kumar), **Rashtriya Janata Dal** (founded 1997 by Lalu Prasad), the **Congress**, **Lok Janshakti Party (Ram Vilas)**, **Hindustani Awam Morcha (Secular)**, **CPI(ML) Liberation**, **Vikassheel Insaan Party** and **Jan Suraaj** (launched in **2024** by Prashant Kishor). Bihar's politics has been shaped by the JP Movement (1974), the Mandal era (1990), coalition shifts between the NDA and the Mahagathbandhan, and the 2023 caste-based survey.

## Anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule)

- Added by the **52nd Amendment Act, 1985** (Rajiv Gandhi government). It amended Articles **101, 102, 190 and 191**. The background was the "**Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram**" politics of the late 1960s (a Haryana MLA, Gaya Lal, changed parties three times in a day in 1967).

### Grounds for disqualification

| Member | Disqualified if |
|---|---|
| Member of a political party | (a) **Voluntarily gives up** membership of the party; or (b) **votes or abstains** contrary to the party's direction (whip) without prior permission, and the act is **not condoned** by the party within **15 days** |
| Independent member | **Joins any political party** after the election |
| Nominated member | **Joins a political party after six months** from taking his seat (can join within six months) |

### Exceptions

- **Merger** — no disqualification if the original party merges with another party and at least **two-thirds** of the members of the **legislature party** agree to the merger (Paragraph 4).
- The earlier exception for a **split** by **one-third** of members was **deleted** by the **91st Amendment (2003)**.
- A person elected as **presiding officer** can leave his party and rejoin after leaving the office.

### Deciding authority and cases

- Decided by the **presiding officer** (Speaker/Chairman). The original Paragraph 7 barred courts.
- ***Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu (1992)*** — upheld the Tenth Schedule but struck down Paragraph 7; the presiding officer acts as a **tribunal** and his decision is subject to **judicial review** (on grounds like mala fides, perversity, violation of natural justice).
- ***Ravi S. Naik (1994)*** — "voluntarily giving up membership" can be **inferred from conduct**, not only formal resignation.
- ***Keisham Meghachandra Singh (2020)*** — Speakers should decide disqualification petitions within **three months**; suggested Parliament consider an **independent tribunal**.
- ***Subhash Desai v Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra (2023)*** — the political party (not just the legislature party) issues the whip; the Governor should not use floor tests to settle intra-party disputes.

> **Mains angle:** Strengths — stability, curbs horse-trading. Weaknesses — curbs **free speech** of legislators (whips on every vote), Speaker's **partisanship and delays**, the **merger loophole** (mass "mergers" of two-thirds), resignations to trigger by-elections. Reforms — decision by the **President/Governor on ECI advice** (Dinesh Goswami Committee, Law Commission 170th report), whips only on confidence motions and money bills, time limit for decisions, independent tribunal.

## Amendment of the Constitution (Article 368, Part XX)

### Procedure

1. An amendment bill can be introduced in **either House** of Parliament (**not** in state legislatures).
2. By a **minister or a private member**; **no prior permission of the President** is needed.
3. Passed in **each House separately** by a **special majority** — a **majority of the total membership** of the House **and** **two-thirds of members present and voting**.
4. **No joint sitting** in case of disagreement — the bill fails.
5. If it affects **federal provisions**, it must also be **ratified by the legislatures of half of the states** by a **simple majority** (no time limit for ratification).
6. Then presented to the **President**, who **must give assent** (**24th Amendment, 1971**) — he cannot withhold it or return it.

**Federal provisions needing state ratification:** election of the President and its manner (54, 55); extent of executive power of the Union and states (73, 162); **Supreme Court and High Courts**; distribution of legislative powers (Seventh Schedule, Articles 245–255); **GST Council** (279A); representation of states in Parliament; **Article 368** itself.

**Changes by simple majority (not "amendments" under 368):** admission/creation of new states (Articles 2, 3, 4); creation or abolition of Legislative Councils (169); Second Schedule (salaries); quorum; privileges; citizenship (Article 11 laws); use of English in Parliament; Fifth and Sixth Schedules; delimitation; elections to Parliament and state legislatures; number of SC judges; official language.

> **Remember:** The amending power is limited by the **basic structure doctrine** (*Kesavananda Bharati, 1973*). Amendments that destroy the basic structure are struck down — e.g. parts of the **39th** (Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain, 1975), the **42nd** (Minerva Mills, 1980), and the **99th** (NJAC, 2015).

### Amendment bills in the news (as of 2026 — check the latest)

- **Bill numbers differ from Act numbers.** For example, the 106th Amendment **Act** was passed as the **Constitution (128th Amendment) Bill, 2023**. Many bills lapse or fail, so bill numbers run ahead.
- **Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024** — **simultaneous elections**; referred to a **JPC**.
- **Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025** — introduced in **August 2025**; provides for the **removal of the PM, a CM or a minister** who is **arrested and detained for 30 consecutive days** on charges carrying a sentence of 5 years or more; referred to a **JPC**.
- **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026** — introduced in **April 2026** to allow women's reservation to start soon after a new delimitation; it was **reported defeated in the Lok Sabha (17 April 2026)** for want of the two-thirds majority (verify against official Lok Sabha records). As a result, the **106th Amendment's timeline** (after census and delimitation) continues.
- As of 2026, **no 107th Amendment Act** has been reported (check the latest).

> **Bihar connection:** For women's reservation answers, cite Bihar as a forerunner: **50% for women in panchayats (2006)** and **urban bodies (2007)**, **35% reservation for women in state government jobs** (since 2016; later limited to women domiciled in Bihar, 2025 — check the latest), and women's self-help groups (**JEEViKA**). The 106th Amendment will add **one-third** women MLAs in the 243-member Assembly (about **81** seats) once it comes into effect.

## Key terms

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Adult suffrage | Right of every adult citizen to vote |
| Delimitation | Fixing the boundaries and number of constituencies |
| FPTP | Candidate with the most votes wins, even without a majority |
| VVPAT | Paper slip that shows the voter whom the EVM recorded the vote for |
| Model Code of Conduct | ECI's rules for parties and governments during elections |
| Whip | Party's instruction to its legislators on how to vote |
| Defection | Leaving one's party or voting against its whip |
| Merger (Tenth Schedule) | Joining of parties supported by two-thirds of a legislature party |
| Special majority | Majority of total membership + 2/3 of present and voting |
| Ratification | Approval of an amendment by state legislatures |

### Mains questions

**1. Discuss the major electoral reforms needed in India to strengthen democracy. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Free and fair elections — basic structure; India's scale.
- **Body:**
  - Money power: expenditure limits, electoral bonds verdict, state funding (Indrajit Gupta).
  - Criminalisation: ADR 2002, Lily Thomas 2013, Public Interest Foundation 2018, fast-track courts for MPs/MLAs.
  - ECI independence: 2023 Act vs Anoop Baranwal.

**2. "The anti-defection law has achieved stability but at the cost of legislative freedom." Critically examine. (38 marks)**

**3. Explain the procedure for amending the Indian Constitution. Is it rigid or flexible? (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Article 368; South African influence.

- **Conclusion:** A balance — "neither too rigid nor too flexible" — with the basic structure as the anchor.

**4. Analyse the significance of the 106th Constitutional Amendment for women's political empowerment, with reference to Bihar's experience. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023.

- **Conclusion:** A milestone that needs timely implementation and capacity building.
