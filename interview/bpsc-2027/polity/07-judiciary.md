India has a single, **integrated judiciary**: one Supreme Court at the top, High Courts in the states, and subordinate courts below. This chapter covers how judges are appointed and removed, what kinds of cases each court hears, the writs, judicial review and public interest litigation. For BPSC, the **Patna High Court** (1916) is a must-know Bihar fact, and Bihar has given India some of its most famous PIL cases.

> **In simple words:** The courts decide disputes, protect your rights, and check whether laws and government actions follow the Constitution. The Supreme Court is the final court of appeal and the guardian of the Constitution. Each state (or group of states) has a High Court. Judges are protected from political pressure through fixed salaries, security of tenure and a hard removal process.

## The Supreme Court (Articles 124–147)

### Establishment and strength

- **Article 124** — "There shall be a Supreme Court of India." It was **inaugurated on 28 January 1950**, replacing the **Federal Court** (1937) and the **Privy Council** as the highest court of appeal.
- First Chief Justice of India (CJI): **H.J. Kania**.
- Strength: originally **8** (CJI + 7). Parliament raised it several times; the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019** fixed it at **34** (**CJI + 33**).
- **Seat** — Delhi (**Article 130**); the CJI can, with the President's approval, hold sittings elsewhere (no regional benches yet, though often demanded).
- CJI (as of 2026 — check the latest): **Justice Surya Kant**, who became the 53rd CJI on **24 November 2025**.

### Qualifications of a Supreme Court judge

A citizen of India who has been:
- a judge of a High Court for **5 years**, or
- an advocate of a High Court for **10 years**, or
- a **distinguished jurist** in the opinion of the President.

**No minimum age** is prescribed.

### Appointment — from consultation to collegium

| Stage | Case / law | What it decided |
|---|---|---|
| Text | **Article 124(2)** | President appoints after **consultation** with SC and HC judges he deems necessary; for other judges, the CJI must always be consulted |
| **First Judges case** | *S.P. Gupta v Union of India*, **1981** | "Consultation" does **not** mean "concurrence"; the executive had primacy |
| **Second Judges case** | *SC Advocates-on-Record Association*, **1993** | Overruled S.P. Gupta: **consultation means concurrence**; the CJI's opinion (formed with two senior-most judges) has **primacy** — birth of the **collegium** |
| **Third Judges case** | *In re Presidential Reference* (Article 143), **1998** | Collegium = **CJI + 4 senior-most judges** for SC appointments (CJI + 2 for HC) |
| **99th Amendment + NJAC Act** | **2014** | Created the **National Judicial Appointments Commission** (CJI, 2 senior judges, Law Minister, 2 eminent persons) |
| **Fourth Judges case** | *SC AoR Association v Union of India*, **16 October 2015** | Struck down the 99th Amendment and NJAC (4:1) as violating **independence of the judiciary** (basic structure); the collegium system revived |

- Appointment of the CJI: by convention, the **senior-most judge** of the SC (exceptions in 1973 — A.N. Ray; 1977 — M.H. Beg, during the controversy over supersession).
- **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)** guides appointments.
- **Acting CJI** — Article 126; **ad hoc judges** — Article 127 (when quorum is lacking, a HC judge qualified for the SC); **retired judges** — Article 128.

### Tenure and removal

- Retire at **65 years** (HC judges at **62** — raised from 60 by the **15th Amendment, 1963**).
- Can resign by writing to the **President**.
- **Removal — Article 124(4)**, on grounds of **proved misbehaviour or incapacity** ("impeachment" is the popular name; the Constitution does not use it for judges):
  1. A motion signed by **100 Lok Sabha members** or **50 Rajya Sabha members** is given to the Speaker/Chairman.
  2. The Speaker/Chairman may admit or reject it.
  3. If admitted, a **three-member committee** investigates: the **CJI or a SC judge**, a **Chief Justice of a High Court**, and a **distinguished jurist**.
  4. If the committee finds the judge guilty, each House must pass the motion by a **special majority** (majority of total membership and two-thirds of present and voting) **in the same session**.
  5. The **President** then passes the removal order.
- Procedure under the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**.
- **No judge of the SC has been removed so far.** Justice **V. Ramaswami** (1991–93) — the first removal motion; it failed in the Lok Sabha. Justice **Soumitra Sen** (Calcutta HC) — the Rajya Sabha passed the motion in **2011**, but he resigned before the Lok Sabha voted. In **2025**, a removal motion against Justice **Yashwant Varma** (over cash found at his residence) was admitted in the Lok Sabha and an inquiry committee was set up (status as of 2026 — check the latest).

### Independence of the judiciary — safeguards

- Mode of appointment (collegium; judiciary's role).
- **Security of tenure** — removal only by the hard process above.
- **Fixed service conditions** — salaries and allowances cannot be reduced to their disadvantage after appointment (except in a financial emergency).
- **Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** — not voted by Parliament.
- **Conduct of judges cannot be discussed** in Parliament or state legislatures, except on a removal motion (**Articles 121 and 211**).
- **Ban on practice after retirement** — a retired SC judge cannot plead in any court in India (a retired HC judge cannot plead in that HC or lower courts, but can in the SC and other HCs).
- **Power to punish for contempt** (Articles 129, 215).
- **Separation from the executive** (Article 50).
- Freedom to appoint its own staff and decide service conditions.

### Jurisdiction and powers

| Jurisdiction | Article | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Original** | **131** | Disputes between the **Centre and states**, or between states. **Exclusive** original jurisdiction. Excludes disputes from pre-Constitution treaties, inter-state water disputes (Article 262), Finance Commission matters etc. |
| **Writ** | **32** | Enforcement of **Fundamental Rights**; not exclusive (HCs under 226) |
| **Appellate — constitutional** | **132** | Appeals from HC if it certifies a **substantial question of law** about the interpretation of the Constitution |
| **Appellate — civil** | **133** | If HC certifies a substantial question of law of general importance |
| **Appellate — criminal** | **134** | Where HC (a) reverses an acquittal and gives **death**, (b) withdraws a case and gives death, or (c) certifies the case fit for appeal |
| **Special Leave Petition (SLP)** | **136** | **Discretionary** power to grant special leave to appeal against **any judgment of any court or tribunal** (except military tribunals) — the widest power |
| **Advisory** | **143** | President may refer a question of **law or fact** of public importance. The SC **may** give its opinion (for 143(1)); for **pre-Constitution treaty** disputes (143(2)) it **must**. The opinion is **not binding** on the President |
| **Court of record** | **129** | Its judgments are records of evidence; it can **punish for contempt of itself** |
| **Review** | **137** | Can review its own judgment. **Curative petition** — created in *Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002)* as a last remedy after review is dismissed |
| **Binding law** | **141** | Law declared by the SC is **binding on all courts** in India |
| **Complete justice** | **142** | Can pass any decree or order necessary for **complete justice** (used, for example, in the Ayodhya case 2019, and to grant divorce on irretrievable breakdown, 2023) |
| **Transfer** | **139A** | Can transfer cases between HCs or to itself |

- **Constitution Bench** — at least **5 judges** must hear cases involving a substantial question of interpretation of the Constitution or Article 143 references (Article 145(3)).
- Important **Article 143** references: Delhi Laws Act (1951), Kerala Education Bill (1958), **Berubari (1960)**, Keshav Singh (1965), Presidential Election (1974), Special Courts Bill (1978), **Cauvery (1991)**, Ram Janmabhoomi (1993 — the SC **declined** to answer), **Third Judges case (1998)**, Gujarat elections (2002), 2G spectrum/natural resources (2012), and the **2025 reference on timelines for Governors and the President to act on state bills** (the Constitution Bench, in November 2025, said courts cannot fix rigid timelines or declare "deemed assent", but Governors cannot sit on bills indefinitely — check the latest details).

> **Prelims angle:** The SC's **exclusive** original jurisdiction is under **Article 131** (Centre–state disputes). **Article 32** is **not** exclusive (the HCs share it). **Article 136** (SLP) is discretionary and the broadest. **Article 143** opinion is advisory, not binding. **Article 141** — SC law binds all courts (but not the SC itself).

## Judicial review

- **Judicial review** = the power of courts to examine whether laws and executive actions are **constitutional**, and to declare them **void** if not.
- Borrowed from the **USA** (*Marbury v Madison*, 1803). In India, it is not named as such but flows from Articles **13, 32, 131–136, 143, 226, 227, 245, 246, 251, 254 and 372**.
- Three areas: (1) **Fundamental Rights** violations; (2) **federal** distribution of powers; (3) **constitutional amendments** (only on the **basic structure** test).
- Judicial review is itself part of the **basic structure** (*Minerva Mills 1980*, *L. Chandra Kumar 1997*).

### Judicial activism and PIL

- **Judicial activism** — courts taking an active role to protect rights and push governance where other organs fail. **Judicial overreach** — courts going beyond their domain into policy-making (criticised, e.g. in some environment and administration cases). **Judicial restraint** — courts deferring to the legislature and executive.
- **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** — a case filed by **any public-spirited person** for the rights of those who cannot approach courts (poor, prisoners, workers). The rule of **locus standi** (only the aggrieved can sue) was relaxed in ***S.P. Gupta (1981)***.
- Pioneers: **Justice P.N. Bhagwati** and **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer**. **Epistolary jurisdiction** — courts treating **letters or postcards** as petitions (e.g. *Sunil Batra 1978*, *Bandhua Mukti Morcha 1984*).
- Guidelines to prevent misuse: *State of Uttaranchal v Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010)*.

> **Bihar connection:** India's PIL story began largely with **Bihar**. ***Hussainara Khatoon v State of Bihar (1979)*** — a petition based on newspaper reports by **Kapila Hingorani** about undertrials in **Bihar jails** held for years longer than their possible sentences; the SC ordered the release of thousands and declared **speedy trial** a part of Article 21. It is widely called **India's first PIL**. ***Khatri v State of Bihar (1981)*** — the **Bhagalpur blindings** case (police blinded undertrials with acid); the SC held the state must give **free legal aid** from the first production. ***Kadra Pahadiya v State of Bihar (1981)*** — undertrial tribal boys held for years; speedy trial again.

## High Courts (Articles 214–231)

### Basic facts

- **Article 214** — a High Court for each state. **Article 231** — Parliament can set up a **common High Court** for two or more states/UTs.
- There are **25 High Courts** (as of 2026). The three oldest — **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras** — were set up in **1862** under the **Indian High Courts Act 1861**. **Allahabad** followed in 1866. The newest are **Manipur, Meghalaya, Tripura (2013)**, **Telangana and Andhra Pradesh (separate HCs from 1 January 2019)**.
- **Common High Courts:**

| High Court | Jurisdiction |
|---|---|
| **Bombay** | Maharashtra, Goa, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu |
| **Calcutta** | West Bengal, Andaman & Nicobar Islands |
| **Guwahati** | Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh |
| **Madras** | Tamil Nadu, Puducherry |
| **Kerala** | Kerala, Lakshadweep |
| **Punjab & Haryana** | Punjab, Haryana, Chandigarh |
| **J&K and Ladakh** | UTs of J&K and Ladakh |

### Appointment, tenure and transfer

- **Article 217** — appointed by the **President** after consulting the **CJI**, the **Governor** of the state and the **Chief Justice of that HC** (in practice, the **SC collegium of CJI + 2** and the HC collegium).
- Qualifications: citizen; **10 years** as a judicial officer in India **or** **10 years** as an advocate of a HC. (No "distinguished jurist" option, unlike the SC.)
- **Oath** — administered by the **Governor** of the state.
- Retirement at **62**; removal like a SC judge.
- **Article 222** — **transfer** of a judge from one HC to another by the President after consulting the CJI.
- **Article 223** — acting Chief Justice; **224** — additional and acting judges (up to **2 years**); **224A** — retired judges can sit again.

### Jurisdiction

- **Original** — in admiralty, will, marriage, divorce, company law, contempt, election petitions for state/MP elections.
- **Writ (Article 226)** — for Fundamental Rights **and for "any other purpose"** (legal rights) — wider than Article 32.
- **Appellate** — civil and criminal appeals from district courts.
- **Supervisory (Article 227)** — superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its area (except military tribunals); this is administrative **and** judicial.
- **Control over subordinate courts (Article 235)** — postings, promotions, leave of district judges and other judicial officers.
- **Court of record (Article 215)** — can punish for contempt.
- **Judicial review** of state and central laws.

### The writs (summary)

| Writ | Use |
|---|---|
| **Habeas corpus** | Produce a detained person; available against public and **private** persons |
| **Mandamus** | Order a public official to perform a **public duty** (not against President/Governor/private person) |
| **Prohibition** | Stop a lower court/tribunal **before** it exceeds jurisdiction |
| **Certiorari** | **Quash** an order of a lower court/tribunal/administrative authority **after** it is passed |
| **Quo warranto** | Ask a person "by what authority" he holds a **public office**; any interested person can apply |

(Details and examples are in [Fundamental Rights](/interview/bpsc-2027/polity/fundamental-rights).)

## Subordinate courts (Articles 233–237)

- **Article 233** — **district judges** are appointed by the **Governor** in consultation with the **High Court**. Qualification: 7 years as an advocate or pleader (for direct recruitment).
- **Article 234** — appointment of other judicial officers by the Governor after consulting the **State Public Service Commission** (in Bihar, **BPSC**) and the High Court.
- **Article 235** — control of the High Court over subordinate courts.
- **Article 236** — "district judge" includes judge of a city civil court, additional district judge, sessions judge, chief presidency magistrate etc.
- In a district, the **District and Sessions Judge** is the highest judicial authority; below on the civil side are **Civil Judges (Senior Division)** and **(Junior Division)/Munsifs**; on the criminal side, **Chief Judicial Magistrate** and **Judicial Magistrates**. Criminal procedure is now governed by the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** (in force from **1 July 2024**, replacing the CrPC 1973), along with the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS)** replacing the IPC and the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA)** replacing the Evidence Act.
- The **All India Judicial Service (AIJS)** is permitted under **Article 312** (42nd Amendment) but **not yet created**.

## Alternative and people's courts

| Institution | Basis | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Lok Adalat** ("people's court") | **Legal Services Authorities Act 1987** (statutory status) | First held in **Gujarat (1982)**. Settles disputes by compromise; **no court fee**; award is **final and binding**, deemed a civil court decree; **no appeal**. **National Lok Adalats** are held across the country on fixed days. **Permanent Lok Adalats** for public utility services (2002 amendment) |
| **NALSA** | Same Act; constituted **1995** | National Legal Services Authority; **patron-in-chief = CJI**; executive chairman = second senior-most SC judge. State Legal Services Authorities (Bihar SLSA, patron-in-chief = Chief Justice of Patna HC), District LSAs. Free legal aid under **Article 39A** |
| **Gram Nyayalayas** | **Gram Nyayalayas Act 2008** (in force 2 October 2009) | Mobile village courts at the intermediate panchayat level; presided by a "Nyayadhikari" |
| **Family Courts** | **Family Courts Act 1984** | For marriage, divorce, custody disputes |
| **Fast Track Courts** | 2000 onwards (11th Finance Commission); special POCSO and rape FTSCs (2019) | Speedy disposal |
| **Tribunals** | **Articles 323A** (administrative — e.g. CAT, 1985) and **323B** (other matters) — **42nd Amendment** | *L. Chandra Kumar (1997)*: tribunal decisions are subject to **HC review**. **Tribunals Reforms Act 2021** abolished several tribunals |
| **National Green Tribunal (NGT)** | **NGT Act 2010** | Environmental cases; India is the third country (after Australia and New Zealand) with such a specialised body |

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar has a unique village justice system — the **Gram Kachahari**. Under the **Bihar Panchayat Raj Act 2006**, every Gram Panchayat has a Gram Kachahari headed by a directly elected **Sarpanch**, with **Panchs** and a legally trained **Nyay Mitra** (and a **Nyay Sachiv**) to assist. It tries **minor civil disputes and petty criminal cases**, mainly by conciliation, and can impose small fines (up to about **₹1,000** under its rules — check the current limit). It reduces the burden on regular courts and brings justice closer to villagers. Bihar also holds frequent Lok Adalats under the Bihar SLSA.

## Issues in the judiciary (for Mains)

- **Pendency** — crores of cases are pending across all courts (see the **National Judicial Data Grid** for the latest figure; it is above **5 crore** in recent years — check the latest). Bihar's district courts carry a heavy backlog, worsened by the large number of **prohibition-law cases**.
- **Vacancies** in HCs and lower courts; low judge-population ratio (the Law Commission (120th report, 1987) suggested 50 judges per 10 lakh people).
- **Collegium opacity** — demands for transparency; debate with the executive.
- **Access** — cost, language (English in higher courts), distance (demand for HC benches).
- **Undertrials** — a large share of prisoners in India (and in Bihar) are undertrials; link to Hussainara Khatoon.
- **Reforms** — e-Courts Mission Mode Project (phase III), virtual hearings, live-streaming of Constitution Bench hearings (since 2022), translation of judgments into regional languages (SUVAS — the Patna HC also publishes Hindi translations), ADR, AIJS, more Lok Adalats and Gram Kachaharis.

### Mains questions

**1. Discuss the evolution of the system of appointment of judges in India. Suggest reforms. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Article 124(2) and 217 — "consultation".
- **Body:**
  - First (1981), Second (1993), Third (1998) Judges cases.
  - 99th Amendment and NJAC; Fourth Judges case (2015) — independence of judiciary as basic structure.
  - Criticism of collegium: opacity, nepotism claims, delays, lack of diversity.
  - Executive's concerns vs judicial independence; Memorandum of Procedure.
  - Reforms: transparent criteria, a secretariat, time limits, diversity (women, SC/ST/OBC, regional), publishing reasons.
- **Conclusion:** Balance independence with accountability; a reformed collegium or a consensus-based commission.

**2. "Public Interest Litigation has transformed the role of the Indian judiciary." Discuss with examples, highlighting cases from Bihar. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** PIL — relaxation of locus standi (S.P. Gupta 1981); Bhagwati and Krishna Iyer.
- **Body:** Origin — Hussainara Khatoon (undertrials, Bihar), Khatri (Bhagalpur), Kadra Pahadiya; other milestones — Bandhua Mukti Morcha, M.C.
