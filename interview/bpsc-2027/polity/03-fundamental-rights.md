Fundamental Rights (FRs) are the most tested area of Polity in every PSC. BPSC asks article numbers ("Which Article abolishes untouchability?"), case names ("Which case expanded Article 21?"), and Mains questions on the right to life, reservation and the balance between rights and duties. Learn this chapter article by article.

> **In simple words:** Fundamental Rights are the basic rights every person (or every citizen) gets from the Constitution. They protect you against the government. If the government violates them, you can go straight to the Supreme Court (Article 32) or the High Court (Article 226). The courts can strike down any law that takes them away.

## Basics of Part III

- Part III, **Articles 12 to 35**. Called the **Magna Carta of India**. Taken from the **US Bill of Rights**.
- Originally **seven** FRs. The **right to property** (Article 31) was removed by the **44th Amendment (1978)** and is now a **legal right** under **Article 300A** (Part XII). So now there are **six** FRs.

| Category | Articles |
|---|---|
| 1. Right to Equality | 14–18 |
| 2. Right to Freedom | 19–22 |
| 3. Right against Exploitation | 23–24 |
| 4. Right to Freedom of Religion | 25–28 |
| 5. Cultural and Educational Rights | 29–30 |
| 6. Right to Constitutional Remedies | 32 |

### Features of FRs

- Some are available **only to citizens** (Articles **15, 16, 19, 29, 30**); others to **all persons**, including foreigners (e.g. 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 25–28).
- They are **not absolute** — the state can impose **reasonable restrictions**.
- Most are **negative** (they limit the state); some are positive (e.g. 21A education).
- **Justiciable** — enforceable in courts.
- Defended by the **Supreme Court** (Article 32); the person can go to the SC directly.
- Can be **amended** by Parliament, but not to destroy the basic structure.
- Can be **suspended during a national emergency** (except **Articles 20 and 21** — after the 44th Amendment).
- Can be restricted in areas under **martial law** (Article 34).
- Some are directly enforceable (e.g. 17, 23, 24); others need a law.

## Article 12 and Article 13

- **Article 12 — definition of "State"**: the Government and Parliament of India; government and legislature of each state; all **local authorities** (municipalities, panchayats, district boards); and **other authorities** (statutory or non-statutory bodies such as LIC, ONGC, SAIL). The courts use the test of "instrumentality or agency of the state" (Ajay Hasia, 1981). **The judiciary in its judicial role is not "State"** for FR purposes.
- **Article 13** — laws inconsistent with or in derogation of FRs are **void**. This is the source of **judicial review** for FRs. "Law" includes ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, customs and usages. By the **24th Amendment (1971)**, a constitutional amendment is **not** "law" under Article 13 (but it is still subject to the basic structure).

> **Remember:** **Doctrine of eclipse** — a pre-Constitution law that violates an FR is not dead, only "eclipsed"; if the FR is later amended, the law can revive. **Doctrine of severability** — only the bad part of a law is struck down, if it can be separated.

## Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)

### Article 14 — equality before law and equal protection of laws

- "**Equality before law**" — a negative concept from **Britain**: no one is above the law (Dicey's **rule of law**). The Supreme Court has held the rule of law is part of the basic structure.
- "**Equal protection of laws**" — a positive concept from the **USA**: equal treatment in equal circumstances. **Reasonable classification** is allowed (e.g. tax slabs), but **class legislation** is not.
- Available to **all persons**.
- **Exceptions:** Article 361 (President and Governors are not answerable to any court for their official acts; no criminal proceedings during their term), diplomatic immunity, MPs' and MLAs' privileges (Articles 105, 194), Article 31C.
- **E.P. Royappa (1974)** — Article 14 strikes at **arbitrariness**.

### Article 15 — no discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth (citizens only)

- **15(1)–(2)** — no discrimination by the state; no denial of access to shops, restaurants, hotels, wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and public resorts.
- **15(3)** — special provisions for **women and children** (e.g. reservation for women in local bodies — Bihar's 50%).
- **15(4)** — special provisions for **socially and educationally backward classes (SEBCs), SCs and STs** — added by the **1st Amendment (1951)** after *State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan*.
- **15(5)** — reservation in **educational institutions, including private** (aided or unaided, except minority institutions) — **93rd Amendment (2005)**. Upheld in **Ashoka Kumar Thakur (2008)**.
- **15(6)** — up to **10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS)** in educational institutions — **103rd Amendment (2019)**.

> **Common mistake:** Article 15 does **not** include "descent", "residence" or "language". "Descent" and "residence" appear in **Article 16(2)**. The ground "place of birth" is in both.

### Article 16 — equality of opportunity in public employment (citizens only)

- **16(1)–(2)** — no discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, **descent**, place of birth, **residence**.
- **16(3)** — Parliament can prescribe **residence** as a condition for certain jobs in a state/UT (only Parliament, not states).
- **16(4)** — reservation in appointments for **backward classes** not adequately represented.
- **16(4A)** — reservation in **promotion** for SCs/STs (**77th Amendment, 1995**); "with consequential seniority" (**85th Amendment, 2001**).
- **16(4B)** — **carry forward** of unfilled reserved vacancies; the 50% ceiling does not apply to the backlog (**81st Amendment, 2000**).
- **16(5)** — religious institutions can reserve posts for their own religion.
- **16(6)** — up to **10% EWS reservation** in jobs (**103rd Amendment, 2019**).

**Reservation cases to know**

| Case | Year | Held |
|---|---|---|
| **Indra Sawhney v Union of India** (Mandal case) | 1992 | Upheld **27% OBC reservation**; **creamy layer** must be excluded; total reservation should **not exceed 50%** except in extraordinary situations; **no reservation in promotions** (overturned by the 77th Amendment); backward class can be identified on caste basis |
| **M. Nagaraj** | 2006 | Upheld 77th, 81st, 82nd, 85th Amendments; state must show **backwardness, inadequate representation and administrative efficiency** before reservation in promotion |
| **Jarnail Singh** | 2018 | No need to collect data on backwardness of SC/ST for promotions; creamy layer principle applies to SC/ST promotions |
| **Janhit Abhiyan v Union of India** | 2022 (Nov) | **Upheld the 103rd Amendment (EWS)** by **3:2**; economic criteria alone can be the basis; the 50% ceiling is not inflexible |
| **State of Punjab v Davinder Singh** | 2024 (Aug) | 7-judge bench (6:1): states **can sub-classify SCs** for reservation (overruled E.V. Chinnaiah 2004) |

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar has a long history in the reservation debate. The **Karpoori Thakur formula (1978)** gave separate quotas to the Most Backward (Extremely Backward) Classes and Backward Classes, women and economically backward — an early model of sub-categorisation. (Karpoori Thakur was awarded the **Bharat Ratna in 2024**.) After the **Bihar caste-based survey (2023)**, Bihar raised reservation for SC, ST, EBC and BC from 50% to **65%** (plus 10% EWS = 75%) in November 2023. The **Patna High Court struck down this increase in June 2024** for breaching the 50% ceiling of Indra Sawhney; Bihar appealed to the Supreme Court (status as of 2026 — check the latest). This is a strong Mains example.

### Article 17 — abolition of untouchability

- "Untouchability" is abolished and its practice in any form is **forbidden**. Enforcement of any disability arising from it is an **offence** punishable by law.
- Laws: **Untouchability (Offences) Act 1955**, renamed **Protection of Civil Rights Act (1976)**; **SC and ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989**.
- The right is available against **private individuals** too. The word "untouchability" is **not defined** in the Constitution.

### Article 18 — abolition of titles

- The state shall not confer titles except **military or academic distinctions**.
- A citizen cannot accept a title from a foreign state.
- **Balaji Raghavan (1996)** — **Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, Padma Shri** are **not "titles"** under Article 18; they are awards and cannot be used as a prefix or suffix.

## Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)

### Article 19 — six freedoms (citizens only)

| Clause | Freedom | Grounds of reasonable restriction |
|---|---|---|
| 19(1)(a) | **Speech and expression** | 19(2): sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the state, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence |
| 19(1)(b) | **Assemble peaceably and without arms** | Sovereignty and integrity, public order |
| 19(1)(c) | **Form associations, unions or co-operative societies** ("co-operative" added by 97th Amendment 2011) | Sovereignty and integrity, public order, morality |
| 19(1)(d) | **Move freely** throughout India | Interest of the general public, protection of Scheduled Tribes |
| 19(1)(e) | **Reside and settle** in any part of India | Interest of the general public, protection of STs |
| 19(1)(g) | **Practise any profession, occupation, trade or business** | Interest of the general public; professional qualifications; state monopoly |

- **19(1)(f)** — right to property — was deleted by the 44th Amendment (1978).
- "Sovereignty and integrity of India" was added to 19(2) by the **16th Amendment (1963)**.
- **Freedom of the press** is included in 19(1)(a) (Romesh Thappar 1950, Sakal Papers 1962). Other parts read into 19(1)(a): **right to information** (Raj Narain 1975), right to silence, right to fly the national flag (Naveen Jindal 2004), right to know about election candidates (ADR 2002), commercial advertisement.
- **Shreya Singhal (2015)** — struck down **Section 66A** of the IT Act (vague restriction on online speech).
- **Anuradha Bhasin (2020)** — internet shutdowns must be necessary and proportionate; freedom of speech and trade over the internet is protected.

### Article 20 — protection in respect of conviction for offences (all persons)

1. **No ex-post-facto criminal law** — no one can be punished under a law made after the act was committed, or given a heavier penalty than the law allowed then. (Applies only to criminal laws, not civil or tax laws.)
2. **No double jeopardy** — no one can be prosecuted and punished **for the same offence more than once** (only for proceedings before a court or judicial tribunal).
3. **No self-incrimination** — no person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. **Selvi v State of Karnataka (2010)** — narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping tests cannot be done without consent.

### Article 21 — protection of life and personal liberty (all persons)

"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to **procedure established by law**."

| Case | Year | Held |
|---|---|---|
| **A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras** | 1950 | Narrow view: protection only against arbitrary **executive** action; "procedure established by law" (from **Japan**) — any procedure in a valid law is enough |
| **Maneka Gandhi v Union of India** | 1978 | **Landmark**: the procedure must be "**just, fair and reasonable**", not arbitrary; brought in the American "**due process of law**" in substance. Linked Articles **14, 19 and 21** — the "**golden triangle**". Right to travel abroad is part of personal liberty |
| **Hussainara Khatoon v State of Bihar** | 1979 | **Right to speedy trial** — thousands of **undertrials in Bihar jails** had been held longer than the maximum sentence; led to their release. A foundation of **PIL** |
| **Khatri v State of Bihar** (Bhagalpur blindings) | 1981 | **Right to free legal aid** from the moment of production before a magistrate; state liable for police atrocities |
| **Olga Tellis** | 1985 | **Right to livelihood** (pavement dwellers of Bombay) |
| **M.C. Mehta** (several) | 1986 onward | Right to a **pollution-free environment**; **absolute liability** (Oleum gas leak, 1987) |
| **Mohini Jain / Unni Krishnan** | 1992 / 1993 | **Right to education** — up to 14 years in Unni Krishnan; led to **Article 21A** |
| **Parmanand Katara** | 1989 | Right to emergency medical aid |
| **Vishaka v State of Rajasthan** | 1997 | Guidelines against **sexual harassment at the workplace** (later the POSH Act 2013) |
| **D.K. Basu** | 1997 | Guidelines on arrest and custody; against custodial torture |
| **Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v Union of India** | 2017 | 9-judge bench: **Right to privacy** is an FR under Article 21 (overruled M.P. Sharma 1954 and Kharak Singh 1962). Aadhaar case (2018) upheld Aadhaar with limits |
| **Common Cause v Union of India** | 2018 | **Right to die with dignity** — **passive euthanasia** and **living will** allowed (Aruna Shanbaug 2011 had first allowed passive euthanasia) |
| **Navtej Singh Johar** | 2018 | Decriminalised consensual same-sex relations (read down **Section 377** IPC) |
| **Joseph Shine** | 2018 | Struck down adultery (Section 497 IPC) |

Other rights read into Article 21: right to live with **human dignity**, **shelter**, **health**, **clean water**, **food**, **sleep**, **reputation**, **fair trial**, **against handcuffing**, **against delayed execution**, **against solitary confinement**, **against bonded labour**, **to marry a person of one's choice** (Shafin Jahan/Hadiya 2018), **right to be protected from adverse effects of climate change** (M.K. Ranjitsinh, 2024).

> **Memory trick:** "**G**opalan **G**ave a narrow road, **M**aneka **M**ade it a highway." A.K. Gopalan (1950) = narrow; Maneka Gandhi (1978) = wide.

### Article 21A — right to education

- Added by the **86th Amendment (2002)**. Free and compulsory education to **all children aged 6 to 14 years**.
- Law: **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009**, in force from **1 April 2010**.
- The 86th Amendment also changed **Article 45** (DPSP) to early childhood care and education for children **below 6**, and added **Fundamental Duty 51A(k)** (parents to provide opportunities for education to children 6–14).

### Article 22 — protection against arrest and detention

| Punitive detention (ordinary arrest) — 22(1), (2) | Preventive detention — 22(3)–(7) |
|---|---|
| Right to be informed of the **grounds of arrest** | Detention without trial to **prevent** a future act |
| Right to **consult and be defended by a lawyer** of one's choice | Detention beyond **3 months** needs the report of an **advisory board** (judges of HC) |
| Right to be produced before a **magistrate within 24 hours** (excluding journey time) | Grounds to be communicated (facts against public interest can be withheld) |
| Right to be released after 24 hours unless the magistrate authorises further detention | Right to make a representation |
| **Not available** to an enemy alien or a person held under preventive detention law | Parliament makes the law; laws: **NSA 1980**, COFEPOSA 1974, UAPA 1967 (amended) |

- India is one of the few democracies that allows **preventive detention in peacetime** in its Constitution.
- Parliament and state legislatures **both** can make preventive detention laws on matters of security of a state, public order, supplies (Concurrent List); only Parliament for defence, foreign affairs and security of India.

## Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)

- **Article 23** — prohibits **traffic in human beings, begar (forced labour without payment) and other forms of forced labour**. Available against the state and private persons. Exception: **compulsory service for public purposes** (e.g. military service, social service) without discrimination. Laws: **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976**, Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act 1956, Minimum Wages Act 1948. *People's Union for Democratic Rights (Asiad workers, 1982)* — paying less than minimum wage is forced labour.
- **Article 24** — no **child below 14 years** shall be employed in **factories, mines or other hazardous employment**. Law: **Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986** (as amended in **2016** — complete ban on employment of children below 14 in all occupations except family enterprises and entertainment after school hours; adolescents 14–18 banned from hazardous work). *M.C. Mehta v State of Tamil Nadu (1996)* — Child Labour Rehabilitation Welfare Fund.

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar is a major source state for **child labour and trafficking** (to brick kilns, embroidery units, and to other states). Mains answers on Article 23–24 should mention rescue operations, the Bihar State Child Labour Commission, and links with poverty and migration.

## Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)

| Article | Content |
|---|---|
| **25** | Freedom of **conscience** and free **profession, practice and propagation** of religion (all persons). Subject to public order, morality, health. The state can regulate secular activities linked to religion and throw open Hindu religious institutions to all classes. **Sikhs** may wear and carry **kirpans**. "Hindus" includes Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists for this purpose. Right to propagate does **not** include right to forcibly convert (Rev. Stainislaus, 1977) |
| **26** | Freedom to **manage religious affairs** — every religious denomination can establish institutions, manage its affairs, own and acquire property |
| **27** | No person shall be compelled to pay **taxes** for the promotion of any particular religion (fees are allowed) |
| **28** | No **religious instruction** in institutions **wholly maintained** out of state funds. Allowed in institutions administered by the state but set up under a trust that requires it. In state-recognised or aided institutions, no one can be compelled to attend religious instruction |

- **Essential religious practices** test — courts decide which practices are essential (Shirur Mutt 1954). Cases: **Shayara Bano (2017)** — instant **triple talaq** (talaq-e-biddat) unconstitutional (later the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act 2019); **Indian Young Lawyers Association (Sabarimala, 2018)** — women of all ages may enter (referred to a larger bench in 2019).

## Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

- **Article 29** — any section of **citizens** having a distinct **language, script or culture** has the right to conserve it. No citizen can be denied admission to state-maintained or aided institutions on grounds of religion, race, caste or language. (Protects minorities and also the majority.)
- **Article 30** — **all minorities, religious or linguistic**, have the right to **establish and administer educational institutions** of their choice. The state shall not discriminate in giving aid. Compensation if property of a minority institution is acquired (44th Amendment).
- **T.M.A. Pai Foundation (2002)** — minority status to be decided at the **state level**.
- The word "**minority**" is **not defined** in the Constitution. Under the **National Commission for Minorities Act 1992**, six communities are notified as minorities at the national level: **Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis (Zoroastrians) and Jains** (Jains added in 2014).

## Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

- Ambedkar called Article 32 "**the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it**".
- The **Supreme Court** is the **defender and guarantor** of FRs. A person can **move the SC directly**.
- The SC can issue **writs**: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto.
- Parliament can empower other courts to issue writs (it has not done so yet; HCs already have Article 226).
- Article 32 is part of the **basic structure** — it cannot be removed by amendment. It can be **suspended** only during a national emergency for the rights suspended (Article 359).

### The five writs

| Writ | Meaning | Against whom | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Habeas corpus** | "To have the body (of)" | Public authorities **and private individuals** | Produce a detained person in court; release if detention is illegal. Most valuable for personal liberty |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | Public officials, public bodies, corporations, inferior courts, tribunals, government | Command to **perform a public duty**. **Not** against the President, Governors, a private person, a private body (unless performing public duty), or a CJ acting in judicial capacity; not to enforce a departmental instruction without statutory force |
| **Prohibition** | "To forbid" | Judicial and **quasi-judicial** bodies only (not administrative, legislative or private bodies) | Higher court **stops a lower court** from exceeding jurisdiction — **before** the order is passed (preventive) |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified / to be informed" | Judicial, quasi-judicial and (since 1991) **administrative authorities** affecting rights | Higher court **quashes** an order of a lower court/tribunal — **after** the order is passed (curative). Not against legislative bodies or private persons |
| **Quo warranto** | "By what authority / warrant" | Holder of a **substantive public office** created by statute or the Constitution | Inquire into the legality of a person's claim to a public office. **Any interested person** can seek it, not only the aggrieved person. Not for ministerial or private offices |

> **Memory trick:** "**H**ave **M**y **P**en, **C**at, **Q**ueen" — **H**abeas (body), **M**andamus (must do duty), **P**rohibition (stop before), **C**ertiorari (cancel after), **Q**uo warranto (question the seat).

### Article 32 vs Article 226

| Point | Article 32 (Supreme Court) | Article 226 (High Court) |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Only enforcement of **Fundamental Rights** | FRs **and "any other purpose"** (legal rights) — wider |
| Territorial reach | Whole of India | Within its territorial jurisdiction (or where the cause of action arises) |
| Nature | Itself a Fundamental Right; SC cannot refuse to exercise | Discretionary; HC may refuse |
| Suspension | Can be suspended during national emergency (Article 359) | Not a Fundamental Right; cannot be suspended in the same way |

## Articles 33–35 and suspension

- **Article 33** — Parliament can restrict or abrogate FRs of the **armed forces, paramilitary forces, police, intelligence agencies** and similar forces.
- **Article 34** — restrictions on FRs while **martial law** is in force in an area (martial law is not defined in the Constitution). Parliament can indemnify officials for acts done to restore order.
- **Article 35** — Parliament alone (not states) can make laws on certain FR matters (e.g. 16(3), 32(3), 33, 34) and punishments for offences under FRs (e.g. untouchability).
- **Article 358** — when a national emergency is declared on the ground of **war or external aggression**, **Article 19 is automatically suspended**.
- **Article 359** — the President can by order suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of FRs (**except Articles 20 and 21**).

## Amendment of FRs — the long debate

| Case / Amendment | Year | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Shankari Prasad** | 1951 | Parliament can amend FRs under Article 368 |
| **Sajjan Singh** | 1965 | Same view |
| **I.C. Golaknath v State of Punjab** | 1967 | 11 judges: Parliament **cannot** amend FRs (FRs are "transcendental and immutable") |
| **24th Amendment** | 1971 | Parliament can amend any part including FRs; President must give assent to amendment bills |
| **25th Amendment** | 1971 | Inserted **Article 31C** — laws giving effect to DPSP 39(b) and (c) cannot be challenged under Articles 14 and 19 |
| **Kesavananda Bharati** | 1973 | Parliament can amend FRs but **not the basic structure** |
| **42nd Amendment** | 1976 | Extended 31C to all DPSPs; declared no limits on amending power |
| **Minerva Mills** | 1980 | Struck down 42nd Amendment's extension of 31C and the "unlimited power" clause; **harmony between FRs and DPSPs** is basic structure |
| **Waman Rao** | 1981 | Amendments to the 9th Schedule after **24 April 1973** (date of Kesavananda) are open to challenge |
| **I.R. Coelho** | 2007 | 9-judge bench: laws in the 9th Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be tested against the basic structure |

## Key terms

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Justiciable | Can be enforced by a court |
| Reasonable restriction | A limit on a right that the court finds fair and necessary |
| Due process of law | Courts check whether a law itself is fair, not just whether it was properly passed |
| Procedure established by law | Courts check only whether a valid law was followed |
| Preventive detention | Holding a person to stop a possible future crime, without trial |
| Creamy layer | Better-off members of a backward class who are excluded from reservation |
| Writ | A formal written order of a higher court |
| Double jeopardy | Being tried twice for the same offence |
| Doctrine of eclipse | An old law against FRs becomes inactive, not dead |
| Essential religious practice | A practice so central to a religion that the state cannot interfere |

### Mains questions

**1. "Article 21 has become the reservoir of many rights through judicial interpretation." Discuss. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Text of Article 21; from A.K. Gopalan's narrow reading to Maneka Gandhi's "just, fair and reasonable".
- **Body:**
  - Maneka Gandhi (1978) — golden triangle, due process in substance.
  - Livelihood (Olga Tellis), environment (M.C. Mehta), education (Unni Krishnan → 21A), health and emergency care (Parmanand Katara), privacy (Puttaswamy), dignity in death (Common Cause), climate change (2024).
  - Bihar's contribution to jurisprudence: Hussainara Khatoon (speedy trial, undertrials), Khatri (free legal aid, Bhagalpur blindings).
  - Role of PIL and epistolary jurisdiction.
  - Criticism: judicial overreach, implementation gaps (e.g. pendency, overcrowded jails in Bihar).
- **Conclusion:** Article 21 has turned into the guarantee of a dignified life; the state must match it with resources.

**2. Examine the constitutional provisions and judicial pronouncements on reservation in India, with reference to Bihar. (38 marks)**

- **Intro:** Equality includes substantive equality — Articles 15(4), 16(4).
- **Body:**
  - Constitutional provisions: 15(4), 15(5), 15(6), 16(4), 16(4A), 16(4B), 16(6), 330, 332, 335, 338–338B.
  - Indra Sawhney: 27% OBC, creamy layer, 50% cap.
