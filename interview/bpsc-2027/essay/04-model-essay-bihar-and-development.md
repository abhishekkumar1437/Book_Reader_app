Bihar-centric topics are the most likely kind of essay in a BPSC paper, and also the easiest to write badly. Many candidates either praise every scheme or list every problem. This chapter shows a balanced, well-built essay on Bihar's development, explains each choice the writer made, and gives you two more outlines to practise.

> **In simple words:** Read the essay once for the flow. Read it again with the breakdown table beside it. Then close the book and try to rebuild its outline from memory — that is how you learn structure.

## The topic

**"From Nalanda to New Bihar: The Road to Inclusive Development"**

*Type:* descriptive with an argumentative thesis. *Keywords:* Nalanda (past glory), New Bihar (present change), road (process, path), inclusive development (growth that reaches everyone).

*The demand in one line:* Trace how Bihar went from glory to backwardness to recovery, define what inclusive development means, examine the main sectors with balance, and propose a realistic path.

## The model essay (about 1,100 words)

### From Nalanda to New Bihar: The Road to Inclusive Development

About fifteen hundred years ago, scholars from China, Korea and Tibet travelled for months to reach Nalanda, one of the greatest centres of learning the world had seen. Today, the journey often runs the other way. Every week, crowded trains leave Patna, Darbhanga and Saharsa carrying young Biharis to Delhi, Surat, Punjab and Kerala in search of work. Between these two pictures lies the story of Bihar — a land that once taught the world, then fell behind, and is now working hard to rise again. The real question is not whether Bihar can develop, but what kind of development it needs. The answer is development that is inclusive, sustainable and built on the strength of its people.

Bihar's past gives it every reason for confidence. Magadha built India's first great empires, and Pataliputra was the seat of the Mauryas and the Guptas. Vaishali was among the earliest known republics. The Buddha found enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, Mahavira attained nirvana at Pawapuri, and Aryabhata worked on astronomy at Kusumapura. Centuries later, Sher Shah Suri of Sasaram built roads and reformed revenue in ways the British would later copy. In 1917, Champaran gave India Gandhi's first satyagraha in the country. Bihar, in short, has never lacked ideas or leadership.

Why, then, did it fall behind? The causes are many. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 created a class of landlords who extracted rent but invested little in land. After independence, slow land reform kept most farmers poor, and the freight equalisation policy, as many economists argue, weakened the advantage of the region's mineral wealth. Annual floods in the north and droughts in the south damaged crops and roads. In 2000, the creation of Jharkhand took away most of the mines and heavy industry. For years, weak governance and law-and-order problems also kept investors away.

The last two decades, however, tell a different story. Thousands of kilometres of rural roads and many new bridges over the Ganga and its tributaries have connected villages to markets. Most homes now have electricity. School enrolment has risen sharply, helped by schemes such as the bicycle scheme for girls in class nine. Bihar has recorded one of the faster growth rates among Indian states in several recent years. Yet the state still has the lowest per-capita income among major states. Growth has begun, but it has started from a very low base, and it has not yet reached everyone equally.

This is why the word "inclusive" matters. Development is not only a rising growth figure. As Amartya Sen argues, true development expands people's real freedoms — to be healthy, educated, safe and able to earn a living with dignity. A new highway means little to a landless labourer's family if their children are malnourished and their daughter leaves school at fourteen. Gandhi's Talisman puts it simply: before any step, recall the face of the poorest person you have seen and ask whether it will help him. For Bihar, this is the only test that counts.

Agriculture must be the first engine. Most of Bihar's rural workers still depend on farming, and the fertile Gangetic plains are among the richest soils in India. Bihar grows most of India's makhana and is famous for the Shahi litchi of Muzaffarpur, both with GI tags. But farms are tiny and fragmented, and farmers sell raw produce cheaply. Food processing, cold storage, farmer producer organisations and better access to markets can turn these crops into incomes. The state's successive Agriculture Road Maps show that planning exists; the challenge is to reach the smallest farmer.

The second engine must be jobs outside farming. Only about one in nine people in Bihar lived in towns in Census 2011, and the state has few large factories. Its density — over a thousand people per square kilometre — makes land for big industry scarce. Bihar's path may therefore lie in small and medium enterprises: food processing, textiles, leather, logistics, tourism and services. Migration will not stop overnight, and it does bring remittances and skills home. But the aim should be that young people leave by choice, not by compulsion.

Third, Bihar must invest in its people. National Family Health Survey data (2019–21) showed that about four in ten young children in Bihar were stunted. Classrooms are fuller, but learning levels remain low in many schools. Better primary health centres, nutrition through anganwadis, trained teachers and skills linked to real jobs are not costs; they are the foundation of every other gain.

Women are already showing what inclusion can achieve. In 2006, Bihar became the first state to reserve half of all panchayat seats for women. Through JEEViKA, lakhs of self-help groups have brought rural women savings, credit and confidence. Thirty-five per cent reservation in state government jobs has brought women into the police and administration. Each of these steps has gaps — proxy leadership by husbands, low wages, safety concerns — but together they prove that when women are included, villages change faster.

Development must also respect nature. The Kosi, rightly called the "Sorrow of Bihar", showed its power when its embankment broke at Kusaha in August 2008 and flooded large parts of north Bihar. Climate change is now adding heatwaves, lightning and erratic rain. Embankments alone cannot tame such rivers. Bihar needs early warning, flood-resilient housing, better drainage, revived ponds and wetlands, and crops that suit changing seasons. The Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali campaign, which links water, greenery and life, is a step in the right direction.

None of this is possible without good governance. The Bihar Right to Public Services Act of 2011 promised time-bound delivery of certificates and services, and digital portals have reduced some middlemen. But many offices still face staff shortages, delays and petty corruption. Stronger panchayats, transparent spending and officials who see citizens as partners, not petitioners, will decide whether money reaches the ground.

Some argue that Bihar's problems — high population, floods, poverty and social divisions — are too large to solve. History disagrees. States once considered poor, like Kerala in health or Gujarat in dairy, changed through steady policy and people's participation. Bihar's young population, if educated and skilled, is not a burden but its greatest asset.

The road ahead, then, is clear in direction even if long in distance: modern agriculture with value addition, small industries and services for jobs, strong schools and hospitals, empowered women, climate-resilient planning and honest, responsive administration. Nalanda's scholars once came to Bihar because it offered knowledge and opportunity. If Bihar builds development that includes its poorest, its youngest and its daughters, the trains of tomorrow may carry not only workers leaving the state, but people coming back — to build a new Bihar worthy of its old glory.

## Why this essay works — annotated breakdown

| Para | What it does | Technique to copy |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opens with a contrast: Nalanda's visitors vs today's migrant trains; ends with a clear thesis | **Contrast opening** + **thesis** in last line. Covers both keywords "Nalanda" and "New Bihar" at once |
| 2 | Past glory | Dense, accurate Bihar facts in one paragraph; ends with a one-line summary ("never lacked ideas") |
| 3 | Causes of decline | **Multi-causal** — colonial, policy, natural, political, administrative. "As many economists argue" hedges a debated point |
| 4 | Recent turnaround — with honesty | **Balance:** gains first, then "Yet…" with the per-capita gap. Uses trend language instead of risky numbers |
| 5 | Defines "inclusive development" | Addresses the main keyword directly; uses Sen's idea and Gandhi's Talisman (both correctly attributed) |
| 6 | Agriculture | Point → Bihar evidence (makhana, litchi, GI) → problem → solution → link to policy (Agriculture Road Maps) |
| 7 | Industry, jobs, migration | Explains *why* big industry is hard (density); proposes a realistic alternative (MSMEs); fair view on migration |
| 8 | Human development | One reliable figure with its source (NFHS-5); turns "costs" into "foundation" |
| 9 | Women | Three Bihar examples, then gaps (proxy leadership), then the lesson |
| 10 | Environment and floods | A specific event (Kusaha breach, 2008); adds climate change; solution list; one scheme |
| 11 | Governance | A law (RTPS 2011); admits problems; ends with a quotable phrase ("partners, not petitioners") |
| 12 | Counter-view and rebuttal | Takes the pessimistic view seriously; answers with examples from other states |
| 13 | Conclusion | Sums up the "engines" in one sentence; **callback** to Nalanda and the trains; hopeful final image |

### What makes it score

- **Keywords are honoured.** "Nalanda", "new Bihar", "road" and "inclusive" all appear in the introduction, the body and the conclusion.
- **Structure is visible without headings.** Signposts ("Agriculture must be the first engine", "The second engine", "Third") guide the examiner.
- **Many dimensions:** historical, economic, social, gender, environmental, governance, ethical (Gandhi's Talisman).
- **Balance:** every success is followed by an honest gap; a full paragraph is given to the counter-view.
- **Safe facts only:** exact numbers are few (1917, 1793, 2000, 2006, 2008, 2011, NFHS-5); fast-changing figures are written as trends.
- **Language:** short sentences, no jargon, varied transitions ("however", "yet", "then", "in short").

> **Mains angle:** Notice that the essay never names a political party or leader in praise or blame. Policies are described as the state's policies. This keeps the essay neutral, which is exactly what an examiner of a civil services exam expects.

> **Common mistake:** Writing a Bihar essay as a list of schemes ("Saat Nischay, JEEViKA, cycle scheme, Kanya Utthan…"). Schemes are evidence, not arguments. Use two or three, attach each to a point, and mention their gaps.

## Alternate outline 1 — "Grassroots democracy is the foundation of Bihar's development"

*Type:* argumentative. *Keywords:* grassroots democracy (panchayats and urban local bodies), foundation, development.

- **Intro idea:** A woman mukhiya in a Bihar village chairs a gram sabha on a new drain and school wall — the image of democracy at its smallest and most real scale. Thesis: development lasts only when people at the bottom help plan it.
- **Body points:**
  1. Meaning — 73rd Amendment (1992): three tiers, gram sabha, State Election Commission, State Finance Commission; Article 243D.
  2. Historical roots — Vaishali's republic; Gandhi's idea of village self-rule; Bihar Panchayati Raj Act, 2006.
  3. Political dimension — Bihar's 50% reservation for women (2006) and reservation for backward classes in panchayats; new leadership.
  4. Economic dimension — panchayats implementing rural schemes (MGNREGA, lanes and drains under Saat Nischay), local planning.
  5. Social dimension — women and marginalised groups gain voice; dignity and confidence.
  6. Accountability — gram sabhas, social audits, RTI; closer to citizens than distant offices.
  7. Problems — weak finances and own revenue, staff shortages, proxy leaders, elite capture, low gram sabha attendance.
  8. Counter-view — "panchayats spread corruption to villages" → answer: the remedy is transparency, not centralisation.
  9. Way forward — devolve funds, functions and functionaries (the "3 Fs"), training of elected members, digital record-keeping, strong gram sabhas.
- **Bihar examples:** 50% women's reservation (2006); Bihar Panchayati Raj Act 2006; panchayat role in Saat Nischay works; Vaishali heritage.
- **Quote:** Ambedkar — "Political democracy cannot last unless there lies at the base of it social democracy."
- **Conclusion idea:** A strong house needs a strong foundation; Bihar's democracy will be as strong as its gram sabhas.

## Alternate outline 2 — "Bihar's heritage: from pride of the past to engine of the future"

*Type:* descriptive with a development angle. *Keywords:* heritage (built, spiritual, living culture), engine (economic driver), future.

- **Intro idea:** A Japanese pilgrim meditating under the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya, and a Madhubani artist selling her work online — two faces of the same heritage. Thesis: Bihar's heritage can create jobs, pride and soft power if managed well.
- **Body points:**
  1. Built heritage — Nalanda and Mahabodhi (UNESCO sites), Vikramshila, Rajgir, Vaishali, Sasaram's Sher Shah tomb.
  2. Spiritual circuits — Buddhist circuit, Jain sites (Pawapuri), Patna Sahib (Sikh), Sufi shrines; unity in diversity.
  3. Living heritage — Madhubani, Manjusha, Sikki, Tikuli art; Chhath; folk music in Bhojpuri, Maithili, Magahi.
  4. Economic value — tourism jobs (guides, hotels, transport, crafts), GI-tagged crafts and foods, women artists' incomes.
  5. Soft power — Buddhist connection with Asian countries; revived Nalanda University (new campus 2024) as a symbol of knowledge diplomacy.
  6. Challenges — poor last-mile connectivity, limited hotels, safety perceptions, weak marketing, encroachment and neglect of lesser sites.
  7. Risks — over-commercialisation; damage to fragile sites; artists losing out to middlemen.
  8. Way forward — circuit-based tourism, homestays, trained local guides, digital marketing, protecting sites with the ASI, fair-price platforms for artisans.
- **Bihar examples:** Bodh Gaya, Nalanda, Rajgir, Patna Sahib, Madhubani painting (GI tag), Bhagalpuri silk.
- **Quote:** Buddha's teaching "Appo deepo bhava" (Be a lamp unto yourself) — Bihar's heritage can light its own way.
- **Conclusion idea:** Heritage is not a museum piece; when it feeds families and builds friendships across Asia, the past becomes an investment in the future.

> **Bihar connection:** These two outlines show the two main ways to bring Bihar into an essay: through **institutions and policy** (panchayats) and through **identity and culture** (heritage). Practise both.

## Key terms

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Inclusive development | Growth whose benefits reach all groups, especially the poor |
| Per-capita income | Average income per person in a state or country |
| Freight equalisation | A former policy that kept the price of some goods (like steel and coal) the same everywhere in India, which reduced the location advantage of mineral states |
| Permanent Settlement | 1793 land revenue system in Bengal Presidency (including Bihar) fixing revenue permanently with zamindars |
| Value addition | Processing raw produce into a product that sells for more |
| Farmer producer organisation (FPO) | A group of farmers who buy and sell together to get better prices |
| Remittance | Money sent home by migrant workers |
| Signposting | Words that tell the reader where the essay is going |

## Practice

### Writing tasks

**Task 1 — Rebuild the outline (10 minutes).** Close the book. Write the 13-paragraph outline of the model essay from memory. Then compare.

**Task 2 — Full essay (60 minutes, timed).** Write a full essay on Alternate outline 1 or 2 in the exam time of about 56 minutes (10 planning + 43 writing + 3 review). Aim for 900–1,000 words.

**Task 3 — Rewrite a paragraph (15 minutes).** Rewrite paragraph 7 (jobs and migration) of the model essay from the point of view of a migrant worker's family, keeping it balanced.

**Task 4 — Your own topic (60 minutes).** Write a full essay on "Bihar's youth: the key to its future." Use at least three ideas from this chapter.

**Self-check rubric (score each 0–2; total out of 20)**

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction catches interest and ends in a thesis | Missing | One of the two | Both |
| Topic keywords used in intro, body and conclusion | No | Partly | Fully |
| At least 6 dimensions covered | < 4 | 4–5 | 6+ |
| Bihar examples (3–5), spread out | < 2 | 2 | 3+ spread |
| Balance: gaps admitted and a counter-view paragraph | None | Gaps only | Both |
| Facts accurate and dated; no invented figures | Errors | Minor doubts | All safe |
| One idea per paragraph, with signposts | No | Partly | Yes |
| Conclusion: summary + way forward + memorable line | Weak | Two of three | All three |
| Language: simple, correct, varied transitions | Many errors | Few errors | Clean |
| Completed within time and word limit | No | Slightly over | Yes |

A score of **16+** is exam-ready. Below 12, re-read Chapter 2 and repeat Task 2 next week.
