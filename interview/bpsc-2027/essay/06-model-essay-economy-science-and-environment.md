Economy, science and environment topics reward candidates who can connect them. An essay on "climate change" that ignores jobs, or an essay on "technology" that ignores the poor, looks one-sided. This chapter's model essay shows how to hold three themes together — growth, technology and nature — with Bihar at the centre.

> **In simple words:** In these topics, examiners look for **trade-offs** (what we gain and what we lose), **solutions** (often technology and policy), and **people** (who benefits, who pays). Keep asking: growth for whom, at what cost, and how can science reduce that cost?

## The topic

**"Development Without Destruction: Can Growth and Environment Go Together?"**

*Type:* argumentative (a question topic). *Keywords:* development, without destruction, growth, environment, "can… go together" (you must give a clear answer, with conditions).

*The demand in one line:* Explain the conflict between growth and environment, show why a poor state like Bihar cannot choose one over the other, and prove — with technology, policy and community examples — that they can go together if we plan well.

## The model essay (about 1,100 words)

### Development Without Destruction: Can Growth and Environment Go Together?

In May, many villages in south Bihar wait anxiously for water as ponds dry up and hand pumps fail. By August, large parts of north Bihar are under water as the Kosi, Gandak and Bagmati overflow. The same state, in the same year, suffers from too little water and too much. These are not only natural events. They are also the result of how we have used land, rivers, forests and air in the name of development. For a state that still needs rapid growth to remove poverty, the question is urgent: must Bihar choose between growth and environment? The answer is no. Growth and environment can go together — but only if development is planned with science, honesty and the participation of people.

The idea of sustainable development answers this question in principle. The Brundtland Commission in 1987 described it as development that "meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." It rests on three pillars: economic growth, social equity and environmental protection. If any one pillar breaks, the structure falls. A factory that creates jobs but poisons a river destroys the livelihoods of fishermen and farmers downstream. Growth that destroys its own base is not development; it is borrowing from the future.

History explains why the conflict feels so sharp. The industrial revolution made Europe and North America rich, but it filled the air with carbon that is now warming the planet. Developed countries are responsible for most of the carbon released in the past, while countries like India face many of the effects. This is why international climate agreements, from the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change of 1992 to the Paris Agreement of 2015, accept the principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities". India's per-capita emissions remain far below those of rich nations, and its first duty is to lift millions out of poverty.

Yet India has not used this as an excuse. It has committed to reaching net-zero emissions by 2070 and has rapidly expanded solar and wind power. Through Mission LiFE — Lifestyle for Environment — it has also asked citizens to adopt simple habits such as saving water and energy and reducing waste. The message is clear: India wants to grow, but it wants to grow differently from the path the rich world took.

For Bihar, this is not a distant debate. It is a matter of survival. North Bihar is among the most flood-prone regions of India, and the 2008 Kusaha breach of the Kosi embankment showed how a single disaster can wipe out years of progress. South Bihar faces recurring drought. Heatwaves and lightning now kill many people each year. Groundwater in several districts along the Ganga contains arsenic, and cities like Patna face serious air pollution in winter. Every flood that destroys a road, every drought that ruins a harvest, and every illness caused by polluted air reduces Bihar's growth. Ignoring the environment is therefore bad economics.

Science and technology offer the bridge between growth and nature. Solar power is now among the cheapest sources of electricity, and solar irrigation pumps can free farmers from costly diesel. Satellite data from ISRO and better weather models allow earlier flood forecasts and lightning warnings, giving families time to move to safety. Drip and sprinkler irrigation save water. Biogas plants can turn cattle dung into cooking fuel and fertiliser. Electric vehicles, energy-efficient buildings and modern waste processing can make Bihar's growing towns cleaner. Technology does not remove the need for wise choices, but it widens the choices available.

Agriculture, which supports most of rural Bihar, is where this bridge matters most. Practices such as zero tillage, direct seeding of rice, crop diversification and timely weather advisories help farmers cope with changing rainfall while cutting costs. Bihar has promoted climate-resilient farming through demonstration villages in all districts (check latest status). Makhana grown in wetlands, fish farming in flood-prone fields and fruit orchards on marginal land show that the environment can itself become a source of income.

Environment-friendly growth also creates jobs. Installing and maintaining solar panels, running waste-recycling units, reviving ponds, planting and caring for trees, and running eco-tourism around places like the Valmiki Tiger Reserve and the Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary all need workers. Such "green jobs" can be created in villages and small towns, close to where people live, which is exactly what a migration-hit state like Bihar needs.

Good policy turns these possibilities into reality. Bihar's Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali Abhiyan, launched in 2019, links water conservation, revival of ponds and wells, tree planting and renewable energy into one campaign. Environmental impact assessment of large projects, protection of wetlands, strict control of illegal sand mining and better drainage in cities are equally important. Above all, planning must give rivers space: building houses and roads on floodplains invites disaster, however strong the embankments are.

Critics argue that a poor state cannot afford to be green, and that environmental rules slow down industry. This argument is weaker today than ever. Clean energy is often cheaper than fossil fuel over its lifetime, and the cost of disasters, disease and lost harvests is far higher than the cost of prevention. The real choice is not between growth and environment, but between short-term growth that collapses and long-term growth that lasts.

Finally, development without destruction needs a change in values as well as technology. Bihar's own culture offers a guide. During Chhath, millions of people stand in rivers and ponds to offer prayers to the setting and rising sun, cleaning the ghats before the festival. This is a living tradition of respect for water and light. If that spirit is carried into everyday life — saving water, refusing single-use plastic, planting trees, protecting ponds — citizens become partners in sustainable growth, not just spectators.

Mahatma Gandhi is widely quoted as saying that "the earth provides enough to satisfy every man's needs, but not every man's greed." Bihar needs more roads, factories, power and jobs; that need is real and urgent. But if it builds them with science, plans them with care and shares their benefits fairly, the same state that today faces both flood and drought can become an example of how a developing region grows without destroying the ground beneath its feet. Growth and environment can go together. In fact, in the long run, neither can survive without the other.

## Why this essay works — annotated breakdown

| Para | What it does | Technique to copy |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opens with a striking **paradox**: drought and flood in the same state, same year; answers the question directly in the thesis ("The answer is no… but only if…") | For question topics, **answer the question** in the introduction, with conditions |
| 2 | Defines sustainable development (Brundtland, correctly quoted) and its three pillars | **Concept paragraph**; a concrete example (factory and river) to make it real |
| 3 | Global history and equity (CBDR) | **International dimension**; stable facts (1992, 2015) |
| 4 | India's response — net zero 2070, Mission LiFE | **National dimension**; positive but measured |
| 5 | Bihar's vulnerabilities | Several **Bihar facts** (Kusaha 2008, arsenic, heat, lightning, air); ends with the key argument: "ignoring environment is bad economics" |
| 6 | Technology as the bridge | **Science dimension**; list of solutions linked to Bihar's problems; a caveat ("does not remove the need for wise choices") |
| 7 | Agriculture | Applies the idea to Bihar's biggest sector; "check latest" honesty on a scheme; turns environment into income (makhana, fish) |
| 8 | Green jobs | **Economic dimension**; links to migration (a Bihar problem) |
| 9 | Policy and governance | Names one scheme with its year; adds EIA, sand mining, floodplain planning |
| 10 | Counter-view and rebuttal | Reframes the choice: "short-term vs long-term growth" — a quotable insight |
| 11 | Values and culture | **Cultural/ethical dimension** through Chhath — a fresh, rooted example |
| 12 | Conclusion | Quote carefully framed as "widely quoted"; restates thesis; final line goes one step further ("neither can survive without the other") |

### What makes it score

- **It answers the question.** "Can growth and environment go together?" — the answer appears in the introduction, is tested in the body and is restated, more strongly, in the conclusion.
- **Three themes integrated.** Economy (growth, jobs, agriculture), science (solar, satellites, irrigation) and environment (floods, climate, wetlands) appear together in most paragraphs.
- **Moves from global to local:** world → India → Bihar → village → individual.
- **Counter-argument handled with logic, not emotion.**
- **Fresh example:** Chhath as an environmental value is memorable and authentic.

> **Common mistake:** Listing "solutions" without connecting them to the problem. "Solar pumps" means more when tied to "costly diesel" and "drought in south Bihar". Always pair a solution with the specific problem it solves.

## Alternate outline 1 — "Scientific temper: the need of the hour"

*Type:* descriptive-argumentative. *Keywords:* scientific temper (a habit of questioning, evidence and reasoning), need of the hour (why now).

- **Intro idea:** A child in a Bihar village with a fever is taken to an exorcist instead of a health centre, while a few kilometres away students watch a live broadcast of a Chandrayaan launch. Thesis: India's real progress needs not just science, but a scientific way of thinking among all citizens.
- **Body points:**
  1. Meaning — questioning, evidence, openness to change; not opposition to faith but to blind belief.
  2. Constitutional basis — Article 51A(h): duty to develop scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry; Nehru's idea in *The Discovery of India*.
  3. Indian heritage of inquiry — Aryabhata at Kusumapura, Nalanda's debates, the Buddha's advice to test teachings rather than accept them blindly (a commonly cited teaching).
  4. Achievements — ISRO (Chandrayaan-3 landing, 23 August 2023), vaccines, Green Revolution, digital public infrastructure.
  5. Gaps — superstition, witch-hunting, quack medicine, fake news on social media, rote learning.
  6. Health dimension — vaccine hesitancy, self-medication; role of ASHAs.
  7. Economic dimension — innovation, start-ups, evidence-based policy.
  8. Counter-view — "science without ethics is dangerous" → scientific temper includes humility and ethics.
  9. Way forward — inquiry-based teaching, science fairs and labs in government schools, science communication in local languages, fact-checking, rewarding curiosity.
- **Bihar examples:** Aryabhata; Nalanda; Vashishtha Narayan Singh (mathematician from Bhojpur); school science labs; awareness drives against witch-hunting.
- **Quote:** Atal Bihari Vajpayee's addition to the slogan — "Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan, Jai Vigyan."
- **Conclusion idea:** Satellites reach the Moon; scientific temper must reach the village.

## Alternate outline 2 — "Food processing: the next green revolution for Bihar"

*Type:* descriptive with a policy argument. *Keywords:* food processing (turning farm produce into higher-value products), next green revolution (a big jump in farm incomes), Bihar.

- **Intro idea:** A litchi farmer in Muzaffarpur watches part of his crop rot within days of harvest, while a packet of litchi juice in a Delhi supermarket sells for many times the farm price. Thesis: Bihar's next leap will come not from growing more, but from processing what it grows.
- **Body points:**
  1. Bihar's farm strengths — makhana (most of India's output, GI tag), Shahi litchi (GI), maize, vegetables, fruit, honey, milk (COMFED – Sudha), fish.
  2. The problem — post-harvest losses, poor cold storage, distress sales, tiny holdings, middlemen.
  3. Economic logic — value addition raises farm incomes and creates rural jobs; food processing is labour-intensive.
  4. Social dimension — women's SHGs (JEEViKA) in pickles, papad, makhana packaging; reduced migration.
  5. Policy support — central PM-FME scheme for micro food enterprises; Bihar's industrial and food-processing incentives; One District One Product approach (check latest).
  6. Technology — cold chains, solar dryers, food testing labs, e-NAM and online sales.
  7. Lessons — Amul's cooperative model; Sudha in Bihar itself.
  8. Challenges — power, credit, quality standards, branding, transport, land for units.
  9. Counter-view — "processing helps companies, not farmers" → answer: FPOs and cooperatives keep value with farmers.
  10. Way forward — FPOs, cluster-based parks, cold chains, skill training, export links, quality certification.
- **Bihar examples:** Mithila makhana; Muzaffarpur litchi; Sudha dairy; JEEViKA women's enterprises.
- **Quote:** Lal Bahadur Shastri's slogan "Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan" — today the farmer also needs a factory gate.
- **Conclusion idea:** The first green revolution filled granaries; Bihar's processing revolution can fill farmers' pockets.

## Key terms

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Sustainable development | Development that meets today's needs without harming future generations |
| Net zero | Balancing the greenhouse gases a country emits with those it removes |
| CBDR | Common but differentiated responsibilities — all countries must act, but rich countries more |
| Mission LiFE | India's campaign for environment-friendly lifestyles (Lifestyle for Environment) |
| Green jobs | Jobs that protect or restore the environment |
| Zero tillage | Sowing seeds without ploughing, which saves water, fuel and soil |
| Floodplain | Low land next to a river that is naturally flooded |
| Scientific temper | A habit of thinking based on reason, evidence and questioning |
| Value addition | Processing a product so it sells for more |

## Practice

### Writing tasks

**Task 1 — Pair problems and solutions (15 minutes).** List five environmental problems of Bihar. Against each, write one technology and one policy solution.

**Task 2 — Full essay (60 minutes, timed).** Write Alternate outline 1 or 2 as a full essay of 900–1,000 words.

**Task 3 — Question-topic introduction (10 minutes).** Write introductions that directly answer these questions with conditions: (a) "Is artificial intelligence a threat to jobs?" (b) "Can Bihar become an industrial state?"

**Task 4 — Integration drill (15 minutes).** Write one paragraph on "solar energy in Bihar" that includes an economic, a scientific, an environmental and a social point.

**Self-check rubric (score each 0–2; total out of 20)**

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question answered clearly in intro and conclusion | No | Only once | Both, with conditions |
| Economy, science and environment integrated | One theme | Two | All three |
| Global → India → Bihar → local movement | None | Partly | Clear |
| Every solution tied to a specific problem | No | Some | All |
| Bihar examples (3–5) | < 2 | 2 | 3+ |
| Facts accurate; changing items flagged | Errors | Minor doubts | All safe |
| Counter-view handled logically | None | Weakly | Strongly |
| Values / culture / individual angle | None | Brief | Developed |
| Language: clear, short sentences | Confusing | Mostly clear | Clear |
| Time and word limit kept | No | Slightly over | Yes |
