A good essay is built, not just written. Toppers spend the first 8–10 minutes on each essay thinking and planning, and only then start writing. This chapter gives you a repeatable method: **decode → brainstorm → outline → introduce → develop → conclude**. Once you practise it 10–15 times, it becomes automatic in the exam hall.

> **In simple words:** First understand exactly what the topic asks. Then list ideas from many angles (social, economic, political, and so on). Arrange them in a sensible order. Open with something interesting, explain your ideas one paragraph at a time with examples, and end with a hopeful, practical way forward.

## Step 1 — Decode the topic

Read the topic three times. Then do three things.

1. **Underline the keywords.** In "Education of girls is the best investment for Bihar", the keywords are *education*, *girls*, *best investment* and *Bihar*. Your essay must talk about all four. An essay on "women empowerment in general" would miss "investment" and "Bihar".
2. **Identify the type.** Is it descriptive (explain an issue), argumentative (two sides, your view), or philosophical (an idea)? See [How the Essay Paper Works](/interview/bpsc-2027/essay/how-the-essay-paper-works).
3. **Write the "demand" in one line** in your rough space. For example: "Show why girls' education gives the highest returns — social, economic, health, political — with Bihar evidence, admit the barriers, suggest steps."

| Topic | Hidden demand |
|---|---|
| "Technology: a servant or a master?" | Both sides; when it serves and when it controls; who decides; your balanced view |
| "Bihar's migrant: a story of pain and hope" | Push factors (pain) and remittances, skills, success (hope); policy response |
| "Ends do not justify the means" | Meaning of ends and means; why means matter (Gandhi); counter-view; examples; today |
| "Floods in north Bihar: a natural or man-made disaster?" | Natural causes vs human causes (embankments, deforestation, silt); verdict: both; solutions |

> **Common mistake:** Reading "or" topics as one-sided. "Natural or man-made?" expects you to examine both and usually conclude "both, in different ways". A one-sided answer looks shallow.

## Step 2 — Brainstorm across dimensions

The fastest way to find content is to ask: **how does this topic look from different angles?** Use a fixed checklist so you never go blank.

| Dimension | Question to ask | Example for "Girls' education in Bihar" |
|---|---|---|
| Historical | What was the past? How did we get here? | Nalanda's learning tradition; low female literacy after independence |
| Social | How does it affect family, caste, gender, community? | Later marriage, more say in family decisions |
| Economic | Income, jobs, growth, costs? | Higher earnings; women in workforce; returns to the state economy |
| Political | Laws, policies, governance, participation? | 50% panchayat seats for women; women voters' turnout |
| Legal / constitutional | Which articles, acts, rights? | Article 21A (right to education); RTE Act 2009 |
| Health | Effects on health and nutrition? | Lower infant mortality; better child nutrition |
| Technological | Role of technology? | Digital classrooms; online scholarships |
| Environmental | Any link to nature, climate? | Educated women lead self-help groups in water and tree work |
| Cultural | Traditions, beliefs, art, language? | Attitudes about "paraya dhan"; changing norms |
| Ethical | Right and wrong, values, justice? | Equal dignity; justice to half the population |
| Individual | What does it mean for one person? | Confidence, choice, voice |
| Bihar / India / Global | Where are the examples? | Bihar cycle scheme; Kerala's experience; global evidence |

> **Memory trick:** **"Her Sister Eats Pickles; Little Tina Eats Cold Eggs Instantly"** — Historical, Social, Economic, Political, Legal, Technological, Environmental, Cultural, Ethical, Individual. Or simply draw the wheel in your rough space each time.

### Other quick brainstorming frames

- **Past – Present – Future:** what it was, what it is, what it should be.
- **Individual – Family – Society – State – World:** move outward from a person to the planet. Excellent for philosophical topics.
- **Causes – Effects – Responses – Way forward:** best for problem topics (floods, unemployment).
- **Arguments for – Arguments against – Synthesis:** best for debatable topics.
- **5W1H:** What, Why, Who, Where, When, How.

## Step 3 — Make the outline

Now arrange your scattered points into **8–12 body paragraphs** in a logical order. Write the outline in short phrases on the rough-work page — not full sentences. Cross out points you will not use.

### Choosing an order

| Order | Use when | Example flow |
|---|---|---|
| Chronological | Topic has a history | Ancient → colonial → after 1947 → today → future |
| Problem–solution | An issue topic | Meaning → scale → causes → effects → current efforts → gaps → solutions |
| Dimension-wise | Broad topic | Social → economic → political → environmental → ethical |
| Dialectic (for–against–synthesis) | Debatable topic | Case for → case against → evidence → balanced view |
| Concentric (micro to macro) | Philosophical topic | Individual → family → society → nation → humanity |

> **Remember:** Put your **strongest, most relevant** points early in the body and keep one powerful point for just before the conclusion. Examiners remember the start and the end best.

### A sample outline (topic: "Education of girls is the best investment for Bihar")

```text
Intro: story of a girl cycling to school in a Bihar village → thesis: girls' education gives
       the highest returns of any public spending.
1. Meaning of "investment": returns over time; education as human capital
2. Historical: Bihar's learning heritage (Nalanda) vs low female literacy (Census 2011)
3. Health returns: later marriage, fewer and healthier children, lower infant deaths
4. Economic returns: women's income, workforce, household savings, state growth
5. Social returns: less child marriage and dowry pressure, more voice at home
6. Political returns: 50% panchayat seats — educated women lead better
7. Bihar's steps: cycle and uniform schemes, Kanya Utthan, JEEViKA, 35% jobs (check latest)
8. Barriers: dropouts after class 8, safety, distance, poverty, social norms, quality
9. Counter-view: "jobs are few, so why educate?" → education's value beyond jobs
10. Way forward: secondary schools nearby, female teachers, toilets, skills, digital access
Conclusion: return to the girl on the cycle — she is Bihar's future; quote on women's progress.
```

## Step 4 — Write a strong introduction

The introduction is about **8–10% of the essay** — around 80–120 words in a 1,000-word essay. It has two jobs: (1) catch the reader's interest, and (2) tell the reader what the essay will argue.

### Six ways to open

| Opening type | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Story / anecdote | A short scene, real or clearly imagined | Social topics, Bihar topics |
| Quote | A well-known line you are sure of | Philosophical, value topics |
| Striking fact | One reliable fact or figure | Economy, health, environment |
| Question | A thought-provoking question | Debatable topics |
| Contrast | Past vs present, two pictures | Development, change topics |
| Definition | Explain a key concept simply | Abstract topics (but never as the only opening) |

> **Common mistake:** Starting with a dictionary definition such as "According to the Oxford dictionary, education means…". It is dull and every second candidate does it. Use a definition inside the second paragraph instead.

### Weak vs strong introduction

**Weak:**

> Education is very important. Girls' education is also very important. In Bihar girls are not educated. Government is doing many schemes. In this essay we will discuss girls' education.

Why it is weak: repetitive, no interest, no thesis, ends with "in this essay we will discuss" (a filler phrase), and a sweeping claim ("girls are not educated") that is not true.

**Strong:**

> Every morning, in villages across Bihar, groups of girls in school uniforms ride bicycles along narrow roads to their high schools. Twenty years ago, this sight was rare. It reflects a quiet revolution, because a girl who completes school marries later, earns more, raises healthier children and speaks with greater confidence in her family and village. For a state that is young, poor in income but rich in human energy, educating its daughters is not charity. It is the best investment Bihar can make.

Why it is strong: a vivid image, a contrast (then vs now), a chain of returns that previews the body, and a clear thesis in the last line.

> **In simple words:** The last sentence of the introduction should be your **thesis** — one clear sentence of what you believe about the topic. Everything in the body supports it.

## Step 5 — Develop the body

The body is about **80% of the essay**. Each paragraph carries **one idea**.

### The PEEL paragraph

| Letter | Part | Example (health returns of girls' education) |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | Point — topic sentence | "The first and quickest return is better health." |
| **E** | Evidence — fact, example, data | "Studies across India show that mothers with more schooling have lower infant deaths and better-nourished children." |
| **E** | Explanation — why it matters | "An educated mother understands vaccination, nutrition and hygiene, and visits health centres in time." |
| **L** | Link — connect to thesis or next point | "Healthier children grow into more productive adults, which brings us to the economic return." |

A PEEL paragraph is 80–120 words. Ten such paragraphs make a complete body.

### Transition words

Transitions make an essay flow. Learn a small set and use them naturally.

| Purpose | Transitions |
|---|---|
| Adding | Moreover, In addition, Furthermore, Equally important |
| Contrast | However, On the other hand, Yet, At the same time, Despite this |
| Cause / effect | Therefore, As a result, Consequently, This is why |
| Example | For instance, Take the case of, A good example is |
| Sequence | First, Next, Beyond this, Finally |
| Emphasis | Above all, Most importantly, Indeed |
| Concession | It is true that…, Admittedly, Critics argue that… |
| Concluding | In the end, Taken together, Thus |

### Balance: the counter-view paragraph

Every good essay has at least one paragraph that admits the other side or the limits of the argument. Then it answers them. This shows maturity.

> **Example:** "It is true that many educated young women in Bihar still do not find jobs, and some families ask why they should spend on a daughter's schooling. But this view measures education only by salary. An educated woman manages money better, keeps her children in school longer, and is harder to cheat or silence. The answer to too few jobs is more jobs, not less education."

### Evidence: how much and what kind

- **Examples beat data.** One concrete Bihar example often does more than three numbers.
- **Use data only if you are sure.** If you are not sure of an exact figure, write the trend: "Bihar's female literacy, the lowest among states in Census 2011, has been rising." Wrong numbers cost credibility.
- **Name schemes correctly.** Full name first, then short name.
- **Mix sources of examples:** Bihar, other states, India, other countries, history, literature, everyday life.

> **Bihar connection:** For every essay, aim for **at least three Bihar references** spread across the body — not all in one paragraph. Chapter 3 has a ready bank: [Building Content: Quotes, Data and Examples](/interview/bpsc-2027/essay/building-content-quotes-data-and-examples).

### Weak vs strong body paragraph

**Weak:**

> Women are getting reservation in panchayat. This is very good. Bihar gave 50% reservation. Now women are mukhiya. But husbands are doing work. This is called mukhiya pati. So reservation is not working.

**Strong:**

> Political participation is perhaps the most visible return. In 2006 Bihar became the first state to reserve **50% of panchayat seats** for women, well above the constitutional minimum of one-third. Critics point to the "mukhiya-pati" problem, where husbands act on behalf of elected wives. Yet with each election, more women attend meetings, handle files and speak for themselves — and the more schooling they have, the faster this happens. Education, therefore, turns reserved seats into real power.

The strong version has a clear topic sentence, a correct fact, the counter-view, a reasoned answer and a link back to the thesis (education).

## Step 6 — Write a hopeful, practical conclusion

The conclusion is about **8–10% of the essay**. It should leave the examiner with a clear and positive final impression.

A good conclusion does three things:

1. **Sums up** the main line of argument in one or two sentences (not a list of every point).
2. **Looks forward** — a vision or a practical way forward.
3. **Ends with a memorable line** — a callback to the opening story, a quote you are sure of, or a strong sentence of your own.

**Weak:**

> So we can say that girls' education is very important and government and society should work together. Then Bihar will develop.

**Strong:**

> The girl on the bicycle carries more than her schoolbag. She carries the health of her future children, the income of her family and the quality of Bihar's democracy. Building secondary schools within reach, hiring women teachers, making schools safe and linking education to skills will multiply these returns. Few investments repay a state so richly, and none so fairly. If Bihar educates its daughters, its daughters will build Bihar.

> **Common mistake:** Introducing a new issue in the conclusion ("Also, there is the problem of corruption…"). The conclusion closes the discussion; it does not open a new one.

## Special case: philosophical essays

Philosophical topics need a slightly different plan.

1. **Literal meaning** — what do the words say?
2. **Deeper meaning** — what value or idea lies under them?
3. **Origin or context** — only if you are sure (for example, a well-known teaching of Buddha or Gandhi).
4. **The individual** — how does the idea apply to a person's life? (a student, a farmer, an officer)
5. **Society and nation** — how does it apply to communities, institutions, governments?
6. **History and literature** — Ashoka after Kalinga; the freedom struggle; Gandhi at Champaran; characters from epics.
7. **Counter-view** — when does the idea fail or need limits?
8. **Relevance today** — for India, Bihar, public service, technology.
9. **Conclusion** — a balanced, inspiring takeaway.

> **Mains angle:** In a philosophical essay, connecting the idea to **public administration** (honesty in office, empathy for the poor, courage to say no) is a strong move in a civil services exam. It shows the examiner how you would think as an officer.

## Presentation rules

- Write the topic as the title, exactly as printed.
- **No headings inside the essay.** Paragraphs only.
- One blank line between paragraphs. 10–14 paragraphs for a full essay.
- Rarely, a simple flow line (A → B → C) inside a paragraph can help, but most toppers avoid diagrams in essays. Do not draw maps or charts.
- Underline only two to three key phrases per page.
- Cross out mistakes with a single neat line.

## Key terms

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Thesis | One sentence stating your main argument |
| Topic sentence | The first sentence of a paragraph that states its idea |
| PEEL | Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link — a paragraph pattern |
| Transition | A word or phrase that connects ideas (however, moreover) |
| Counter-view | The opposite argument, presented fairly and then answered |
| Dialectic | A flow of argument: case for, case against, then a synthesis |
| Synthesis | A balanced conclusion that combines the best of both sides |
| Anecdote | A short story used to open or illustrate a point |
| Callback | Returning in the conclusion to an image from the introduction |
| Concentric order | Moving from individual outward to family, society, nation, world |

## Practice

### Recall check

1. What are the six steps of the method?
2. What share of the essay should the introduction and conclusion each take?
3. What does PEEL stand for?
4. Which opening type should you avoid as the very first line, and why?
5. Name three brainstorming frames apart from the dimension wheel.
6. What should the last sentence of the introduction usually be?
7. Which order suits a philosophical topic?
8. What must a conclusion never do?

### Answers

1. Decode, brainstorm, outline, introduce, develop, conclude.
2. About 8–10% each.
3. Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link.
4. The dictionary definition — it is dull and overused.
5. Past–present–future; causes–effects–responses–way forward; for–against–synthesis (also 5W1H, individual-to-world).
6. The thesis statement.
7. Concentric order (individual → family → society → nation → world), after explaining literal and deeper meaning.
8. Introduce a new issue.

### Writing tasks

**Task 1 — Brainstorm (10 minutes).** Topic: "Floods in north Bihar: a natural or man-made disaster?" Draw the dimension wheel and write at least two points on each of eight spokes.

**Task 2 — Outline (10 minutes).** Turn your brainstorm into a 10-point outline using the problem–solution or dialectic order. Mark where your three Bihar examples go.

**Task 3 — Three introductions (20 minutes).** Write three different introductions (story, fact, question) of about 100 words each for the topic in Task 1. Choose the best.

**Task 4 — Two PEEL paragraphs (15 minutes).** Write one PEEL paragraph on "embankments" and one counter-view paragraph.

**Task 5 — Conclusion (10 minutes).** Write a conclusion that calls back to your chosen introduction.

**Self-check rubric**

- [ ] My outline covers at least six different dimensions.
- [ ] The topic's keywords ("natural", "man-made") appear in my thesis and conclusion.
- [ ] Each PEEL paragraph has one clear topic sentence and one concrete example.
- [ ] I wrote a fair counter-view and answered it.
- [ ] My introduction ends with a one-sentence thesis.
- [ ] My conclusion has a way forward and a memorable final line, with no new issue.
- [ ] I used at least four different transition words.
- [ ] Every fact I used is one I am sure of (or written as a trend).
