The Essay paper is the single biggest paper in BPSC Mains. It carries **300 marks** — exactly as much as GS Paper I or GS Paper II — and it decides your final rank as much as any GS paper. This chapter explains how the paper is set, what the examiner rewards, how to choose a topic in the exam hall and how to use your three hours.

> **In simple words:** In the Essay paper you write three long essays, one from each section, in three hours. Each essay is worth about 100 marks. The examiner wants a clear, balanced, well-organised essay with real examples — especially from Bihar — written in simple, correct English. You do not need fancy words. You need clear thinking.

## Where the essay fits in BPSC Mains

BPSC Mains has five papers. Only three of them count in your final merit. The table below shows the pattern of the **72nd CCE**, which this book assumes the **73rd CCE (BPSC 2027)** will follow. Confirm it against the official notification on **bpsc.bihar.gov.in** when it is released.

<div class="formula">

| Paper | Marks | Counted in merit? |
|---|---|---|
| General Hindi | 100 | No — qualifying (minimum 30%) |
| GS Paper I | 300 | Yes |
| GS Paper II | 300 | Yes |
| **Essay** | **300** | **Yes** |
| Optional subject (objective) | 100 | No — qualifying (for general posts) |
| Interview | 120 | Yes |
| **Final merit total** | **1,020** | GS I + GS II + Essay + Interview |

</div>

So the essay is **300 out of 1,020 marks — about 29% of your final merit.** That is more than the interview (120) and equal to each GS paper.

### Why the essay is a "rank decider"

- In GS papers, many candidates know the same facts, so scores bunch together.
- In the essay, there is no fixed answer key. Two candidates who know the same facts can score very differently. The one who **organises** ideas better, gives **better examples** and writes **more clearly** scores higher.
- A difference of even 20–30 marks in the essay can move you hundreds of places on the merit list. This is why serious candidates practise essays every week in the Mains phase.

> **Mains angle:** The essay was added to BPSC Mains in recent cycles (from the 68th CCE onward, as commonly reported — verify with the notification). Because it is still fairly new, many candidates under-prepare for it. That is your opportunity.

## The pattern of the paper

- **Three sections.** Each section gives you a few topics. You must write **one essay from each section** — three essays in all.
- **Time:** 3 hours for the whole paper.
- **Marks:** 300 in total; usually understood as about 100 per essay. Check the question paper for marks shown against each section.
- **Word limit:** The question paper states the expected length. Follow it exactly. If the paper does not state it, a length of roughly **700–1,000 words** per essay is a sensible target in three hours. (The model essays in this book are 1,000–1,200 words so that you can see more content; in the exam you will trim to the stated limit.)
- **Medium:** This book assumes you write in English. Whatever medium you choose, use it for the whole paper.

> **Remember:** Always read the instructions printed on the cover of the question-answer booklet. The number of topics per section, the word limit and the medium rules can change from one cycle to the next.

### What the three sections usually test

BPSC does not publish fixed section themes, and they may change. In general, topics in state PSC essay papers come from these families. Expect a mix across the three sections.

| Topic family | What it looks like | Example topic (in BPSC style, not a real PYQ) |
|---|---|---|
| Bihar-centric | Bihar's development, history, migration, floods, culture | "Bihar's journey from Nalanda to new growth" |
| Social issues | Women, caste, education, health, youth, family | "Educated women are the foundation of a strong society" |
| Economy | Agriculture, jobs, industry, poverty, inclusive growth | "Agriculture is still the backbone of Bihar" |
| Science and technology | Digital India, AI, space, health tech | "Technology: a servant or a master?" |
| Environment | Climate change, water, floods, sustainable development | "Development without destruction" |
| Polity and governance | Democracy, panchayats, corruption, rights and duties | "Grassroots democracy is real democracy" |
| Culture and values | Unity in diversity, language, festivals, heritage | "Culture is the soul of a nation" |
| Philosophical / quote-based | An abstract idea or a saying | "Character is the true wealth of a person" |

> **Bihar connection:** A state PSC wants officers who understand their state. In almost every essay — even a philosophical one — one or two Bihar examples (Nalanda, Champaran, JP, panchayat reservation for women, JEEViKA, Kosi floods, Chhath, Madhubani art) make your essay stand out. Chapter 3 gives you a full bank of such examples.

## How an essay differs from a GS answer

Many candidates write the essay like a long GS answer, with sub-headings and bullet points. That is a mistake. An essay is a **connected piece of prose** with a clear line of thought.

| Feature | GS answer | Essay |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 150–300 words | Several hundred to 1,000+ words |
| Style | Points, sub-headings, short paragraphs | Flowing paragraphs, joined by transitions |
| Bullet points | Welcome | Avoid (a short list inside a paragraph is fine) |
| Diagrams | Often useful | Rarely needed; usually avoid |
| Content | Direct facts on what is asked | Many dimensions: history, society, economy, ethics, future |
| Opening | One- or two-line intro | A real introduction that pulls the reader in |
| Your voice | Neutral | Balanced but thoughtful; your own reasoning shows |
| Judgement | "What do you know?" | "How do you think?" |

> **Common mistake:** Writing "Introduction:", "Body:", "Conclusion:" as headings, or turning the essay into 30 bullet points. Examiners read this as a lack of essay skill. Write paragraphs.

## What the examiner rewards

There is no public official marking scheme for the BPSC essay. But every essay examiner looks for broadly the same qualities. Think of them as the "seven pillars" of a good essay.

| Pillar | What it means | How you show it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Relevance | You answer the topic actually given | Define the topic; keep linking back to its keywords |
| 2. Structure | Clear intro, logical body, strong conclusion | Plan before writing; one idea per paragraph |
| 3. Depth | You go beyond the obvious | Ask "why?" and "so what?"; give causes and effects |
| 4. Many dimensions | You look from several angles | Social, economic, political, cultural, environmental, ethical, Bihar, global |
| 5. Balance | You see both sides | Admit problems and limits; then give your reasoned view |
| 6. Evidence | Your claims are backed | Examples, data (with care), schemes, history, case stories |
| 7. Language | Simple, correct, readable English | Short sentences; no errors; neat handwriting |

> **In simple words:** An examiner asks three questions while reading: "Did this person understand the topic? Did they think deeply and fairly? Can they express it clearly?" If all three answers are yes, you score well.

### What pulls marks down

- Going off-topic (writing a prepared essay on "women" when the topic is about "education of girls and the economy").
- Only praising or only criticising — no balance.
- Wrong facts, wrong data, or quotes given to the wrong person.
- A weak, abrupt ending — often because time ran out.
- Repeating the same point in different words to fill pages.
- Political party propaganda or abuse of any group. Be fair and respectful at all times.
- Very poor handwriting, crossed-out paragraphs, no paragraph breaks.

## Kinds of essay topics and how to treat them

### 1. Descriptive topics

Example: "Floods in Bihar." The topic asks you to **explain an issue**: what it is, why it happens, its effects, what is being done and what more can be done. The usual flow is: background → causes → impacts → responses → way forward.

### 2. Argumentative or debatable topics

Example: "Is prohibition the right way to social reform?" Here the topic invites **two sides**. Present both fairly, then give your reasoned judgement. The usual flow is: context → arguments for → arguments against → what evidence shows → a balanced position → way forward.

### 3. Philosophical or abstract topics

Example: "Ends do not justify the means." The topic is an idea, not an issue. First explain the **literal meaning**, then the **deeper meaning**, then test it with examples from the individual, society, the nation, history and literature, and finally apply it to today. These topics separate toppers from average writers, because they need thinking, not just facts.

### 4. Quote-based topics

Example: "Where the mind is without fear…" Treat it like a philosophical topic. Explain who said it and in what context only if you are sure; otherwise focus on the meaning.

> **Mains angle:** Philosophical topics often have fewer takers. If you have practised them, they can give you a wider choice and less competition. But choose one only if you can think of at least six solid paragraphs on it.

## How to choose your topic in the exam hall

You may face three to four topics in each section. Spend the **first 10 minutes** of the paper reading all of them and choosing. Do not change your choice after you start writing.

Use this quick test for each topic you are considering:

1. **Do I understand exactly what it asks?** If the keywords are unclear to you, drop it.
2. **Can I list 8–10 points in 2 minutes?** Jot them in the margin. If you reach only four, drop it.
3. **Do I have examples?** At least three concrete examples, one from Bihar.
4. **Can I be balanced?** Can I see both sides?
5. **Can I write a strong conclusion?** A hopeful, practical way forward.

Pick the topic that scores best on these five tests — **not** the one that sounds most impressive.

> **Common mistake:** Choosing a "safe-looking" topic like "Women empowerment" and then writing a general essay that does not match the exact wording (for example, "Women empowerment begins at home"). The exact wording decides the content. Underline the keywords before you plan.

## Your three-hour time plan

<div class="formula">

| Time | Task |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:10 | Read all topics in all three sections; choose one per section; jot quick points beside each |
| For each essay (about 56 minutes) | |
| — 8–10 minutes | Brainstorm dimensions and write a short outline (on the rough-work page) |
| — 42–44 minutes | Write the essay |
| — 2–3 minutes | Read through; fix spelling, missing words, add a link sentence if needed |
| Last 2 minutes | Check that all three essays are complete and properly numbered |

</div>

Order of writing: start with the essay you are **most confident** about. It builds momentum and secures marks early. Keep the hardest topic for the middle, not the end, so that tiredness does not hurt it most. Many candidates prefer: confident → hardest → medium.

> **Remember:** A finished essay of 800 words scores more than an unfinished essay of 1,100 words. Watch the clock. If you are running late, shorten the remaining body paragraphs but **always write the conclusion**.

### How to estimate word count quickly

Count the words on 5 normal lines of your handwriting and divide by 5. If you write about 10 words per line and a page has 22 lines, then one page ≈ 220 words, and 800 words ≈ 3.5–4 pages. Learn your own number during practice so that you never need to count in the exam.

## Language and presentation

- **Simple English is best.** The examiner is not testing vocabulary. Short, clear, correct sentences score better than long, confused ones.
- **One idea per paragraph.** Leave a line between paragraphs.
- **Neat handwriting.** If your writing is hard to read, slow down a little. A readable essay always beats a messy one.
- **Underlining:** You may underline a few key phrases (one or two per page). Do not underline whole lines.
- **No slang, no SMS language, no abbreviations** like "govt", "w.r.t.", "&". Write words in full. Use the full name of a scheme the first time, then the short form.
- **Title:** Write the topic exactly as given at the top of the essay.

> **Memory trick:** **"R-S-D-B-E-L"** — Relevance, Structure, Depth, Balance, Evidence, Language. Check your essay against these six letters before you submit.

## Key terms

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Essay | A connected piece of writing that explores one topic in depth |
| Merit | The final ranking; built from GS I, GS II, Essay and Interview |
| Qualifying paper | A paper you must pass but whose marks are not added to merit |
| Descriptive topic | A topic that asks you to explain an issue |
| Argumentative topic | A topic with two sides that asks for your reasoned view |
| Philosophical topic | A topic based on an abstract idea or value |
| Dimension | An angle from which you look at a topic (social, economic, etc.) |
| Outline | A short plan of the essay's paragraphs, made before writing |
| Way forward | Practical suggestions for the future, usually near the end |
| Keyword | The important word or phrase in a topic that decides its exact meaning |

## Practice

### Recall check

1. How many marks does the Essay paper carry, and is it counted in merit?
2. How many essays do you write, and how are they chosen?
3. What share of the final 1,020 marks does the essay make up?
4. Name the four kinds of essay topics.
5. How many minutes should you spend at the start choosing topics?
6. Name the six checks in "R-S-D-B-E-L".
7. Why should you avoid sub-headings in an essay?
8. What must you always write even if time is short?

### Answers

1. 300 marks; yes, it is counted in merit.
2. Three essays — one from each of the three sections.
3. About 29%.
4. Descriptive, argumentative, philosophical, quote-based.
5. About 10 minutes.
6. Relevance, Structure, Depth, Balance, Evidence, Language.
7. An essay is connected prose; headings and bullets make it look like a GS answer and break the flow.
8. The conclusion.

### Writing tasks

**Task 1 — Topic decoding (15 minutes).** For each of these topics, underline the keywords, write the topic type (descriptive / argumentative / philosophical) and one sentence on what it really asks:
(a) "Migration from Bihar: compulsion or choice?" (b) "Floods in north Bihar." (c) "Simple living, high thinking." (d) "Technology has made us connected but lonely."

**Task 2 — The 2-minute test (10 minutes).** Take the four topics above. For each, give yourself exactly 2 minutes to list as many points as you can. Which topic would you choose in the exam, and why?

**Task 3 — Find your word rate (10 minutes).** Copy any paragraph from this chapter by hand at your normal exam speed for 5 minutes. Count the words. Work out your words per minute and words per page.

**Self-check rubric**

- [ ] I identified the topic type correctly for all four topics.
- [ ] My one-line "what it asks" uses the topic's own keywords.
- [ ] In the 2-minute test, I reached at least 8 points for the topic I chose.
- [ ] My chosen topic has at least one Bihar example in the list.
- [ ] I know my words per page and how many pages 800 words fill.
- [ ] I wrote at 15+ words per minute (if not, practise speed daily for 10 minutes).
