BPSC Prelims gives **+1** for a correct answer and takes away **1/3 mark** for a wrong one. There is also a compulsory fifth option, **E**, and a rule that punishes a row left completely blank. These rules decide whether a good candidate clears Prelims or misses by two marks. This chapter teaches the arithmetic with worked examples, shows when guessing is smart, and gives you a safe OMR routine.

> **In simple words:** Right = +1. Wrong = −1/3. Mark E when you choose not to answer = 0. Leave the row empty = −1/3, the same as a wrong answer. So **fill every row**, and guess only when you can remove at least one option.

## The rules (72nd CCE advertisement)

The official text, in short:

1. Each question has **five options: A, B, C, D and E**. You must darken **one** option for each question.
2. The correct answer is one of **A, B, C or D**.
3. If you **do not want to answer** a question, you **must** darken **E**.
4. If you darken **none** of the five options, that question is treated as **un-answered**, and **1/3 mark is deducted**.
5. Each **wrong** answer carries **1/3 mark negative marking**.
6. Choose only **one** answer for each question.

<div class="formula">

| What you do on the OMR row | Marks |
|---|---|
| Darken the correct option (A–D) | **+1** |
| Darken a wrong option (A–D) | **−1/3 ≈ −0.33** |
| Darken **E** (not answering) | **0** |
| Leave the row fully blank | **−1/3** |
| Darken two or more options | Treat as wrong/invalid: expect **−1/3** (check your OMR instructions) |

</div>

> **Remember:** These rules come from the 72nd advertisement (May 2026). Secondary reports describe the same thing. The 73rd notification may change the wording. Read the instructions on **your** notification, admit card and OMR sheet, and practise with that exact rule in every mock.

### Why option E exists

Without E, an examiner cannot tell whether a blank row was left on purpose or filled in later by someone else. With a compulsory E, **every row must have a mark**. This protects the OMR from tampering and makes every decision yours. It also means a moment of carelessness (forgetting a row) now costs you 1/3 mark.

### E used to mean something else

In some earlier CCE papers, coaching summaries describe option E as an answer like "More than one of the above / None of the above". Under the 72nd rule, E is **not an answer**; it means "I am not attempting this". A question can still have "More than one of the above" or "None of the above" as option **C** or **D**. Read options carefully.

## The basic formula

<div class="formula">

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Score | R − (W + B) ÷ 3 |
| R | number of right answers |
| W | number of wrong answers |
| B | number of fully blank rows |
| E-marked questions | add 0 |
| Check | R + W + B + E = 150 |

</div>

### Worked example 1 — the standard case

You attempt 100 questions. 70 are right, 30 are wrong. You mark E for the other 50.

- Right: 70 × 1 = **70**
- Wrong: 30 × 1/3 = **10** deducted
- E: 50 × 0 = 0
- **Score = 70 − 10 = 60**

### Worked example 2 — the same paper, but you forgot to fill E

Same 70 right and 30 wrong, but you left the 50 unattempted rows **blank** instead of marking E.

- Right: 70
- Wrong + blank: (30 + 50) ÷ 3 = 80 ÷ 3 ≈ **26.67** deducted
- **Score ≈ 43.33**

Forgetting E cost you **16.67 marks** — more than enough to fail. This is the single most expensive mistake you can make.

### Worked example 3 — attempting more

Candidate A attempts 90, gets 72 right, 18 wrong, marks E for 60.
Score = 72 − 18/3 = 72 − 6 = **66**.

Candidate B attempts 130, gets 88 right, 42 wrong, marks E for 20.
Score = 88 − 42/3 = 88 − 14 = **74**.

B made far more mistakes but still scored 8 more. More attempts helped **because B's extra answers were mostly right** (B got 16 more right and 24 more wrong on 40 extra questions: 16 − 24/3 = 16 − 8 = +8).

### Worked example 4 — attempting too much

Candidate C attempts all 150: 85 right, 65 wrong.
Score = 85 − 65/3 ≈ 85 − 21.67 = **63.33**.

C answered more than B but scored lower, because the last 20 attempts were nearly pure guesses (C: 85 right; B: 88 right on fewer questions).

### Worked example 5 — the break-even accuracy

When does attempting one more question **increase** your score on average? If p is your chance of being right:

Expected gain = p × 1 − (1 − p) × 1/3

This is above zero when p > 1/4, that is, **when you are more than 25% sure**.

| Your chance of being right | Expected marks from attempting | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| 100% (you know it) | +1.00 | Answer |
| 75% | 0.75 − 0.25 × 1/3 ≈ **+0.67** | Answer |
| 50% (two options left) | 0.50 − 0.50 × 1/3 ≈ **+0.33** | Answer |
| 33% (three options left) | 0.33 − 0.67 × 1/3 ≈ **+0.11** | Small gain — answer if your elimination is genuine |
| 25% (pure guess among four) | 0.25 − 0.75 × 1/3 = **0.00** | No gain on average, adds risk — mark E |
| below 25% (confused, two "traps") | negative | Mark E |

> **Memory trick:** "**Cut one, take a shot. Cut none, mark E.**" If you can honestly remove at least one wrong option, the guess is worth it on average.

### Worked example 6 — a run of 50/50 guesses

You have 20 questions where you are stuck between two options. If you answer all 20, on average you get 10 right and 10 wrong:

10 − 10/3 ≈ 10 − 3.33 = **+6.67 marks**.

If you mark E on all 20, you get 0. So well-founded 50/50 guesses are worth **about 6–7 marks per 20 questions** — often the difference between selection and failure.

### Worked example 7 — the danger of "pure" guesses

You blindly guess 30 questions where you know nothing. Expected right = 30 × 1/4 = 7.5; wrong = 22.5.
Expected marks = 7.5 − 22.5/3 = 7.5 − 7.5 = **0**.

The average is zero, but the result can swing either way by several marks. Blind guessing adds **luck** but no expected marks. Most candidates over-estimate their luck.

### The three-round method in the exam hall

1. **Round 1 (about 60–65 min):** Go through all 150. Answer every question you are sure of. Put a small mark in the question booklet (not the OMR) beside questions that are "50/50" or "need time". Skip the rest.
2. **Round 2 (about 30–35 min):** Return to the marked questions. Use elimination. Answer the ones where you can remove at least one option.
3. **Round 3 (last 15–20 min):** Darken **E** for every remaining question. Then check that **no row is empty**, that the question-booklet series is filled, and your roll number is correct.

> **Common mistake:** Filling the OMR only at the end. If time runs out, you may leave 40 rows blank and lose about 13 marks. Fill the OMR **as you go** (for example, after every page or every 25 questions), and keep the last 10 minutes for E-filling and checking.

### Elimination tricks that work

- **Extreme words** like "only", "always", "never", "all" make a statement more likely to be false (not always — but a useful hint).
- **Statement questions:** If you are sure statement 1 is wrong, remove every option that includes 1. Often this leaves two options.
- **Match-the-following:** If you are sure of one or two pairs, many options disappear.
- **Bihar questions:** Use your geographical sense. A river in south Bihar cannot be a tributary joining the Ganga from the north.
- **Number questions:** Approximate. A wrong option that is far away from your estimate can be removed.

### When **not** to guess

- You find two options that both look right and you cannot decide between them for any reason other than a "feeling" — that is still 50/50, so a guess is fine.
- But: you have **no idea** at all, or the question is a fact you never studied (a new index rank, an obscure place) — mark E.
- You are **already safely above** your target (from mocks, you know your accuracy is high) — reduce risky guesses; protect the score.
- You are **below** your target — take more well-founded 50/50 guesses; you need the expected gain.

## Cut-off thinking (qualitative)

### Two different "cut-offs"

1. **Minimum qualifying marks** (official, fixed in the notification): **General 40%** (60/150), **BC 36.5%** (≈ 54.75), **EBC 34%** (51), **SC/ST, women and PwD 32%** (48). You must score at least this.
2. **Actual cut-off** for Mains: BPSC selects about **10 times the vacancies**, category-wise. The actual cut-off is the score of the last candidate in that list. It is usually **higher** than the minimum, and it changes every year with the difficulty of the paper and the number of posts.

### How to think about it

- **Do not chase a published number.** Cut-offs of past cycles are on the BPSC result notices. They move up and down with paper difficulty and vacancies. Coaching "expected cut-offs" are guesses.
- **Set a safe personal target.** A sensible approach: from Phase 4 mocks, aim to score clearly above the recent cut-off band for your category, with a cushion of several marks, because mock papers and the real paper differ.
- **Fewer posts → higher cut-off** (fewer seats for the 10× list). **Harder paper → lower cut-off** (everyone scores less).
- **Prelims marks do not count later.** Clearing by one mark is as good as clearing by fifty. Your aim is **safe qualification**, not a high Prelims score. So avoid wild risk-taking.

> **Prelims angle:** Your score depends on two numbers: **accuracy** (right ÷ attempted) and **attempts**. In Phase 4, track both in every mock. If accuracy is above about 75%, you can usually attempt more; if it is below about 60%, attempt less and study more.

### Worked example 8 — reading your mock sheet

| Mock | Attempted | Right | Wrong | Score | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 95 | 62 | 33 | 62 − 11 = **51** | 65% |
| 2 | 110 | 78 | 32 | 78 − 10.67 ≈ **67.3** | 71% |
| 3 | 125 | 84 | 41 | 84 − 13.67 ≈ **70.3** | 67% |
| 4 | 118 | 88 | 30 | 88 − 10 = **78** | 75% |

What the sheet tells you: Mock 3 attempted more but accuracy fell, so the gain was small. Mock 4 is the best balance: fewer attempts than mock 3 but higher accuracy. Your goal is to move to the "mock 4" pattern: answer what you know, guess only where you can eliminate.

## OMR safety routine

<div class="formula">

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| Before starting | Fill roll number, booklet series and other boxes exactly as instructed. Use the pen type stated on the admit card. |
| During | Darken bubbles fully and neatly. No tick marks, no half-filling. |
| Every page / 25 questions | Transfer answers to the OMR. Check that question number = row number. |
| Last 10 minutes | Darken **E** in every remaining row. Scan all 150 rows for blanks. |
| Never | Darken two bubbles; use whitener or an eraser (usually banned); write anything extra on the OMR. |

</div>

> **Common mistake:** A one-row shift. You skip question 41 in the booklet but fill question 42's answer in row 41, and every answer after that is in the wrong row. Check the row number every time you return to a skipped question.

## How to practise this before the exam

- From your **first** full-length mock (Phase 4, from about Day 246), use the exact rule: +1, −1/3, E = 0, blank = −1/3.
- Use a printed OMR sheet with five bubbles A–E. Practise filling E at the end.
- Keep a mock analysis sheet (see [Revision and Mock Test Strategy](/interview/bpsc-2027/exam-guide/revision-and-mock-test-strategy)) with columns for "right by knowledge", "right by guess", "wrong by guess", "wrong by careless reading".
- After 6–8 mocks, compute your **guess accuracy**. If your 50/50 guesses are right more than about 40% of the time, keep guessing at that level. If they are right less than 25% of the time, your "eliminations" are not real — be stricter.

## Key points

- Score = R − (W + B)/3. E adds 0. A blank row costs the same as a wrong answer.
- Example: 100 attempted, 70 right → 70 − 30/3 = **60**. Forgetting to mark E for the other 50 drops it to about 43.
- A guess is worth it when you are more than 25% sure, that is, when you can remove at least one option. A 50/50 guess is worth about +1/3 mark on average.
- Fill the OMR as you go; keep the last 10 minutes for E and checking.
- Minimum qualifying marks: 40% / 36.5% / 34% / 32%. The actual cut-off comes from selecting about 10 × vacancies and changes every year.
- Prelims marks do not count in merit: aim for safe qualification, not a high score.

## Practice

1. Calculate the score for: (a) 120 attempted, 80 right; (b) 105 attempted, 79 right; (c) 140 attempted, 85 right, with 10 rows accidentally left blank. (Answers: (a) 80 − 40/3 ≈ 66.67; (b) 79 − 26/3 ≈ 70.33; (c) 85 − (55 + 10)/3 ≈ 63.33.)
2. Print a 150-row OMR with options A–E. In your next practice set, time yourself filling E for all unattempted rows and checking for blanks. Note how many minutes it took.
3. Write your personal guessing rule on one card (for example: "Answer if sure. Guess only after removing at least one option. Otherwise E.") and read it before every mock.
4. After your first three mocks, fill in a table like Worked example 8 and decide whether you should attempt more or fewer questions.
5. When the 73rd notification arrives, read its marking rules and note any change from this chapter.
