The Revolt of 1857 was the biggest armed challenge to British rule in the 19th century. BPSC asks about it almost every year: centres and leaders, British commanders, dates, and above all **Veer Kunwar Singh of Jagdishpur**. In Mains you may get "Assess the role of Bihar in the Revolt of 1857" or "Was 1857 the first war of Indian independence?"

> **In simple words:** In May 1857 Indian soldiers (sepoys) of the Company's Bengal Army rebelled. Princes who had lost their states, landlords who had lost their land, and peasants and artisans joined them in north and central India. They made the old Mughal emperor their leader. The British crushed the revolt by 1858–59. After it, the British Crown took over India from the Company.

## Causes

The cartridge was only the spark. The fuel had collected for a hundred years.

### Political causes

- **Doctrine of Lapse** (Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur) and the **annexation of Awadh (1856)** on the ground of "misgovernment".
- Dalhousie refused the pension of **Nana Saheb** (adopted son of Peshwa Baji Rao II).
- The British announced that after **Bahadur Shah Zafar** the Mughal family would have to leave the **Red Fort** and would lose the title of emperor.
- Indian rulers felt no state was safe.

### Economic causes

- **Heavy land revenue**; in Awadh, the **taluqdars** (big landlords) lost estates in the summary settlement of 1856.
- Ruin of **artisans and handicrafts**; loss of jobs for those who served Indian courts.
- Peasant **indebtedness**; sale of land by courts to moneylenders.
- Indians were kept out of high jobs.

### Social and religious causes

- Fear of **conversion** to Christianity; activity of missionaries.
- Laws seen as interference: abolition of sati (1829), **Widow Remarriage Act (1856)**, the **Religious Disabilities Act (1850)** (allowed a convert to inherit his ancestral property).
- Racial arrogance of the British.

### Military causes

- Indian sepoys were about **five-sixths** of the Company's army but could not rise above the rank of **subedar**. Pay was low.
- **General Service Enlistment Act (1856)** (Canning): new recruits could be sent **overseas** (crossing the sea was against the caste beliefs of many high-caste sepoys).
- Loss of **foreign service allowance (bhatta)** when Sindh and Punjab became part of British India.
- About **75,000 sepoys** of the Bengal Army came from **Awadh** (the commonly quoted figure), and many from **Bihar** (the "Purbiya" soldiers of Bhojpur and Shahabad). The annexation of Awadh hurt their families directly.

### The immediate cause

- The **Enfield rifle** cartridges had to be bitten before loading. A rumour spread that they were greased with **cow and pig fat** — offensive to both Hindus and Muslims.

> **Prelims angle:** Distinguish the **immediate cause** (greased cartridges, Enfield rifle) from the long-term causes. The Act that made sepoys liable to serve overseas was the **General Service Enlistment Act, 1856**.

## The revolt in Bihar

> **Bihar connection:** This whole section is a core BPSC topic. Read it together with [Modern Bihar and 1857](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/modern-bihar-and-1857).

### Early events

- **Rohini (near Deoghar, now in Jharkhand)**, June 1857: soldiers attacked British officers — usually counted as the first outbreak in the Bihar region.
- **Patna**: the Commissioner **William Tayler** arrested suspected leaders. **Peer Ali**, a bookseller of Patna and organiser of the rebels, led an attack on **3 July 1857** in which a British officer (Dr Lyall) was killed. Peer Ali was **hanged on 7 July 1857** with several companions. He refused to name other rebels in exchange for his life.
- **Waris Ali**, a police jamadar in Tirhut (Muzaffarpur), was arrested and hanged in 1857 for links with the rebels; he is often called one of the first martyrs of 1857 in Bihar.
- Unrest spread to **Gaya, Rajgir, Muzaffarpur, Saran, Champaran** and the Chotanagpur region (Hazaribagh, Ranchi, Palamu).

### Veer Kunwar Singh (c. 1777–1858)

- **Zamindar of Jagdishpur** in the old **Shahabad district** (now **Bhojpur district**), a Ujjainiya Rajput. He was about **80 years old** in 1857.
- His estate was in debt and the British revenue board had taken over its management — a personal grievance added to the general anger.
- **25 July 1857**: three Indian regiments at **Danapur** rebelled and marched to **Arrah**. Kunwar Singh took their command.
- **Siege of Arrah (27 July–3 August 1857)**: a small group of Europeans and loyal Sikh soldiers held out in a fortified building ("the Arrah House") defended by **Herwald Wake** and the railway engineer **Vicars Boyle**.
- **Captain Dunbar's** relief force from Danapur was **ambushed and defeated** (29–30 July 1857) — a major British loss.
- **Major Vincent Eyre** defeated Kunwar Singh near Bibiganj and relieved Arrah (3 August). The British then captured **Jagdishpur** (mid-August 1857) and destroyed his palace and temple.
- Kunwar Singh did not give up. He began a long **guerrilla campaign** across north and central India: **Rewa, Banda, Kalpi, Kanpur, Lucknow** (he met the Lucknow rebels) and **Azamgarh** (he captured Azamgarh in **March 1858** and defeated British forces there).
- **Crossing the Ganga** at **Shivpur Ghat** (near Ballia), he was hit in the wrist by a British bullet. He **cut off his own arm with his sword and offered it to the Ganga**, so that the poison would not spread.
- **23 April 1858**: back at **Jagdishpur**, he **defeated Captain Le Grand's** force and recaptured his home.
- He **died on 26 April 1858**, three days after the victory, from his wound.
- His brother **Amar Singh** continued the fight for months, and companions like **Hare Krishna Singh** and **Nishan Singh** fought on.

> **Remember:** **23 April** is celebrated in Bihar as **Veer Kunwar Singh Vijayotsav** (victory day). **Veer Kunwar Singh University** is at **Arrah**. He is one of the very few leaders of 1857 who was **never defeated in his final battle** and **died a free man**.

> **Mains angle:** Why is Kunwar Singh special? (1) Age — fought at about 80; (2) guerrilla tactics — he moved across UP and central India, tying down British forces; (3) Hindu–Muslim unity in his forces; (4) he won his last battle (23 April 1858); (5) he turned a local sepoy mutiny in Bihar into a people's struggle in Shahabad. Use this as the Bihar angle in any 1857 answer.

### Chotanagpur and Santhal areas

- At **Ranchi and Hazaribagh**, sepoys rebelled in mid-1857. **Thakur Vishwanath Shahdeo** and **Pandey Ganpat Rai** led the rebellion; both were captured and hanged in 1858.
- In **Palamu**, the brothers **Nilambar and Pitambar** (Kharwar/Bhogta) led a tribal revolt (they were later hanged).
- These areas are now in Jharkhand but were part of Bihar's history until 2000 — BPSC still asks about them.

## Why the revolt failed

1. **Limited area**: mainly the Gangetic plain and central India. **Punjab, Bengal (east), most of South India, Rajputana** stayed largely quiet.
2. **Many rulers helped the British**: **Scindia** of Gwalior (and his minister Dinkar Rao), **Holkar** of Indore, the **Nizam** (minister **Salar Jung**), the rulers of Patiala, Nabha, Jind (Sikh states), **Kashmir**, and Nepal (Jung Bahadur sent Gurkha troops). Canning called such rulers "**breakwaters to the storm**".
3. **No unified leadership or common plan**; leaders fought for their own lost rights; Bahadur Shah was old and weak.
4. **No clear vision** of a future political system.
5. **Weapons**: rebels had old guns; the British had Enfield rifles, artillery and the **telegraph** for quick communication.
6. **Modern educated Indians** (Bengal intelligentsia) did not support it; they hoped for progress under British rule.
7. **Superior British resources**: troops from Britain and from the Crimean War; control of the sea; money.

## Nature of the revolt: the debate

| View | Who said it |
|---|---|
| "A **sepoy mutiny**", nothing more | John Seeley ("a wholly unpatriotic and selfish sepoy mutiny"), Sir John Lawrence, many British officials |
| "A **national revolt**" | Benjamin **Disraeli** (British politician) |
| "A **Muslim conspiracy**" | Sir James **Outram**, W. Taylor |
| "**First War of Indian Independence**" | **V.D. Savarkar** — book *The Indian War of Independence, 1857* (**1909**) |
| "**Neither first, nor national, nor a war of independence**" | **R.C. Majumdar** |
| "What began as a fight for religion ended as a war of independence" | **S.N. Sen** — *Eighteen Fifty-Seven* (1957, official centenary history) |
| "A struggle of soldiers and peasants against foreign rule" | Marxist historians |

**Balanced view for your answer:** It began as a sepoy mutiny but soon became a **popular revolt** of peasants, artisans and dispossessed rulers in north India, with remarkable **Hindu–Muslim unity**. It was not "national" in the modern sense (no idea of a nation-state, limited area), but it was the **first big united challenge** to British rule and later inspired the freedom struggle.

> **Prelims angle:** Savarkar's book = 1909, "First War of Indian Independence". R.C. Majumdar = "neither first, nor national, nor war of independence". S.N. Sen = official centenary history. These author–view matches are favourite questions.

## Results of the revolt

1. **End of Company rule**: the **Government of India Act, 1858** — rule by the British Crown through a **Secretary of State**; **Governor-General became Viceroy**.
2. **Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1 November 1858)**, read by Canning at **Allahabad**:
   - No more annexation; the **Doctrine of Lapse was ended** (right of adoption accepted).
   - Respect for the rights, dignity and honour of **native princes**.
   - **Religious tolerance** — no interference in religion.
   - Equal treatment in government jobs "as far as may be".
   - Pardon to rebels except those who had killed British subjects.
3. **Army reorganisation** (**Peel Commission**): more European soldiers (ratio of Europeans to Indians raised; commonly given as about 1:2 in the Bengal Army), artillery only with Europeans, "martial races" recruitment (Sikhs, Gurkhas, Pathans) and less from Awadh and Bihar; regiments mixed by caste and region to prevent unity.
4. **Divide and rule** policy deepened; Muslims were first blamed, later Hindus.
5. **Princes became loyal allies** of the British (they had helped crush the revolt).
6. **End of the Mughal dynasty** and the Peshwa title.
7. **Economic exploitation increased**; Indians' share of costs of suppression added to the public debt.
8. **Inspiration** for the later national movement — Kunwar Singh, Lakshmibai and others became symbols.

> **Common mistake:** The Queen's Proclamation was read at **Allahabad**, not Delhi. The Delhi Durbar of **1877** (Lytton) proclaimed Victoria **Empress of India** (Royal Titles Act 1876) — a different event.

## Key terms

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Sepoy | Indian soldier in the Company's army (from Persian *sipahi*) |
| Taluqdar | Big landlord in Awadh |
| Purbiya | "Easterner" — sepoys from Awadh and Bihar in the Bengal Army |
| Bhatta | Extra allowance for service outside one's home region |
| Guerrilla war | Fighting with small mobile groups, sudden attacks and retreats |
| Summary settlement | Quick land revenue settlement in Awadh (1856) that took land from taluqdars |
| Martial races | British theory that some communities were "naturally" good soldiers |
| Proclamation | Official public announcement by the ruler |

### Mains questions

**1. Assess the role of Bihar in the Revolt of 1857. (38 marks, ~300 words)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Bihar was one of the main theatres of the revolt; its sepoys (Purbiya) formed a large part of the Bengal Army.
- **Early resistance:** Rohini (June 1857); Patna — Peer Ali's rising and execution (July 1857); Waris Ali in Tirhut; Commissioner Tayler's repression.
- **Danapur mutiny (25 July)** and **Kunwar Singh's** leadership at Arrah; defeat of Dunbar's force.
- **Guerrilla campaign** across Rewa, Kalpi, Lucknow, Azamgarh — showed strategic skill; the Ganga crossing at Shivpur Ghat.
- **Final victory at Jagdishpur (23 April 1858)**; death 26 April; Amar Singh continued.
- **Chotanagpur:** Vishwanath Shahdeo, Ganpat Rai, Nilambar–Pitambar — tribal participation.
- **Features:** Hindu–Muslim unity; peasant and zamindar participation; long duration in Shahabad.
- **Limits:** many zamindars (e.g. Darbhanga, Hathwa) stayed loyal; lack of coordination with other centres.
- **Conclusion:** Bihar gave the revolt one of its most heroic leaders and kept it alive in the east until late 1858; Kunwar Singh remains a symbol of Bihar's pride (Vijayotsav, 23 April).

**2. "The Revolt of 1857 was more than a sepoy mutiny." Discuss. (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** The debate — mutiny vs national revolt vs war of independence.
- **Military beginning:** greased cartridges, sepoy grievances, Meerut.
- **Civil participation:** peasants, artisans, taluqdars of Awadh, zamindars like Kunwar Singh, tribal people of Chotanagpur.
- **Political aim:** restoration of Mughal emperor; proclamations in the names of Bahadur Shah and Birjis Qadir.
- **Hindu–Muslim unity** and shared symbols.
- **Limits:** regional spread; loyal princes; no modern national idea; educated middle class aloof.
- **Historians:** Savarkar, R.C. Majumdar, S.N. Sen.
- **Conclusion:** a popular anti-colonial revolt with a sepoy core — the first great fight for freedom, though not "national" in the modern sense.

**3. Explain the changes in British policy in India after 1857. (20 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** 1857 shocked the British — a shift from "reform and annexation" to "caution and control".
- Crown rule (GoI Act 1858); Queen's Proclamation.
- Princes made allies; no annexation.
- Army: Peel Commission; more Europeans; martial races.
- Divide and rule; support to landlords and conservative groups; less social reform.
- Racial distance grew; financial burden on India.
- **Conclusion:** These changes set the stage for the rise of modern nationalism after 1870.
