This chapter starts modern Indian history. It covers the arrival of the European trading companies, the Carnatic wars, the conquest of Bengal and Bihar (Plassey 1757, Buxar 1764, Diwani 1765), the wars against Mysore, the Marathas and the Sikhs, the policies of annexation, and the Permanent Settlement of 1793. BPSC asks Prelims questions on dates, treaties and "who was Governor-General when", and Bihar facts (Buxar, Munger as Mir Qasim's capital, the Patna factory, the Treaty of Sugauli) appear again and again.

> **In simple words:** Europeans came to India to buy spices, cotton cloth, indigo and saltpetre. They fought each other for trade. The English East India Company won. Then it used its army and money to make Indian rulers weak, first in Bengal and Bihar, then everywhere. By 1857 the Company ruled most of India directly or through "protected" princes.

## The coming of the Europeans

In the 15th century the land route from Europe to Asia was controlled by Arab and Ottoman traders. Europeans wanted a direct **sea route** to India. The Portuguese found it first.

### The Portuguese

- **Vasco da Gama** reached **Calicut** (Kozhikode, Kerala) on **20 May 1498**. He landed near Kappad and was received by the local ruler, the **Zamorin**. A Gujarati pilot guided him across the Arabian Sea from Malindi (East Africa).
- **Pedro Alvares Cabral** came in 1500; Vasco da Gama came again in 1502.
- **Francisco de Almeida** (1505–09) was the first Portuguese viceroy. His **"Blue Water Policy"** meant: control the sea, not the land.
- **Afonso de Albuquerque** (1509–15) is the real founder of Portuguese power. He captured **Goa in 1510** from the Sultan of **Bijapur**. He encouraged Portuguese men to marry Indian women.
- Later Portuguese centres: **Daman, Diu, Salsette, Bassein (Vasai), Hooghly** (Bengal). Shah Jahan expelled them from Hooghly in **1632**.
- The **cartaz** system: Indian and Arab ships had to buy a Portuguese pass to sail.
- They brought **tobacco**, the **printing press** (Goa, 1556), and crops like potato, chilli and cashew (via their trade with the Americas).
- Goa, Daman and Diu stayed Portuguese until **1961** (liberated by Indian army action, "Operation Vijay").

> **Prelims angle:** "First to come, last to leave" = the Portuguese (1498 → 1961). Albuquerque captured Goa from Bijapur, not from Vijayanagar.

### The Dutch

- **Dutch East India Company (VOC)** formed in **1602**.
- First factory: **Masulipatnam** (Andhra coast) **1605**. Others: **Pulicat** (their early headquarters), Surat, Chinsura (Bengal), Nagapattinam, Cochin, and **Patna**.
- The Dutch were mainly interested in the **spice islands** of Indonesia. In India they traded cloth, indigo, saltpetre and opium.
- They were defeated by Marthanda Varma of Travancore at **Colachel (1741)**, and by the English at **Bedara (Chinsura), 1759**. After Bedara they stopped being a political power in India.

### The English East India Company

- Founded by a **charter of Queen Elizabeth I on 31 December 1600** ("Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies").
- **Captain William Hawkins** reached Jahangir's court in **1609**.
- **Captain Thomas Best** defeated the Portuguese at **Swally (near Surat), 1612**.
- First permanent factory: **Surat, 1613**, with a farman from Jahangir.
- **Sir Thomas Roe**, ambassador of King James I, was at Jahangir's court **1615–19** and got trading rights.
- **Masulipatnam** factory 1611; **Fort St George, Madras** 1639–40 (Francis Day got the land from a local Nayak).
- **Bombay** came to King Charles II as dowry when he married the Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza (1661); he gave it to the Company in **1668**.
- **Calcutta**: Job Charnock settled at Sutanuti in **1690**. In **1698** the Company bought the zamindari of three villages — **Sutanuti, Kalikata and Govindpur**. **Fort William** was built there.
- **Farman of Farrukhsiyar (1717)**: the Mughal emperor allowed the Company to trade duty-free in Bengal for a fixed **Rs 3,000 a year**. It is called the **"Magna Carta of the Company"**. The Company's servants misused the free passes (**dastaks**) for their private trade, which angered the Nawabs of Bengal.

> **Bihar connection:** The English opened a factory at **Patna** around **1620** (it closed and re-opened later). Bihar was the main source of **saltpetre** (used to make gunpowder), and later of **opium**. The Dutch also had a factory at Patna. The old Company opium godown at **Gulzarbagh, Patna** is now the Government Printing Press. This is why Bihar mattered to the Europeans long before Plassey.

### The Danes and the French

- **Danish East India Company**: 1616. Settlements at **Tranquebar** (Tamil Nadu, 1620) and **Serampore** (Bengal). Serampore became famous for the Baptist missionaries (William Carey) and their printing press. The Danes sold their Indian settlements to the British in **1845**.
- **French East India Company (Compagnie des Indes Orientales)**: **1664**, under minister **Colbert** of King Louis XIV. A state-controlled company (unlike the English one, which was private).
  - First factory **Surat 1668** (François Caron), **Masulipatnam 1669**.
  - **Pondicherry** founded 1673–74 by **François Martin**; it became their capital.
  - **Chandernagore** (Bengal) 1690–92.
  - **Dupleix** became Governor of Pondicherry in **1742**. He tried to build political power by supporting Indian claimants to thrones.

## Anglo-French rivalry: the Carnatic Wars

The **Carnatic** was the Coromandel coast region (Madras to Pondicherry). Three wars were fought here between the English and the French. They were linked to wars in Europe.

| War | Years | Background | Key events | Ended by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | 1746–48 | War of Austrian Succession in Europe | French took Madras; at **St Thome / Adyar (1746)** a small French force defeated the large army of Nawab **Anwaruddin** — showed European-trained troops were superior | **Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)**; Madras returned to English |
| Second | 1749–54 | Succession fights in Hyderabad and Carnatic | Dupleix backed Chanda Sahib and Muzaffar Jang; **Robert Clive seized Arcot (1751)**; Muhammad Ali (English candidate) won | **Treaty of Pondicherry (1754–55)**; Dupleix recalled |
| Third | 1756–63 | Seven Years' War in Europe | **Battle of Wandiwash (1760)**: **Sir Eyre Coote** defeated **Count de Lally**; Pondicherry fell 1761 | **Treaty of Paris (1763)**; French kept trading posts but no army/forts |

**Why the English won:** stronger navy; better finances (a private company with backing of the British Parliament); control of rich Bengal after 1757; better commanders (Clive, Coote) and the recall of Dupleix by his own government.

> **Prelims angle:** Wandiwash (1760) decided the Anglo-French contest; Plassey (1757) decided Bengal. Do not mix them. Lally was later executed in France.

## The conquest of Bengal and Bihar

In the 18th century the **Nawabs of Bengal** ruled Bengal, Bihar and Orissa almost independently of the Mughals.

- **Murshid Quli Khan** (shifted capital from Dacca to **Murshidabad**), **Shujauddin**, **Sarfaraz Khan**, **Alivardi Khan** (1740–56; he defeated Sarfaraz at Giria, 1740, and fought the Maratha raids).
- **Alivardi Khan** had earlier been the **deputy governor (Naib Nazim) of Bihar** at Patna.

### The Battle of Plassey, 23 June 1757

- **Siraj-ud-Daulah**, grandson of Alivardi, became Nawab in 1756.
- Causes of conflict: misuse of **dastaks**; English fortified Calcutta without permission; they sheltered Krishna Das (an enemy of the Nawab).
- Siraj captured **Calcutta (June 1756)**. The **"Black Hole" story** (by J.Z. Holwell — many English prisoners said to have died in a small room) is considered exaggerated by historians, but it was used as propaganda.
- **Clive and Admiral Watson** recaptured Calcutta (January 1757). **Treaty of Alinagar** (February 1757).
- Clive made a secret deal with **Mir Jafar** (commander), **Rai Durlabh**, **Jagat Seth** (banker), **Omichand (Amir Chand)** and **Yar Lutf Khan**.
- At **Plassey (Palashi), on the Bhagirathi, 23 June 1757**, Mir Jafar and Rai Durlabh did not fight. Only **Mir Madan** and **Mohan Lal** fought for the Nawab. Siraj fled and was killed.
- **Result:** Mir Jafar became Nawab. The Company got the zamindari of the **24 Parganas**, huge cash payments, and the right of free trade. Plassey was **a conspiracy more than a battle**, but it gave the Company control over Bengal's wealth.

### Mir Jafar, Mir Qasim and the Battle of Buxar

- **Mir Jafar (1757–60)** could not pay the Company's demands. He was replaced by his son-in-law **Mir Qasim (1760–63)**, who gave the Company **Burdwan, Midnapore and Chittagong**.
- Mir Qasim was able and independent. He shifted his capital from Murshidabad to **Monghyr (Munger) in Bihar**, built a modern army with a **gun factory at Munger**, and **abolished all internal duties** so that Indian merchants could compete with the English. This made the Company angry.
- He was defeated in battles at **Katwa, Giria and Udaynala (1763)** and fled to Awadh. Mir Jafar was made Nawab again.

**Battle of Buxar, 22 October 1764**

- **Allies:** **Mir Qasim** (Bengal), **Shuja-ud-Daulah** (Nawab of Awadh) and **Shah Alam II** (Mughal emperor).
- **English commander:** **Major Hector Munro**.
- **Place:** **Buxar**, on the Ganga in Bihar (now Buxar district).
- The English won a real military victory against the joint forces of three Indian powers. This is why historians say **Buxar was more decisive than Plassey**.

**Treaties of Allahabad (August 1765)**, made by **Robert Clive** (in his second governorship):

- **With Shah Alam II:** the emperor granted the **Diwani** (right to collect revenue) of **Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** to the Company. In return the Company gave him **Rs 26 lakh a year** and the districts of **Kara and Allahabad**.
- **With Shuja-ud-Daulah:** Awadh was returned to him (except Kara and Allahabad); he paid **Rs 50 lakh** as war indemnity and accepted a Company force for his defence at his cost.

### Dual Government (1765–72)

- The Company had the **Diwani** (revenue), while the Nawab kept the **Nizamat** (police and justice) in name. Real power was with the Company; responsibility stayed with the Nawab. This is called **Dual Government** (system of Clive).
- The Company appointed **Naib Diwans**: **Muhammad Reza Khan** for Bengal and **Raja Shitab Rai** for **Bihar**.
- Result: heavy revenue demand, no care for the people. The **Great Bengal Famine of 1770** (Bengal and Bihar) killed perhaps one-third of the population of the affected areas.
- **Warren Hastings** ended Dual Government in **1772**; the Company took direct charge of revenue collection and moved the treasury from Murshidabad to Calcutta.

> **Bihar connection:** Three big turning points of the Company's rise are tied to Bihar: Bihar's **saltpetre and Patna factory** (trade), **Munger** (Mir Qasim's capital and gun factory), and **Buxar** (the decisive battle). The **Diwani of 1765** included Bihar. Raja Shitab Rai was the Naib Diwan of Bihar. Read this with [Modern Bihar and 1857](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/modern-bihar-and-1857).

> **Mains angle:** "Plassey made the British rich; Buxar made them rulers." Use this line in an answer on the establishment of British power. Points: Plassey = conspiracy, wealth flowed to the Company; Buxar = defeat of the combined Mughal–Awadh–Bengal forces; Diwani = legal right to tax 3 provinces; this revenue paid for the later wars in South India.

## Expansion: Mysore, Marathas, Sikhs and others

### Anglo-Mysore Wars

**Hyder Ali** rose from a soldier to the ruler of Mysore (1761). His son **Tipu Sultan** (ruled 1782–99) was a moderniser: new calendar, new coins, a navy plan, contact with the French (he planted a "Tree of Liberty" at Srirangapatna and joined the Jacobin Club, as commonly reported), and use of rockets.

| War | Years | Governor/GG on British side | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | 1767–69 | (Madras government) | Hyder Ali near Madras; **Treaty of Madras (1769)** — mutual return of conquests, defensive alliance |
| Second | 1780–84 | Warren Hastings | Hyder died 1782; Tipu continued; **Treaty of Mangalore (1784)** |
| Third | 1790–92 | **Cornwallis** | **Treaty of Seringapatam (1792)**: Tipu lost **half** his kingdom, paid indemnity, gave two sons as hostages |
| Fourth | 1799 | **Wellesley** | Tipu killed defending **Srirangapatna (May 1799)**; Wodeyar family restored under subsidiary alliance |

### Anglo-Maratha Wars

| War | Years | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| First | 1775–82 | Succession quarrel in Pune (Raghunath Rao); **Treaty of Surat (1775)**, **Purandar (1776)**; **Treaty of Salbai (1782)** — status quo, Mahadji Scindia the mediator |
| Second | 1803–05 | After **Treaty of Bassein (1802)**: Peshwa **Baji Rao II** accepted subsidiary alliance; Scindia and Bhonsle fought; battles of **Assaye** and **Laswari** (1803) |
| Third | 1817–18 | Under **Lord Hastings**; Peshwaship **abolished**; Baji Rao II pensioned off to **Bithur** (near Kanpur) — his adopted son was **Nana Saheb** of 1857 |

### The Sikhs and Punjab

- **Maharaja Ranjit Singh** (1780–1839) built a strong Sikh kingdom in Punjab with capital Lahore. **Treaty of Amritsar (1809)** with the British fixed the **Sutlej** as the boundary.
- **First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46)** under Hardinge I: **Treaty of Lahore (1846)**; Kashmir given to **Gulab Singh** (Treaty of Amritsar, 1846).
- **Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–49)**: **Punjab annexed by Dalhousie (1849)**. The Koh-i-Noor diamond went to Queen Victoria.

### Other wars

- **Anglo-Nepal (Gurkha) War 1814–16** under Lord Hastings: **Treaty of Sugauli (1816)** — Nepal gave up Kumaon, Garhwal and the Terai areas; a British Resident was posted in Kathmandu; Gurkhas began to be recruited into the British army.
- **Anglo-Burmese Wars**: First 1824–26 (Amherst; **Treaty of Yandabo** — Assam, Arakan, Tenasserim); Second 1852 (Dalhousie; Lower Burma/Pegu); Third 1885 (Dufferin; Upper Burma annexed 1886).
- **Anglo-Afghan Wars**: First 1839–42 (Auckland — a disaster), Second 1878–80 (Lytton).
- **Sindh** annexed **1843** (Ellenborough; Charles Napier).

> **Bihar connection:** **Sugauli** is in **East Champaran district, Bihar**. The Treaty of Sugauli (1816) still matters for the India–Nepal border. Bihar's long border with Nepal makes this a favourite BPSC fact.

## Tools of annexation

### Subsidiary Alliance (Lord Wellesley, 1798)

An Indian ruler who accepted it had to:

1. Keep a **British army** in his state and pay for it (in cash or by giving territory).
2. Keep a **British Resident** at his court.
3. Not employ any other European without British consent.
4. Not make war or peace with other states without British consent.

In return the British promised to protect him. Result: Indian rulers lost their armies and foreign policy; the British got a big army paid for by Indians.

- **First to accept: Nizam of Hyderabad (1798).** Others: Mysore (1799), Tanjore (1799), **Awadh (1801)**, **Peshwa (1802, Treaty of Bassein)**, Bhonsle (1803), Scindia (1803–04).
- Critics said the system kept rulers on their thrones only until they were weak enough to be swallowed — which is exactly what happened to Awadh in 1856.

> **Common mistake:** The **idea** of a ring-fence and forces for allies existed before (Dupleix used similar methods), but the **Subsidiary Alliance as a system** is credited to **Wellesley**. The first state to accept it was **Hyderabad**, not Mysore.

### Doctrine of Lapse (Lord Dalhousie, 1848–56)

If a ruler of a **dependent state** died **without a natural heir**, his adopted son could not inherit the kingdom; the state "lapsed" to the British.

| State | Year |
|---|---|
| **Satara** (first) | 1848 |
| Jaitpur, **Sambalpur** | 1849 |
| Baghat | 1850 |
| Udaipur (a small state in central India, not Mewar) | 1852 |
| **Jhansi** | 1853 |
| **Nagpur** | 1854 |

- **Awadh (1856)** was annexed on the ground of **misgovernment**, **not** under the Doctrine of Lapse. Nawab **Wajid Ali Shah** was exiled to Calcutta. This hurt the many sepoys from Awadh — a direct cause of 1857.
- Dalhousie also stopped the pensions and titles of some rulers (e.g. Nana Saheb's pension, the titles of the Nawab of Carnatic and Raja of Tanjore).

> **Prelims angle:** Satara = first state under lapse. Awadh = not lapse, but "misgovernance". Jhansi 1853 → Rani Lakshmibai in 1857. The Queen's Proclamation of 1858 ended the policy of annexation.

## The Permanent Settlement of 1793

After the Diwani, the Company's main income was **land revenue**. Warren Hastings first tried **auctioning** the right to collect revenue for five years (**1772, Quinquennial settlement**), then yearly settlements. These failed: the bidders squeezed the peasants and ran away.

**Lord Cornwallis** introduced the **Permanent Settlement** in **1793** (a ten-year settlement of 1789–90 was declared permanent). It is also called the **Zamindari system** or **Istamrari Bandobast**. Sir John Shore helped plan it.

### Main features

- **Zamindars** (landlords and old revenue collectors) were made **owners of the land**. They could sell, mortgage or pass it on.
- The **revenue demand was fixed for ever**. The zamindar paid **10/11 of the rent** collected from peasants to the Company and kept **1/11**.
- If the zamindar failed to pay by sunset on the due date, his estate was auctioned — the **Sunset Law**.
- **Peasants (ryots)** became **tenants** of the zamindar, with no fixed rights at first.
- Areas: **Bengal, Bihar, Orissa**, **Benares (Varanasi) division**, and parts of northern Madras. About **19%** of British India's area came under it.

### Why the Company did it

- A **fixed, sure income** for the Company.
- A loyal class of **zamindars** who would support British rule.
- The hope that owners would invest in land and improve farming (like English landlords).
- Less cost of yearly assessment.

### Effects

| Group | Effect |
|---|---|
| Company | Sure income, but it **lost** the benefit of later rises in land value and prices |
| Zamindars | Many old zamindars failed under the Sunset Law; their estates were bought by **city merchants and moneylenders** (absentee landlords). Later, rents rose but revenue did not, so zamindars grew rich |
| Peasants | **Worst hit**: high rents, insecurity, illegal cesses (abwabs), forced labour, eviction. Sub-infeudation: many layers of middlemen (patnidars) between zamindar and peasant |
| Agriculture | Little investment; stagnation; famines |
| Politics | Created a loyal landed class; later this class often opposed the national movement; the peasant anger fed peasant movements (see [Tribal and Peasant Movements](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/tribal-and-peasant-movements)) |

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar was a core **Permanent Settlement** area. Big estates like **Darbhanga Raj**, **Bettiah Raj**, **Hathwa Raj** and **Tekari Raj** grew under it. The misery of tenants under this system is the background of the **Champaran Satyagraha (1917)** and of **Swami Sahajanand's Kisan Sabha (1929)**. After independence, **Bihar was the first state to pass a zamindari abolition law (Bihar Land Reforms Act, 1950)**.

> **Mains angle:** A typical question: "Examine the impact of the Permanent Settlement on the peasantry of Bihar." Use: fixed revenue but rising rent; rack-renting; absentee landlords; sub-infeudation; indebtedness; famines; peasant revolts; zamindari abolition after 1950. The other land revenue systems (Ryotwari, Mahalwari) are compared in [British Policies, Reforms and Economic Impact](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/british-policies-reforms-and-economic-impact).

## Governors and Governors-General up to 1858

Until 1773 the Company had separate **Governors** of Bengal, Madras and Bombay. The **Regulating Act, 1773** created the **Governor-General of Bengal**, with control over the other two. The **Charter Act, 1833** made him **Governor-General of India**. In 1858 the GG also became the **Viceroy** (representative of the Crown). The Viceroys after 1858 are listed in [Rise of Nationalism](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/rise-of-nationalism-moderates-and-extremists).

> **Memory trick:** Big reformers by number — **"1773 Hastings, 1793 Cornwallis, 1798 Wellesley, 1829 Bentinck, 1848 Dalhousie, 1857 Canning."** Say it as a chain: "Hastings started it, Cornwallis settled it, Wellesley allied it, Bentinck reformed it, Dalhousie lapsed it, Canning lost it (1857)."

> **Common mistake:** The **Widow Remarriage Act** was drafted under Dalhousie but **passed in July 1856, under Canning**. If a question asks under whom it was **passed**, answer Canning.

## Why the British succeeded

1. **Superior army discipline and arms**, and regular salaries for soldiers.
2. **Naval power** and control of sea trade.
3. **Money**: the Diwani revenue of Bengal and Bihar paid for wars in the south.
4. **Disunity of Indian powers** — Marathas, Mysore, Nizam fought each other; the British used one against another.
5. **Diplomacy**: Subsidiary Alliance, buying traitors (Plassey).
6. **Leadership**: Clive, Hastings, Wellesley, Dalhousie.
7. **Decline of the Mughal centre** after Aurangzeb (1707), and Nadir Shah's (1739) and Ahmad Shah Abdali's raids (Third Battle of Panipat 1761 weakened the Marathas).

### Mains questions

**1. "The Battle of Buxar, rather than Plassey, laid the real foundation of British rule in India." Examine. (38 marks, ~300 words)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Two battles seven years apart — Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764) — both fought for the control of Bengal and Bihar.
- **Plassey:** won by conspiracy (Mir Jafar, Jagat Seth); no real fighting; gave the Company money, 24 Parganas, a puppet Nawab — but Indian powers outside Bengal were not challenged.
- **Buxar:** a real military contest against the joint forces of the Nawab of Bengal, the Nawab of Awadh and the Mughal emperor; showed British military superiority.
- **Treaty of Allahabad (1765):** legal right (Diwani) to the revenue of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa; Mughal emperor became a pensioner; Awadh became a buffer and dependent ally.
- **Financial base:** Diwani revenue financed later wars in Mysore and Maharashtra.
- **Bihar angle:** fought at Buxar in Bihar; Bihar's revenue and saltpetre came under the Company; Shitab Rai as Naib Diwan.
- **Balance:** Plassey was the political beginning (without it no Buxar); Buxar made the conquest final.
- **Conclusion:** Plassey opened the door; Buxar and the Diwani placed the British on the throne of eastern India.

**2. Critically examine the Permanent Settlement of 1793 and its long-term effects on Bihar. (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Cornwallis, 1793; fixed land revenue for ever with zamindars as owners; Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, Benares.
- **Aims:** sure income, loyal class, improvement of agriculture, cheaper administration.
- **Positive (limited):** fixed state demand; a stable revenue base for the Company; some land reclamation.
- **Negative for peasants:** rents raised at will, illegal cesses, forced labour (begar), eviction; no fixed occupancy rights until later tenancy laws (Bengal Tenancy Act 1885).
- **Zamindars:** Sunset Law ruined old families; rise of absentee landlords and many layers of middlemen.
- **Agriculture:** little investment; frequent famines; indebtedness.
- **Bihar outcomes:** big estates (Darbhanga, Bettiah, Hathwa, Tekari); indigo planters leasing land in Champaran; peasant movements — Champaran 1917, Kisan Sabha 1929, Bakasht struggle 1930s.
- **After 1947:** Bihar Land Reforms Act 1950 — first zamindari abolition law; but land inequality continued.
- **Conclusion:** A system designed for the Company's safety left a legacy of agrarian inequality that Bihar still struggles with.

**3. Explain how the Subsidiary Alliance and the Doctrine of Lapse helped British expansion in India. (20 marks, ~200 words)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Two policies of "expansion without war" — Wellesley (1798) and Dalhousie (1848–56).
- **Subsidiary Alliance:** British army at the ruler's cost; Resident; no other Europeans; no independent foreign policy; Hyderabad first, then Mysore, Awadh, Peshwa (Bassein 1802).
- **Effects:** rulers disarmed; territory ceded to pay troops; British army grew at Indian cost; states became dependent.
- **Doctrine of Lapse:** no adoption right for dependent states; Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur annexed.
- **Combined with "misgovernance" (Awadh 1856).**
- **Result:** large areas annexed cheaply; but resentment of princes and sepoys — Rani of Jhansi and Nana Saheb in 1857.
- **Conclusion:** Both policies ended after 1858 when the Crown promised to respect princes' rights.
