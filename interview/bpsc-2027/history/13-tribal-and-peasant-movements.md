This chapter covers the people who resisted British rule from below — forest tribes, peasants and tenants. Most of the important tribal movements happened in the old Bihar region (Rajmahal hills, Santhal Parganas, Chotanagpur), so BPSC asks about them very often, even though the areas are now in Jharkhand. Prelims asks leader–movement–year matches. Mains asks "Discuss the causes and nature of tribal revolts in Bihar and Jharkhand" or "Assess the role of the Kisan Sabha movement in Bihar".

> **In simple words:** British rule brought outsiders — zamindars, moneylenders, traders, contractors and police — into tribal forests and villages. They took land, raised rents and forced people to work for free. Tribal people called them **dikus** (outsiders) and rose in revolt. Peasants in plains areas also fought against planters, landlords and high rents. Early revolts were violent and local; later peasant movements became organised and joined the national movement.

## Why these revolts happened

### Causes of tribal revolts

1. **Loss of land**: tribal land (held by the community) was given to outside zamindars; the British land laws did not recognise **community ownership** (for example the **khuntkatti** system of the Mundas).
2. **Moneylenders and traders** (mahajans) cheated tribal people with high interest; land was lost for small debts.
3. **Forest laws**: forests were declared government property (Indian Forest Act **1865, 1878**); shifting cultivation (**jhum**) and free use of forest produce were banned.
4. **Forced labour** (**begar / beth-begari**) by landlords and officials.
5. **Christian missionaries** and changes in tribal culture; at the same time, some tribals used missionary education.
6. **Courts and police** that tribals did not understand and that favoured outsiders.

### Causes of peasant revolts

- High **land revenue** and **rent**; illegal cesses (**abwabs**).
- Eviction from land; no security of tenure.
- **Forced cultivation** of cash crops like **indigo**.
- Indebtedness to moneylenders.
- Famines and falling prices (especially the 1930s Depression).

> **Mains angle:** The common link is the **colonial economy** — new land laws, commercialisation and forest control. Write this as your introduction in any question on these movements. Then classify: (a) **civil/restoration revolts** led by dispossessed rulers (early); (b) **tribal revolts**; (c) **peasant movements** (from 1850s, more organised after 1918).

## Tribal movements of Bihar and Jharkhand

> **Bihar connection:** All of these happened in the old Bihar region (Bhagalpur, Santhal Parganas, Chotanagpur). Questions may say "present-day Jharkhand". See also [Modern Bihar and 1857](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/modern-bihar-and-1857).

### Pahariya revolt and Tilka Manjhi (1780s)

- **Tilka Manjhi** (also called **Jabra Pahariya**) of the **Rajmahal hills** is often called the **first tribal martyr (Adi Vidrohi)** against the British.
- The Collector of **Bhagalpur**, **Augustus Cleveland**, tried to win over the Pahariyas with money and jobs. Tilka Manjhi resisted and is said to have **shot Cleveland with an arrow (1784)**; Cleveland later died.
- Tilka Manjhi was caught and **hanged at Bhagalpur (1785)**.
- **Tilka Manjhi Bhagalpur University** is named after him.

### Kol uprising (1831–32)

- **Area:** **Chotanagpur** (Ranchi, Singhbhum, Palamu, Hazaribagh).
- **Tribes:** Kols — a British name covering **Mundas, Oraons and Hos**.
- **Leaders:** **Buddhu Bhagat**, Joa Bhagat, Madara Mahato.
- **Cause:** land given to outsiders (Sikhs and Muslim farmers given leases by the Raja of Chotanagpur), high rents, moneylenders.
- Thousands of outsiders were attacked; the revolt was suppressed by the army.
- **Result:** the British created the **South-West Frontier Agency (1833–34)** — a special administration for Chotanagpur.

### Bhumij revolt (1832–33)

- **Ganga Narayan Singh** led the Bhumij in **Manbhum and Dhalbhum**. The British called it "Ganga Narain's hangama".

### Santhal Hul (1855–56) ⭐

- **Date:** began **30 June 1855** (now observed as **Hul Diwas**).
- **Place:** **Bhognadih** village in the **Damin-i-Koh** area (Rajmahal hills; now **Sahebganj district, Jharkhand**).
- **Leaders:** brothers **Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu**, with **Chand and Bhairav**, and sisters **Phulo and Jhano**.
- **Background:** Santhals had moved into the Damin-i-Koh and cleared forests for farming. Then **zamindars, moneylenders (mahajans), police and railway contractors** exploited them. Interest rates were very high; land was lost; officials did not help.
- Sidhu and Kanhu claimed divine orders (the "Thakur") and called a gathering of thousands of Santhals; they declared the end of Company rule in their area.
- They attacked moneylenders, police stations and railway works. The **army and martial law** were used; thousands of Santhals were killed; Sidhu and Kanhu were captured and executed (1855–56).
- **Result:** a separate district, **Santhal Parganas** (by **Act XXXVII of 1855**), with special rules protecting Santhals. The **Santhal Parganas Tenancy Act** came later (1949).

> **Prelims angle:** Santhal Hul = **1855**, Sidhu–Kanhu, Bhognadih. Do not mix with 1857. Hul (Santhali) = revolt; Ulgulan (Mundari) = great tumult (Birsa); Hangama = Bhumij revolt.

### Later Santhal movements

- **Kherwar (Safa Hor) movement (1870s)** — led by **Bhagirath Manjhi**; a reform and revival movement (cleanliness, giving up liquor, monotheism) and against high rents.

### Sardari Larai (1858–95)

- "War of the chiefs" by Christian Mundas and Oraons in Chotanagpur, against landlords and for restoration of **khuntkatti** rights; mostly by petitions and legal fights. It prepared the ground for Birsa.

### Birsa Munda and the Ulgulan (1899–1900) ⭐

- **Born:** **15 November 1875**, at **Ulihatu** (now **Khunti district, Jharkhand**).
- Studied at a German Lutheran mission school (Chaibasa) and was briefly a Christian; then turned against the missionaries.
- Around 1895 he declared himself a messenger of God. His followers (**Birsaits**) called him **"Dharti Aba"** (father of the earth). He preached one God, giving up liquor and superstition, and refusing to pay rent.
- Arrested in **1895** and jailed for two years (released 1897).
- **The Ulgulan ("great tumult")** began around **Christmas 1899** (24 December). Followers attacked police stations, churches and landlords' houses.
- The decisive clash was at **Dombari Buru** hill (January 1900), where many Mundas were killed.
- Birsa was captured in **early 1900** and **died in Ranchi jail on 9 June 1900** (officially of cholera).
- **Aims:** end British rule in the Munda country, drive out dikus, restore **khuntkatti** (joint clan ownership of land), end **beth begari** (forced labour).
- **Results:** **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act, 1908** — recognised khuntkatti rights and banned forced labour; restricted sale of tribal land to non-tribals.

> **Remember:** **15 November** = Birsa Munda's birthday = **Jharkhand foundation day (2000)** = **Janjatiya Gaurav Divas** (declared by the Government of India in **2021**). **Birsa Agricultural University** and **Birsa Munda Airport** are in Ranchi.

### Tana Bhagat movement (1914 onwards)

- **Leader:** **Jatra Bhagat** (Jatra Oraon) of **Gumla**, Chotanagpur.
- Began as a **religious reform** movement among the **Oraons**: one God (Dharmes), no liquor, no meat, no animal sacrifice, no belief in ghosts.
- Turned **political**: refused to pay rent and taxes, refused to do forced labour.
- Later the Tana Bhagats became **Gandhi's followers** — they joined the **Non-Cooperation** and **Civil Disobedience** movements, wore Khadi and the **Gandhi cap**, and attended Congress sessions (including **Gaya 1922** and **Ramgarh 1940**).
- They are an example of a tribal movement **merging with the national movement**.

## Peasant movements

### Indigo revolt (1859–60) ⭐

- **Where:** Bengal — **Nadia**, Jessore, Pabna.
- **Why:** European **indigo planters** forced peasants to grow indigo on their best land, gave cash advances that trapped them in debt, and used violence.
- **Leaders:** **Digambar Biswas and Bishnu Biswas** (Nadia).
- Peasants refused to grow indigo and resisted planters' armed men.
- **Support:** the Bengali intelligentsia — **Harish Chandra Mukherjee** (*Hindu Patriot*); **Dinabandhu Mitra's play *Neel Darpan* (1860)**, translated into English (as commonly stated, by Michael Madhusudan Dutt) and published by **Rev. James Long**, who was fined and jailed for it.
- **Result:** the **Indigo Commission (1860)**; peasants could not be forced to grow indigo; by 1860s indigo cultivation in Bengal declined — but it **shifted to north Bihar**.

> **Bihar connection:** After the Bengal revolt, indigo planters concentrated in **north Bihar — Champaran, Muzaffarpur, Darbhanga, Saran**. In Champaran the **tinkathia** system forced peasants to grow indigo on **3 kathas of every 20 kathas (3/20 of a bigha)**. When German synthetic dyes made indigo less profitable, planters demanded extra payments (**sharahbeshi** — higher rent; **tawan** — lump-sum compensation). Earlier protests occurred in 1867–68 and especially **1907–08 around Bettiah and Motihari** (leaders like **Sheikh Gulab** and **Sital Rai**). This was the background of **Gandhi's Champaran Satyagraha, 1917** — see [Gandhian Era](/interview/bpsc-2027/history/gandhian-era-champaran-to-civil-disobedience).

### Pabna agrarian league (1873–76)

- **Where:** Pabna (East Bengal).
- **Why:** zamindars raised rents illegally and blocked tenants from getting occupancy rights under the Act of 1859.
- **How:** peasants formed an **agrarian league**, raised funds and fought **legal battles**; mostly non-violent.
- **Leaders:** Ishan Chandra Roy, Shambhu Pal, Keshab Chandra Rai.
- Supported by intellectuals (R.C. Dutt, Surendranath Banerjee's Indian Association).
- **Result:** the **Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885** (also applied to **Bihar**) — more security for occupancy tenants.

### Deccan riots (1875)

- **Where:** Pune and Ahmednagar, starting at **Supa** village (1875).
- **Why:** Ryotwari peasants in debt to **Marwari and Gujarati moneylenders**; cotton prices fell after the American Civil War ended (1865).
- Peasants attacked moneylenders' houses and burnt **debt bonds**.
- **Result:** **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879**.

## Swami Sahajanand Saraswati and the Kisan Sabha in Bihar ⭐

### The man

- Born **1889** in **Ghazipur district** (UP); his earlier name was **Naurang Rai**. He became a sanyasi and settled in Bihar.
- He founded the **Sitaram Ashram at Bihta** (near Patna), which became the centre of the Kisan movement.
- He joined the Non-Cooperation Movement and was jailed.
- Died **26 June 1950**.
- Books: autobiography ***Mera Jeevan Sangharsh***; ***Kisan Kya Karen***, ***Jhootha Bhay Mithya Abhiman***.
- Famous line attributed to him: "**Jo ann-vastra upjayega, ab so kanoon banayega**" (those who grow food and cloth will now make the laws) — a common Kisan Sabha slogan.

### The movement

- **1927** — he formed the **West Patna Kisan Sabha** at Bihta.
- **1929** — the **Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha (BPKS)** was formed at **Sonepur** (during the Sonepur fair), with Sahajanand as president.
- **1936** — the **All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS)** was formed at **Lucknow (April 1936)**, at the time of the Lucknow Congress session. **Sahajanand was its first president** and **N.G. Ranga** a key organiser (general secretary). The AIKS issued a **Kisan Manifesto (1936)** demanding **abolition of zamindari**, reduction of rent, cancellation of debts and recognition of peasant unions.
- **Demands in Bihar:** abolition of zamindari; end of illegal cesses and begar; return of **bakasht** land; tenancy rights.

### The Bakasht struggle (1937–39)

- **Bakasht land** = land from which tenants had been **evicted** by zamindars for non-payment of rent (especially during the Depression) and which the zamindar then cultivated himself or gave to others.
- After the **Congress ministry (1937)** under **Sri Krishna Sinha** took office, peasants expected relief. The Bihar Tenancy (Amendment) laws gave some relief, but the Kisan Sabha said it was not enough and launched the Bakasht struggle.
- **Main centres and leaders:**
  - **Reora (Gaya)** — **Yadunandan (Jadunandan) Sharma**
  - **Barahiya Tal (Munger)** — **Karyanand Sharma**
  - **Amwari (Saran)** — **Rahul Sankrityayan** (he was attacked and injured)
- Many peasants won back land.

### Relations with Congress and the national movement

- The Kisan Sabha worked inside the national movement but often clashed with Congress leaders over the zamindars. In 1940 Sahajanand joined **Subhas Chandra Bose's Anti-Compromise Conference at Ramgarh**.
- The Kisan Sabha's long campaign made **zamindari abolition** a major demand. Bihar became the **first state to pass a zamindari abolition law (1950)**.

> **Mains angle:** "Assess the role of Swami Sahajanand Saraswati in the peasant movement of Bihar." Points: from religious sanyasi to peasant leader; Bihta ashram; BPKS 1929; AIKS 1936 first president; Kisan Manifesto; Bakasht struggle (Reora, Barahiya, Amwari); writings; criticism of Congress compromise with zamindars; legacy — zamindari abolition 1950, land reform debates. Conclude: he turned the peasant from a passive subject into an organised political actor.

## Nature of these movements

| Feature | Early (before 1857) | Later (after 1900) |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership | Local chiefs, religious figures, ex-rulers | Organised leaders, Kisan Sabhas, Congress, Left |
| Methods | Armed, violent, sudden | Satyagraha, petitions, legal fight, no-rent campaigns |
| Aims | Restore old order, drive out dikus | Rent reduction, tenancy rights, zamindari abolition, freedom |
| Area | Local | Linked across provinces |
| Link to nationalism | Little | Strong — part of the freedom struggle |

**Why they failed or had limited success:** local character, poor weapons, no wider coordination, brutal repression, and in some cases religious or caste divisions. **But they mattered:** they forced protective laws (CNT Act 1908, Santhal Parganas, Bengal Tenancy Act 1885, Champaran Agrarian Act 1918), exposed the colonial economy, and gave the national movement a mass base.

### Mains questions

**1. Discuss the causes and nature of the tribal revolts in Bihar and Jharkhand during British rule. (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Chotanagpur and the Rajmahal hills saw the most frequent tribal revolts in India; they are the earliest anti-colonial struggles of the region.
- **Causes:** land laws ignoring community ownership (khuntkatti); dikus — zamindars, mahajans, contractors; forest laws; begar; missionaries; alien courts.
- **Major revolts:** Tilka Manjhi (1784); Kol (1831–32); Bhumij (1832–33); Santhal Hul (1855–56); Sardari Larai; Birsa's Ulgulan (1899–1900); Tana Bhagat (1914).
- **Nature:** religious-messianic leadership (Sidhu–Kanhu's "Thakur", Birsa as Dharti Aba); attack on symbols of exploitation; violent and local, but some (Tana Bhagat) became Gandhian.
- **Response:** army, martial law, executions; and protective laws — Santhal Parganas district (1855), SWFA (1834), CNT Act (1908).
- **Significance:** first fights for land and identity; legacy in the Jharkhand movement and Fifth Schedule protections.
- **Conclusion:** These revolts show that resistance to colonial rule began in the forests long before the Congress.

**2. Evaluate the role of Swami Sahajanand Saraswati and the Kisan Sabha in the peasant movement of Bihar. (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Permanent Settlement and zamindari oppression in Bihar; Sahajanand as the "Kisan Pran".
- **Organisation:** West Patna Kisan Sabha (1927), BPKS at Sonepur (1929), AIKS at Lucknow (1936) — first president.
- **Programme:** zamindari abolition, rent reduction, end of begar and abwabs, debt relief — Kisan Manifesto.
- **Struggles:** Bakasht movement 1937–39 — Reora, Barahiya Tal, Amwari; leaders Karyanand Sharma, Yadunandan Sharma, Rahul Sankrityayan.
- **Relations with Congress:** cooperation in the national struggle; conflict over zamindars; Anti-Compromise Conference at Ramgarh 1940.
- **Writings:** *Mera Jeevan Sangharsh*, *Kisan Kya Karen*.
- **Achievements:** peasants became a political force; pressure for tenancy laws; Bihar Land Reforms Act 1950.
- **Limits:** little for landless labourers; splits on Left lines.
- **Conclusion:** He linked the struggle against the zamindar to the struggle against the Raj.

**3. "The Champaran Satyagraha had deep roots in earlier peasant resistance against indigo planting." Explain. (20 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Indigo moved from Bengal to north Bihar after 1860.
- Bengal Indigo revolt 1859–60 and Indigo Commission.
- Tinkathia in Champaran; Bettiah Raj leases to planters.
- Sharahbeshi and tawan after synthetic dye.
- Earlier protests (1867–68, 1907–08 Bettiah–Motihari; Sheikh Gulab, Sital Rai).
- Raj Kumar Shukla's persistence → Gandhi 1917.
- **Conclusion:** Gandhi gave organised, non-violent form to a long local struggle.
