This chapter explains how British rule changed India's government, economy and society. It is one of the most important chapters for both papers. Prelims asks about Acts and their features, reformers and their organisations, newspapers and their founders. Mains GS Paper I asks analytical questions like "Discuss the economic impact of British rule" or "Evaluate the role of socio-religious reform movements in the growth of nationalism".

> **In simple words:** The British made laws to control India (Acts), took land revenue in new ways (Permanent, Ryotwari, Mahalwari), and turned India into a supplier of raw materials and a market for British goods (drain of wealth). At the same time, Indians who learnt modern ideas started reform movements against bad social customs. Schools, universities and newspapers spread these ideas — and later, nationalism.

## Constitutional development: the Acts

Each Act gave a little more power to the British Parliament first, and later a little more participation to Indians. Learn them as a chain. (The Constitution-making side is covered in [Making of the Constitution](/interview/bpsc-2027/polity/making-of-the-constitution).)

### Company period (1773–1858)

| Act | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Regulating Act, 1773** | First step of Parliament to control the Company. **Governor of Bengal became Governor-General of Bengal** (Warren Hastings) with a council of 4; Bombay and Madras placed under him; **Supreme Court at Calcutta (set up 1774)** — Chief Justice **Sir Elijah Impey**; Company servants barred from private trade and taking gifts |
| Act of Settlement, 1781 | Corrected defects of 1773; limited the Supreme Court's power over revenue matters and over Company officials acting officially |
| **Pitt's India Act, 1784** | **Board of Control** (government) for political matters; **Court of Directors** (Company) for commerce — **"double government"**; Company's territories called "British possessions in India" for the first time |
| Charter Act, 1793 | Renewed the charter for 20 years; salaries of Board of Control from Indian revenue |
| **Charter Act, 1813** | **Ended the Company's trade monopoly** in India (kept tea and trade with China); **Rs 1 lakh a year for education**; allowed **Christian missionaries** to come |
| **Charter Act, 1833** | **GG of Bengal → Governor-General of India** (William Bentinck first); all legislative power centralised in the GG-in-Council; Company became a **purely administrative body** (trade ended); **Law Commission** (Macaulay); Section 87 — no Indian to be barred from office by religion, birth or colour (not followed in practice) |
| **Charter Act, 1853** | Last charter act; **separated legislative and executive functions** of the GG's council (a separate legislative council — a "mini-parliament"); **open competition** for civil services (Macaulay Committee 1854); no fixed period given for Company rule |

### Crown period (1858–1947)

| Act | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Government of India Act, 1858** | Passed after 1857. Company rule ended; **Crown rule**. **Secretary of State for India** (a British cabinet minister) with a **Council of India (15 members)**; **GG became Viceroy** (Canning first); Board of Control and Court of Directors abolished. Announced by the **Queen's Proclamation (1 November 1858)** read by Canning at **Allahabad** |
| **Indian Councils Act, 1861** | Indians nominated as non-official members of the Viceroy's legislative council (Canning nominated the **Raja of Benares, the Maharaja of Patiala and Sir Dinkar Rao**); legislative powers restored to Bombay and Madras (decentralisation); **portfolio system** recognised; Viceroy could issue **ordinances** |
| **Indian Councils Act, 1892** | More non-official members; members could **discuss the budget** and **ask questions**; **indirect election** in a limited way (recommendations by bodies like universities, municipalities — word "election" not used) |
| **Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley–Minto Reforms)** | Morley = Secretary of State, Minto = Viceroy. Larger councils; **separate electorate for Muslims** ("communal representation"); **Satyendra Prasanna Sinha** became the **first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council** (Law Member). Minto is called the "**Father of Communal Electorate**" |
| **Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms)** | Based on Montagu's declaration of **20 Aug 1917** ("gradual development of self-governing institutions ... responsible government"). **Dyarchy in the provinces**: subjects divided into **transferred** (to Indian ministers — education, health, local government) and **reserved** (with Governor — finance, police, land revenue). **Bicameral** central legislature (Legislative Assembly + Council of State); **direct elections** (limited franchise); separate electorates extended to **Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans**; **High Commissioner for India** in London; **Public Service Commission** (set up 1926); provincial budgets separated from central; a statutory commission after 10 years (→ Simon Commission) |
| **Government of India Act, 1935** | Longest Act (321 sections, 10 schedules). Proposed an **All-India Federation** (provinces + princely states — never came into being); **provincial autonomy** (dyarchy in provinces abolished); **dyarchy at the Centre** (never implemented); **three lists** (Federal, Provincial, Concurrent); **bicameral legislatures in 6 provinces** — Bengal, Bombay, Madras, **Bihar**, Assam and United Provinces; **Federal Court** (1937); **RBI** (1935); separate electorates extended to depressed classes, women and labour; franchise about **10%** of the population; **Burma separated** from India (1937); **Orissa and Sind** made new provinces (1936) |
| **Indian Independence Act, 1947** | Passed **18 July 1947**. Two dominions, **India and Pakistan**, from **15 August 1947**; Viceroy's post abolished — a Governor-General for each dominion; Constituent Assemblies made sovereign; British paramountcy over princely states lapsed; Secretary of State's office abolished |

> **Bihar connection:**
> - **Satyendra Prasanna Sinha (Lord Sinha)** became the **first Indian Governor of a province — Bihar and Orissa (1920)** under the 1919 Act. He was also the first Indian in the Viceroy's Executive Council (1909) and INC president in 1915.
> - Bihar and Orissa became a **Governor's province** under the 1919 Act. Under the 1935 Act **Bihar got a bicameral legislature** (Assembly + Council) — that is why Bihar still has a Legislative Council today.
> - **Orissa was separated from Bihar on 1 April 1936.**

> **Memory trick:** **"R-P-C-C-C"** for the Company era (Regulating, Pitt's, Charters 1813/1833/1853), then **"58-61-92-09-19-35-47"** for the Crown era. Think "1909 = separate electorate, 1919 = dyarchy in provinces, 1935 = provincial autonomy".

> **Common mistake:** Dyarchy was introduced **in the provinces in 1919**. In **1935** it was **abolished in the provinces** and **proposed at the Centre** (but never worked). Do not say "1935 introduced dyarchy" without saying "at the Centre".

## Land revenue systems

Land revenue was the Company's biggest income. Three systems were used.

| Feature | Permanent Settlement (Zamindari) | Ryotwari | Mahalwari |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year / by | **1793, Cornwallis** (planned with John Shore) | **1820, Thomas Munro** in Madras (first tried by **Alexander Read** in Baramahal, 1792); Bombay — Elphinstone, Wingate | **1822, Holt Mackenzie** (NW Provinces); improved by **R.M. Bird and James Thomason** under Bentinck (Regulation of 1833) |
| Who pays the state | Zamindar | Individual **ryot** (peasant cultivator) directly | The **village community (mahal)** jointly, through a headman (lambardar) |
| Ownership | Zamindar owner | Ryot recognised as owner/occupant | Village community |
| Revenue period | **Fixed for ever** | Revised every 20–30 years | Revised periodically |
| Areas | **Bengal, Bihar, Orissa**, Benares, N. Madras (~19% of area) | Madras, Bombay, Assam, Coorg (~51%) | NW Provinces (UP), Punjab, Central India (~30%) |
| Problems | Rack-renting, absentee landlords, sub-infeudation | Very high demand (often 45–55% of produce); the **state became a giant zamindar**; moneylenders | High demand; village elites gained; indebtedness |

> **Prelims angle:** Match the person with the system: Cornwallis–Permanent; Munro and Read–Ryotwari; Holt Mackenzie–Mahalwari. Approximate shares (19% / 51% / 30%) are commonly quoted; treat them as "about".

## Economic impact: the drain of wealth

### The Drain Theory

The **Drain of Wealth** means the part of India's national product that was sent to Britain **without any economic return** to India.

- Main writer: **Dadabhai Naoroji** ("**Grand Old Man of India**"). Works: *The Poverty of India* (paper, 1876), *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (book, **1901**). He estimated India's per-capita income at about **Rs 20 a year** (1867–68).
- **R.C. Dutt**: *The Economic History of India* (2 volumes, 1901–03) — blamed land revenue policy and the destruction of handicrafts.
- **M.G. Ranade**, **G.V. Joshi**, **G.K. Gokhale** also wrote on it.
- Naoroji became the **first Indian member of the British House of Commons (1892**, Finsbury Central, Liberal Party).

**Parts of the drain:**

1. **Home Charges** — money paid in Britain: salaries and pensions of British officials, interest on public debt, cost of the India Office, army expenses (including wars outside India).
2. **Profits** of British companies, banks and shipping sent home.
3. **Interest** on capital invested in railways (guaranteed interest of 5%, whether the railway made profit or not).
4. **Savings** of British officers sent to England.

> **Mains angle:** The drain theory was the **economic critique of colonialism** by the Moderates. It showed that British rule was not a "blessing" — it was the cause of India's poverty. This turned educated Indians into nationalists. Use it as a key point in answers on "economic nationalism".

### De-industrialisation

- India's **handicrafts** (cotton and silk cloth of Bengal and Bihar, Dhaka muslin, etc.) declined.
- Causes: **one-way free trade** — British machine goods entered India with little duty while Indian goods faced high duties in Britain; loss of royal patronage after the princely courts ended; the **Industrial Revolution** in Britain; railways took cheap British goods into villages; forced low prices by Company agents (in Bengal weavers were made to sell to the Company).
- Result: artisans moved to agriculture → **pressure on land**, falling wages, poverty.

### Commercialisation of agriculture

- Farmers were pushed to grow **cash crops** (indigo, opium, cotton, jute, tea) for export, not food.
- Profits went to traders, planters and moneylenders, not peasants.
- Food shortages made **famines** worse.

> **Bihar connection:** North Bihar (Champaran, Muzaffarpur, Saran, Darbhanga) had **indigo** plantations run by European planters under the **tinkathia** system. **Opium** was grown in Bihar under a Company monopoly; factories at **Patna (Gulzarbagh)** and Ghazipur processed it for export to China. Bihar was also the main source of **saltpetre**. All three show how Bihar's land served British trade.

### Famines

- **Bengal famine 1770**; **Great Famine 1876–78** (South and West India); **1896–97** and **1899–1900** famines; **Bengal famine 1943**.
- **Famine Commissions**: **Strachey (1880**, under Lytton) → **Famine Code (1883)**; **Lyall (1897)**; **MacDonnell (1901)**.
- The **Bihar famine of 1873–74** is noted for relief work under Sir Richard Temple, which was later criticised as too costly — this shaped the harsher relief policy of 1876–78.

### Railways and modern industry

- **First railway: Bombay to Thane, 16 April 1853** (Dalhousie). In the east, Howrah–Hooghly in 1854.
- Railways were built mainly for **British trade and army movement**, with guaranteed interest to British investors. But they also helped national unity and later industry.
- Modern industries by Indians: cotton mills in Bombay (first successful one in **1854, C.N. Davar**), jute mills in Bengal (British-owned, Rishra 1855), **Tata Iron and Steel Company (TISCO), Jamshedpur, 1907** (then in Bihar province's Singhbhum region, now Jharkhand).
- Postal system (Post Office Act **1854**), telegraph (first line Calcutta–Agra **1853–54**).

> **Bihar connection:** **TISCO at Sakchi (Jamshedpur), 1907** was in the region that formed part of the Bihar and Orissa province after 1912 and of Bihar until 2000. Questions sometimes ask "In which present state" — answer **Jharkhand**.

## Social and religious reform movements

From the early 19th century, Indians who learnt Western science and rational ideas began to question **sati, child marriage, caste discrimination, idol worship, purdah** and the ban on widow remarriage. These movements are called the **Indian Renaissance**.

### Bengal

**Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833)** — "**Father of the Indian Renaissance**" / "Father of Modern India".
- Founded **Atmiya Sabha (1814/1815)** and the **Brahmo Sabha (20 August 1828)**, later called the **Brahmo Samaj** — one God (monotheism), against idol worship and rituals.
- Fought against **sati** → Bentinck abolished it (**Regulation XVII, 1829**).
- Supported English and science education; helped found **Hindu College (1817)** with David Hare; started **Vedanta College (1825)**.
- Newspapers: ***Sambad Kaumudi*** (Bengali, 1821), ***Mirat-ul-Akhbar*** (Persian, 1822).
- Title "Raja" given by Mughal emperor **Akbar II**; he went to England as Akbar II's envoy and **died at Bristol (1833)**.

**After him:**
- **Debendranath Tagore** — **Tattvabodhini Sabha (1839)**; revived Brahmo Samaj.
- **Keshab Chandra Sen** — split in **1866**: "Brahmo Samaj of India"; his efforts led to the **Native Marriage Act, 1872** (Civil Marriage Act). Another split in **1878**: **Sadharan Brahmo Samaj** (after Keshab married his minor daughter to the Maharaja of Cooch Behar).
- **Henry Vivian Derozio** — teacher at Hindu College; the **Young Bengal Movement** (radical, rational; died 1831).
- **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar** — campaigned for **widow remarriage** → **Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856**; against child marriage and polygamy; girls' schools; Bengali primer *Barna Parichay*.
- **Ramakrishna Paramahamsa** (priest at Dakshineswar) — all religions are true.
- **Swami Vivekananda (Narendranath Dutta, 1863–1902)** — **Parliament of World Religions, Chicago, 1893**; founded the **Ramakrishna Mission (1897)** with headquarters at **Belur**; service to humanity ("Daridra Narayan"). His birthday, **12 January**, is **National Youth Day**.

### Muslim reform

- **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan** — **Aligarh Movement**; Scientific Society (1864); **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) College, Aligarh, 1875** (became **Aligarh Muslim University, 1920**); journal *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq*. He asked Muslims to stay away from the Congress.
- **Deoband Movement (1866)** — Darul Uloom at Deoband (Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi, Rashid Ahmad Gangohi); orthodox, but **anti-British**; supported the Congress.
- **Ahmadiyya Movement (1889)** — Mirza Ghulam Ahmad, Qadian (Punjab).
- **Wahabi Movement** — **Syed Ahmad of Rae Bareli**; a revivalist and **anti-British** movement; **Patna was its main centre** in India.

> **Bihar connection:** The **Wahabi movement** had its Indian headquarters at **Patna** (led locally by **Wilayat Ali** and **Inayat Ali**). The British crushed it through a series of trials in the 1860s–70s (the Ambala and Patna cases). It was one of the strongest anti-British movements between 1820 and 1870. See [Modern Bihar and 1857](/interview/bpsc-2027/bihar-special/modern-bihar-and-1857).

### Sikh and other reform

- **Singh Sabha Movement (Amritsar, 1873)** and later the **Akali Movement** (Gurdwara reform → **Sikh Gurdwaras Act, 1925**; SGPC 1920).
- **Kuka (Namdhari) Movement** — Bhagat Jawahar Mal and **Baba Ram Singh** (Punjab), religious reform turned anti-British; crushed 1872.
- **Parsi**: Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha (1851) — Naoroji, Naoroji Furdonji.

> **Mains angle:** "How did socio-religious reform movements prepare the ground for nationalism?" Points: rational and scientific thought; pride in India's past (Vedas, Vivekananda); attack on caste and gender evils built a united society; the press and associations formed by reformers became political training grounds; leaders like Ranade moved from social to political reform; limits — mostly upper-caste and urban, some revivalist movements later fed communalism.

## Administrative changes

- **Civil services:** Cornwallis started a covenanted civil service only for Europeans; **Fort William College** (1800) and **Haileybury College** (England, 1806) trained officials. Open competition from **1854/55**. **Satyendranath Tagore** was the **first Indian to qualify for the ICS (1863)**. Maximum age was reduced to **19 (1876, Lytton)** — this angered Indians. **Lee Commission (1924)** → Public Service Commission (1926).
- **Judiciary:** Supreme Court Calcutta 1774; Cornwallis Code 1793; **Indian Penal Code 1860** (drafted by Macaulay's Law Commission); **High Courts Act 1861** — High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (1862). **Patna High Court** was set up in **1916**, four years after Bihar became a separate province.
- **Police:** Cornwallis — thanas under **darogas**; Police Commission (Fraser) 1902 under Curzon.
- **Army:** After 1857 — more Europeans, "martial races" theory, artillery only with Europeans (Peel Commission 1859).
- **Local self-government:** **Mayo's financial decentralisation (1870)**; **Ripon's Resolution of 1882** — "**Father of Local Self-Government in India**".

## Key terms

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Charter Act | Act renewing the East India Company's right to trade and rule, usually for 20 years |
| Dyarchy | "Double rule" — some subjects with elected Indian ministers, some with the British Governor |
| Separate electorate | Seats reserved for a community, voted for only by members of that community |
| Provincial autonomy | Provinces run by elected ministers responsible to the provincial legislature |
| Home Charges | Payments made in Britain from Indian revenue |
| Drain of wealth | Transfer of India's wealth to Britain without return |
| De-industrialisation | Decline of traditional handicraft industries |
| Commercialisation of agriculture | Growing crops for sale/export rather than for food |
| Ryot | Peasant cultivator |
| Mahal | A village or group of villages treated as one revenue unit |
| Downward filtration | Policy of educating the upper classes in the hope that learning spreads downward |
| Shuddhi | Arya Samaj ceremony to bring converts back to Hinduism |
| Renaissance | "Rebirth" — revival of learning and questioning of old customs |

### Answers

1. Regulating Act, 1773; Warren Hastings.
2. Charter Act, 1813 (except tea and trade with China).
3. Lord William Bentinck (Charter Act 1833).
4. End of Company rule, Crown rule, no more annexation, respect for princes and religious tolerance; read by Lord Canning at Allahabad, 1 November 1858.

### Mains questions

**1. "British economic policies converted India into an agricultural colony of Britain." Discuss with reference to Bihar. (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** Before British rule India was a manufacturing economy (textiles); by 1900 it exported raw materials and imported finished goods.
- **De-industrialisation:** one-way free trade; Industrial Revolution; loss of courts; decline of weavers of Bihar and Bengal.
- **Land revenue:** Permanent Settlement in Bihar; rack-renting; no investment in land.
- **Commercialisation:** indigo (Champaran tinkathia), opium (Patna factory), saltpetre — for export, not for local welfare.
- **Drain of wealth:** Home Charges, profits, guaranteed railway interest (Naoroji, R.C. Dutt).
- **Famines:** 1770, 1873–74, 1896–97 — weak relief.
- **Railways and modern industry** mainly for British trade; Indian industry limited (TISCO 1907 an exception).
- **Result:** overdependence on agriculture, poverty, migration from Bihar to Bengal, Assam tea gardens and overseas (indentured labour).
- **Conclusion:** These grievances built economic nationalism and later peasant movements in Bihar.

**2. Evaluate the contribution of the socio-religious reform movements of the 19th century to Indian nationalism. (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** The Indian Renaissance — reason, humanism and pride in India's heritage.
- **Reason and science:** Ram Mohan Roy, Brahmo Samaj; Young Bengal.
- **Social unity:** attack on caste (Phule, Narayana Guru), on sati and child marriage (laws of 1829, 1856, 1891, 1929).
- **Cultural pride:** Arya Samaj ("Go back to the Vedas"), Vivekananda at Chicago, Theosophists praising Indian thought.
- **Women's uplift:** Vidyasagar, Karve, Savitribai Phule.
- **Muslim modernism:** Aligarh; Deoband's anti-British stand.
- **Bihar angle:** Wahabi movement at Patna; Arya Samaj spread in Bihar; Brahmo influence among Bengali community of Patna and Bhagalpur.
- **Limits:** mostly urban, upper-caste; some revivalism fed communal identities.
- **Conclusion:** They created the self-confidence and organisational experience on which the Congress was built.

**3. Trace the constitutional development of India from 1909 to 1935. How far did it satisfy Indian aspirations? (38 marks)**

Model answer outline:
- **Intro:** British policy — "rally the Moderates, divide the communities, give small doses of power".
- **1909:** larger councils; separate electorates — sowed communal politics; no responsible government.
- **1919:** dyarchy in provinces; bicameral Centre; extended separate electorates; Congress called it "disappointing and unsatisfactory".
- **1927–32:** Simon Commission (all-white), Nehru Report, Round Table Conferences, Communal Award.
- **1935:** provincial autonomy (Congress ministries 1937 — in Bihar under Sri Krishna Sinha); federal scheme; safeguards and special powers of the Governor; franchise only ~10%.
- **Bihar angle:** Lord Sinha first Indian Governor (1920); Bihar bicameral legislature (1935); Orissa separated (1936).
- **Assessment:** each step too little and too late; Nehru called the 1935 Act "a machine with strong brakes but no engine".
- **Conclusion:** But the 1935 Act became the main base of the Constitution of 1950.
