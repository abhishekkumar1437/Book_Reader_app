This chapter covers about 600 years, from **Harsha (606 CE)** to the coming of the Turks (late 12th century). Historians call it the **early medieval** period. BPSC asks about Harsha's court and Hiuen Tsang, the Palas (who ruled from Bihar and Bengal and patronised **Nalanda and Vikramshila**), the **tripartite struggle**, Rajput dynasties and their temples, and Chola local self-government. Mains uses this period for questions on Buddhist learning centres in Bihar, temple architecture and the nature of "Indian feudalism".

> **In simple words:** After the Guptas, Harsha built the last big north Indian kingdom of ancient times, with Kannauj as its capital. After him, three powers — the Palas of Bihar–Bengal, the Pratiharas of the west and the Rashtrakutas of the Deccan — fought for 200 years to control Kannauj. The Palas were great patrons of Buddhist universities like Nalanda and Vikramshila. Many Rajput clans ruled north India and built temples such as Khajuraho. In the south, the Cholas built a strong navy and had well-run village assemblies. Meanwhile, Arabs took Sindh (712) and Mahmud of Ghazni raided India (1000–1027).

## Harshavardhana (606–647 CE)

### Background — the Pushyabhutis of Thanesar

| Person | Role |
|---|---|
| **Prabhakaravardhana** | Father; king of **Thanesar** (Sthanvishvara, Haryana); took the title Maharajadhiraja |
| **Rajyavardhana** | Elder brother; killed by **Shashanka**, king of **Gauda** (Bengal; capital **Karnasuvarna**) |
| **Rajyashri** | Sister; married **Grahavarman Maukhari** of Kannauj, who was killed by **Devagupta** of Malwa. Harsha rescued her from the Vindhya forest as she was about to enter fire |
| **Harsha** | Became king in **606 CE** (start of the **Harsha era**) at 16; moved the capital to **Kannauj** |

> **Bihar connection:** **Shashanka of Gauda** is said by Hiuen Tsang to have **cut down the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya** and persecuted Buddhists (a hostile Buddhist account — some historians doubt it). Harsha later controlled **Magadha** and gave large endowments to **Nalanda**.

### Harsha's reign

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| Title | **Siladitya**; also "Lord of the North" (*Sakalottarapathanatha*) |
| Extent | Punjab to Bengal–Odisha (after Shashanka's death), Himalayas to the Narmada |
| **Defeat** | By **Pulakeshin II** (Chalukya of Badami) on the **Narmada** — recorded in the **Aihole inscription** (634 CE) written by **Ravikirti** |
| Religion | Shaiva at first; later inclined to **Mahayana Buddhism** under Hiuen Tsang's influence; honoured Shiva, Surya and Buddha |
| **Kannauj assembly (643 CE)** | Religious assembly presided over by **Hiuen Tsang** to spread Mahayana; attended by 20 kings and thousands of monks |
| **Prayag assembly** (*Mahamoksha Parishad*) | Held every **five years** at the Ganga–Yamuna confluence; Harsha **gave away all his wealth** — Hiuen Tsang saw the sixth one |
| China link | Sent an embassy to the Tang emperor **Taizong** (641 CE) |
| Plays written by Harsha | ***Nagananda***, ***Ratnavali***, ***Priyadarshika*** (Sanskrit) |
| Court | **Banabhatta** — ***Harshacharita*** (biography of Harsha, first historical biography in Sanskrit) and ***Kadambari*** (prose romance); **Mayura** (*Surya Shataka*); **Matanga Divakara** |
| Inscriptions | **Banskhera** and **Madhuban** copper plates; **Sonepat** and Nalanda seals |

**Administration:** feudal in character — officers paid by **grants of land** instead of cash; land revenue **one-sixth**. Hiuen Tsang praised it as mild but noted that roads were unsafe (he was robbed). Important officials: **Avanti** (minister of war and peace), **Simhanada** (commander-in-chief), **Kuntala** (cavalry chief), **Skandagupta** (elephant force).

### Hiuen Tsang (Xuanzang)

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| Travel | Left China **629 CE**, returned **645 CE** (in India c. 630–644) |
| Title | **"Prince of Pilgrims"** |
| Book | ***Si-Yu-Ki*** ("Records of the Western World") |
| Nalanda | Studied **Yogachara** philosophy for about five years under **Shilabhadra**; recorded about **10,000 students** and 1,500 teachers (figures may be exaggerated) and that Nalanda was supported by the revenue of **100 villages** (later 200) |
| Observations | Society: four varnas, untouchables outside towns, people honest; Pataliputra was in decline; Prayag assembly; Kannauj a great city |

Another Chinese monk, **I-Tsing (Yijing)**, came by sea in **671–695 CE**, lived about **ten years at Nalanda**, and described its daily life and monastic rules.

> **Prelims angle:** The Arab merchant **Sulaiman (851 CE)** described the Pratiharas as **hostile to the Arabs** (they blocked Arab expansion from Sindh) and the Rashtrakuta king (whom he calls "Balhara") as **friendly** to them and one of the four greatest kings of the world. **Al-Masudi** visited the Pratihara kingdom in 915–16.

## The Palas of Bihar and Bengal (c. 750–1160s CE)

The Palas are the most important early-medieval dynasty for Bihar. They were **Buddhists** (Mahayana and Vajrayana) and ruled for about **400 years** — one of the longest dynasties in Indian history.

| Ruler | Dates (approx.) | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Gopala** | c. 750–770 | **Elected** by the chiefs/people to end anarchy (**matsyanyaya** — "law of the fish", big fish eating small) — recorded in the **Khalimpur copper plate** of Dharmapala. Founded the **Odantapuri** monastery (Bihar Sharif) per Tibetan tradition |
| **Dharmapala** | c. 770–810 | **Founded Vikramshila** Mahavihara (Bhagalpur, Bihar) and **Somapura Mahavihara** (Paharpur, **Bangladesh** — UNESCO site); held a court at Kannauj and placed **Chakrayudha** on its throne; title **Uttarapathaswamin**; defeated by Nagabhata II and Rashtrakuta Dhruva; patron of Buddhist scholar **Haribhadra** |
| **Devapala** | c. 810–850 | Greatest extent of Pala power; **Monghyr (Munger) copper plate**; at the request of the **Sailendra** king **Balaputradeva** of **Suvarnadvipa (Java–Sumatra)**, granted **five villages** to support a monastery at **Nalanda** (**Nalanda copper plate**) |
| Narayanapala, Rajyapala | 9th–10th c. | Decline; Pratihara gains in Bihar |
| **Mahipala I** | c. 988–1038 | **Revived** the Palas — "second founder"; defeated by **Rajendra Chola** in his Ganga expedition; repaired monasteries at Sarnath and Nalanda |
| **Ramapala** | c. 1077–1130 | Crushed the **Kaivarta revolt** (led by **Divya/Divvoka**) in north Bengal; founded **Ramavati**; his life is told in ***Ramacharita*** by **Sandhyakara Nandi**; founded the **Jagaddala** monastery (Bengal) |
| Madanapala | 12th c. | Among the last; the Senas took Bengal and the Palas were reduced to parts of Bihar |

**Pala capitals and camps:** Pataliputra, **Mudgagiri (Munger)**, Ramavati, Gauda.

**Pala art:**
- **Pala school of sculpture** — images in **black basalt (stone)** and **bronze** (cast by the lost-wax method) of Buddha, Bodhisattvas, Tara, and Hindu deities. Famous hoards: **Kurkihar bronzes** (Gaya district) and **Nalanda bronzes**.
- Tibetan historian **Taranatha** names the artists **Dhiman** and his son **Bitpala**.
- **Pala miniature painting** on **palm leaf** — illustrated Buddhist manuscripts like the ***Ashtasahasrika Prajnaparamita*** — the **earliest Indian miniature paintings**.

### Buddhist universities of Bihar under the Palas

| University | Place (district) | Founder | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nalanda** | Nalanda | Kumaragupta I (5th c.) — expanded by Palas | Mahayana; Devapala's grant; Hiuen Tsang, I-Tsing |
| **Vikramshila** | **Antichak, Kahalgaon (Bhagalpur)** | **Dharmapala** (late 8th–early 9th c.) | **Vajrayana / Tantric Buddhism**; strong links with **Tibet**; famous teacher **Atisha Dipankara Srijnana** (went to Tibet in **1042**); degrees awarded by the king; destroyed by **Bakhtiyar Khalji** c. **1203**; excavated by Patna University (B.P. Sinha) and **ASI (1972–82)** — a large cruciform stupa and monks' cells |
| **Odantapuri** | **Bihar Sharif (Nalanda district)** | **Gopala** (or Dharmapala) | Model for the first Tibetan monastery **Samye**; destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khalji (c. 1193) — he mistook it for a fort |
| Somapura | Paharpur (Bangladesh) | Dharmapala | Largest single monastery south of the Himalayas; UNESCO |
| Jagaddala | North Bengal (Bangladesh) | Ramapala | Late Pala |

> **Bihar connection:** Bihar was the world centre of Buddhist learning from the 5th to the 12th century. Monks from **Tibet, China, Korea, Java, Sumatra and Sri Lanka** studied at Nalanda, Vikramshila and Odantapuri. **Atisha** of Vikramshila reformed Tibetan Buddhism; **Shantarakshita** and **Padmasambhava** (8th century) took Buddhism to Tibet. The destruction of these monasteries by **Bakhtiyar Khalji** around **1193–1203** was a major blow to Buddhism in India. (A new **central university at Vikramshila** has been announced — check the latest status in current affairs.)

> **Mains angle:** "Discuss the role of the Palas in the cultural history of Bihar." Points: long rule (≈400 years); election of Gopala (popular consent); patronage of Nalanda, Vikramshila, Odantapuri; links with Tibet and South-East Asia (Balaputradeva, Atisha); Pala sculpture and bronzes (Kurkihar); palm-leaf miniatures; tolerance (grants to Brahmins too); Sanskrit literature (Ramacharita). Conclude with their legacy in Tibetan Buddhism and Bihar's museums.

### The Senas of Bengal (after the Palas)

**Vijayasena** founded Sena power; **Ballalasena** wrote *Danasagara*; **Lakshmanasena** — court poet **Jayadeva** (***Gita Govinda***); his capital **Nadia (Nabadwip)** was taken by **Bakhtiyar Khalji** around **1204–05**.

## The Rajputs (c. 7th–12th century)

### Origin theories

| Theory | Proposed by |
|---|---|
| **Agnikula** (fire-born) — four clans **Pratihara, Paramara, Chauhan, Chalukya (Solanki)** born from a sacrificial fire at **Mount Abu** | **Chand Bardai**, *Prithviraj Raso* |
| Foreign origin (Scythians, Huns, Gurjaras) | **Col. James Tod**, V.A. Smith |
| Indigenous Kshatriya descent | **C.V. Vaidya**, G.H. Ojha |
| Mixed origin — tribes and foreigners absorbed as Kshatriyas | Most modern historians |

### Major Rajput dynasties

| Dynasty | Region / Capital | Famous rulers | Remember for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gurjara-Pratiharas** | Ujjain, then **Kannauj** | **Nagabhata I** (stopped the Arabs), **Nagabhata II**, **Mihira Bhoja** (Adivaraha), Mahendrapala | Court poet **Rajashekhara** (*Karpuramanjari*, *Kavyamimamsa*); held off Arab expansion |
| **Chauhans (Chahamanas)** | **Ajmer** (Shakambhari) | **Prithviraj III** (Rai Pithora) | Battles of **Tarain 1191 and 1192** with Muhammad Ghori; *Prithviraj Raso* by Chand Bardai |
| **Paramaras** | Malwa — **Dhar** | **Raja Bhoja** (c. 1010–1055) | Scholar-king; *Samarangana Sutradhara* (architecture); **Bhojshala** at Dhar; **Bhojpur** lake and Shiva temple (MP) |
| **Chandelas** | Bundelkhand — **Khajuraho**, Mahoba, Kalinjar | **Yashovarman** (Lakshmana temple), **Dhanga**, **Vidyadhara** (Kandariya Mahadeva temple) | **Khajuraho temples** — **UNESCO 1986**; Nagara style |
| **Solankis (Chalukyas of Gujarat)** | **Anhilwara (Patan)** | **Bhima I** (Somnath sacked by Mahmud, 1025), Jayasimha Siddharaja, **Kumarapala** (Jain, with **Hemachandra**) | **Modhera Sun Temple** (1026); **Dilwara temples, Mount Abu** — built by minister **Vimala** (1031) and later Tejapala |
| **Gahadavalas** | Kannauj & **Varanasi** | Govindachandra, **Jaichand** | Jaichand killed by Ghori at **Chandawar (1194)** |
| **Tomaras** | **Delhi (Dhillika)** | **Anangapala** | Founded Delhi (Lal Kot) |
| **Kalachuris (Chedis)** | **Tripuri** (near Jabalpur) | Gangeyadeva, Lakshmikarna | Central India |
| **Guhilas / Sisodias** | **Mewar** (Chittor) | Bappa Rawal | Later Rana Sanga, Rana Pratap |

**Kashmir:** **Karkota** dynasty — **Lalitaditya Muktapida** built the **Martand Sun Temple**; **Utpala** — Avantivarman (engineer **Suyya** drained the Jhelum); later Lohara. **Kalhana's *Rajatarangini*** (1148–49) is India's first proper historical chronicle.

> **Common mistake:** The **Dilwara temples** are **Jain** temples at **Mount Abu (Rajasthan)** built under the **Solankis** of Gujarat — not by the Paramaras. **Khajuraho** has both **Hindu and Jain** temples, built by the **Chandelas**.

## The Deccan and the South (brief)

### Chalukyas of Badami (Vatapi), c. 543–753

- **Pulakeshin I** founded the kingdom; **Pulakeshin II** (c. 610–642) defeated **Harsha** on the Narmada (**Aihole inscription** by **Ravikirti**, in Sanskrit, 634 CE); killed when **Pallava Narasimhavarman I** captured Vatapi (642) — he took the title **Vatapikonda**.
- Temples: **Aihole** ("cradle of Indian temple architecture" — Lad Khan, Durga temples), **Badami** caves, **Pattadakal** (**UNESCO 1987**; **Virupaksha temple** built by queen **Lokamahadevi** to celebrate victory over the Pallavas). Mix of **Nagara and Dravida** styles (*Vesara*).

### Pallavas of Kanchi, c. 575–897

- **Mahendravarman I** — wrote the satire ***Mattavilasa Prahasana***; rock-cut temples.
- **Narasimhavarman I (Mamalla)** — the **rathas** (monolithic temples) at **Mamallapuram (Mahabalipuram)**; *Arjuna's Penance* relief.
- **Narasimhavarman II (Rajasimha)** — **Shore Temple** (Mahabalipuram) and **Kailasanatha temple** (Kanchi). Mahabalipuram is a **UNESCO site (1984)**.

### Rashtrakutas, c. 753–973

- **Dantidurga** (founder — overthrew the Chalukyas); **Krishna I** built the rock-cut **Kailasa temple at Ellora (Cave 16)**; **Dhruva**, **Govinda III** (tripartite struggle); **Amoghavarsha I** (capital **Manyakheta**; wrote the Kannada work ***Kavirajamarga***; Jain-inclined; praised by Sulaiman); **Krishna III** (defeated the Cholas at Takkolam, 949).

### The Cholas (c. 850–1279) — brief

| Ruler | Achievement |
|---|---|
| **Vijayalaya** (c. 850) | Founded the imperial Cholas; capital **Thanjavur** |
| **Parantaka I** | **Uttaramerur inscriptions (919 and 921 CE)** — detailed rules for elections to village assemblies |
| **Rajaraja I** (985–1014) | Built the **Brihadeshvara (Rajarajeshvara) temple, Thanjavur (c. 1010)**; conquered **northern Sri Lanka** and the **Maldives**; strong navy; land survey |
| **Rajendra I** (1014–1044) | **Ganga expedition** — defeated **Mahipala I (Pala)**; title **Gangaikondachola**; new capital **Gangaikondacholapuram**; **naval expedition to Srivijaya** (Sumatra–Malaya, c. 1025); conquered all of Sri Lanka |
| Kulottunga I | Sent an embassy of 72 merchants to China (1077) |

**Chola administration:** empire → **mandalam** (province) → **valanadu** → **nadu** → **kurram/kottam** → **ur** (village).

| Assembly | Who |
|---|---|
| **Ur** | General assembly of ordinary villages |
| **Sabha / Mahasabha** | Assembly of **Brahmin villages** (*brahmadeya*) — worked through committees called **variyams** |
| **Nagaram** | Assembly of **merchants** in towns |

The **Uttaramerur** rules: members chosen by **lot (kudavolai** — names written on palm leaves put in a pot); candidates needed property, age 35–70, Vedic learning; corrupt people and their relatives were barred. **Chola bronzes** (Nataraja), the **Dravida temple style**, and the "Great Living Chola Temples" (Thanjavur, Gangaikondacholapuram, Darasuram) — **UNESCO 1987 (extended 2004)**.

## Mithila and Bihar in the early medieval age

> **Bihar connection:**
> - **Karnata dynasty of Mithila**: founded by **Nanyadeva** in **1097 CE**, capital **Simraongarh** (now in Nepal's Terai). The last king **Harisimhadeva** fled to Nepal when **Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq** attacked (1324–25). His minister-poet **Jyotirishvara Thakur** wrote ***Varna Ratnakara*** — the **earliest known prose work in Maithili**. Harisimhadeva is also credited with the **panji** system (genealogical records for Maithil marriages).
> - **Mithila school of philosophy**: **Vachaspati Mishra** (commentaries on all six darshanas, c. 9th–10th century) and **Gangesha Upadhyaya** — founder of **Navya-Nyaya** (new logic) with ***Tattvachintamani*** (c. 14th century).
> - **Later Guptas of Magadha** (6th–8th century) — Adityasena's **Aphsad inscription** (Gaya district).
> - The **Palas** ruled from Bihar; monasteries at **Nalanda, Vikramshila, Odantapuri**; bronzes from **Kurkihar**.

## Nature of the period: "Indian feudalism"

Historians **R.S. Sharma** and **D.D. Kosambi** describe this period as **feudal**: land grants (*agrahara*, *brahmadeya*) with administrative rights; officers paid in land; many **samantas** (feudal chiefs); decline of trade, towns and coins (after 600 CE); forced labour; peasants tied to land. Others (**B.D. Chattopadhyaya**, **Harbans Mukhia**) argue that this was a period of **state formation** in new regions, agrarian expansion and growth of regional cultures, not a decline.

> **Mains angle:** For "Characterise the early medieval period", give both views: (1) feudal decentralisation and decline (Sharma); (2) regional state formation, temple-centred economy, regional languages (Maithili, Bengali, Kannada, Tamil devotional literature), and new art — so the period was creative, not dark.

### Mains questions

**1. Evaluate Harshavardhana as a ruler, with reference to the accounts of Hiuen Tsang and Banabhatta.** (300 words)

- *Intro:* 606–647 CE; last great ancient north Indian king; sources Harshacharita, Si-Yu-Ki, inscriptions.
- Conquests and limits (Shashanka; Pulakeshin II on the Narmada).
- Administration: feudal, land grants, mild taxes; roads unsafe.
- Religion: tolerance; Kannauj and Prayag assemblies; Mahayana leaning.
- Patron of learning: Nalanda's 100 villages; Bana, Mayura; his own plays.
- Diplomacy with China.
- Limits: personal empire, collapsed after his death; sources biased (court poet, Buddhist pilgrim).
- *Conclusion:* An able, cultured ruler of a transitional age.

**2. Discuss the contribution of the Pala rulers to the culture of Bihar.** (300 words)

- *Intro:* c. 750–1160s; Bihar–Bengal; Gopala's election.
- Buddhist universities: Nalanda (Devapala), Vikramshila (Dharmapala), Odantapuri.
- International links: Tibet (Atisha), Sailendras (Balaputradeva), Sri Lanka.
- Art: black stone and bronze sculpture (Kurkihar, Nalanda), Dhiman and Bitpala; palm-leaf miniatures.
- Literature: Ramacharita; Sanskrit and early Apabhramsha (Charyapadas).
- Religious tolerance: grants to Brahmins too.
- *Conclusion:* The Palas made Bihar the Buddhist cultural capital of Asia until the Turkish conquest.

**3. Describe the local self-government of the Cholas.** (200 words)

- *Intro:* Cholas known for strong centre with active villages.
- Administrative units: mandalam to ur.
- Ur, Sabha, Nagaram.
- Uttaramerur: qualifications, lot system, committees (variyams) for gardens, tanks, justice.
- Functions: land, tax, temples, irrigation, disputes.
- *Conclusion:* An early example of grassroots democracy; compare with today's Panchayati Raj.
