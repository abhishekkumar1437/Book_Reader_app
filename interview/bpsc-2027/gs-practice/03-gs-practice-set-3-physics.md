**Q3.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Light & Optics</span>

Explain the principle of a laser and the properties that make laser light special. Discuss its applications in medicine, industry, communication and defence. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** A laser produces an intense, narrow beam of light by **stimulated emission**, an idea Einstein proposed in 1917. Theodore Maiman built the first one (a ruby laser) in 1960.

**Principle:**

- **Population inversion:** Energy (light, electric discharge or current) is "pumped" in so that more atoms are in an excited state than in the ground state.
- **Stimulated emission:** A passing photon makes an excited atom emit an identical photon with the same wavelength, phase and direction, so the light is amplified.
- **Optical resonator:** Two mirrors, one partly transparent, bounce the light back and forth to build up the beam that comes out.

**Properties:** Monochromatic (one wavelength), coherent (waves in step), highly directional (spreads very little) and very intense.

**Applications:**

- **Medicine:** LASIK eye surgery (excimer laser reshapes the cornea), retina repair, breaking kidney stones, cancer and skin treatment.
- **Industry:** Precise cutting, welding and drilling; barcode scanners, laser printers, 3D scanning.
- **Communication:** Semiconductor laser diodes send data through optical fibres, which carry the internet backbone and BharatNet.
- **Science and measurement:** LiDAR mapping, lunar laser ranging, LIGO's laser interferometers. Nobel Prizes for optical tweezers and ultrashort pulses (2018) and attosecond pulses (2023) built on lasers.
- **Defence:** Range finders, target designators and directed-energy weapons; DRDO demonstrated a high-power laser system against drones in 2025.

**Conclusion:** From eye clinics to missile defence, lasers are a core technology, and India's photonics and quantum missions depend on building them at home.

**Q7.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Waves & Sound</span>

What are beats in sound? How are they used to tune musical instruments? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

When two sounds of slightly different frequencies (f₁ and f₂) are heard together, they add up (superpose) so that the loudness rises and falls regularly. These periodic changes are called beats, and the number of beats per second equals **f₁ − f₂**. The ear can pick them out when the difference is below about 10 Hz. A musician sounds a string together with a standard note and adjusts the tension until the beats slow down and vanish, which means the frequencies now match. Beats are also used to find the unknown frequency of a tuning fork.

**Q8.** <span class="qa-tag">Numerical · Electricity</span>

A school replaces ten 60 W filament bulbs with ten 9 W LED bulbs. Each bulb is used 5 hours a day. If electricity costs ₹6 per unit, how much money does the school save in 30 days?

**Answer: (b) ₹459**

Trap: (d) ₹540 is the whole bill for the old filament bulbs (90 units), forgetting that the LEDs also use power.

**Q11.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Mechanics</span>

Why is it easier to pull a lawn roller than to push it? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

The handle is held at an angle to the ground. When we **pull**, the force has an upward vertical part that lifts slightly on the roller, so its effective weight and the normal reaction from the ground fall; friction becomes smaller. When we **push**, the vertical part of the force acts downward, adding to the weight, so the normal reaction and friction increase. Hence pulling needs less effort.

**Q13.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Electronics & Communication</span>

What is a transistor? Why is it called the building block of modern electronics? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

A transistor is a three-terminal semiconductor device (emitter, base, collector in a BJT; source, gate, drain in a MOSFET) in which a small signal at one terminal controls a large current between the other two. So it works as an **amplifier** and as an on/off **switch** — the 1 and 0 of digital logic. Invented at Bell Labs in 1947 (Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley), it can now be made a few nanometres wide, so a single processor chip holds billions of them.

**Q15.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Electricity</span>

Why is nichrome used for the heating element of an electric heater or iron, while copper is used for connecting wires? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

Heat produced is H = I²Rt, so a heating element needs **high resistance**. Nichrome (an alloy of nickel and chromium) has a high resistivity, a very high melting point (about 1,400 °C), and does not oxidise (burn) easily even when red-hot. Connecting wires should waste as little energy as possible, so they are made of **copper**, which has very low resistivity, is cheap and bends easily. (Aluminium is used in long power lines because it is light.)

**Q19.** <span class="qa-tag">Numerical · Waves & Sound</span>

A ship sends an ultrasonic pulse straight down towards the seabed and receives its echo 4 seconds later. If the speed of sound in seawater is 1,500 m/s, how deep is the sea at that point?

(a) 1,500 m &nbsp; (b) 3,000 m &nbsp; (c) 6,000 m &nbsp; (d) 375 m

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer: (b) 3,000 m**

The pulse goes down and comes back, so it covers twice the depth.

Total distance = speed × time = 1,500 × 4 = 6,000 m.

Depth = 6,000 ÷ 2 = **3,000 m**.

Option (c) forgets to halve the distance. Sound travels about four times faster in water than in air (about 343 m/s), so SONAR timings are much shorter than echoes in air.

**Q28.** <span class="qa-tag">MCQ · Gravitation & Space</span>

Consider the following statements:

1. The escape velocity from the Earth's surface is about 11.2 km/s.
2. The escape velocity of a body depends on its mass, so a heavier satellite needs a higher escape velocity.
3. The Moon has almost no atmosphere, partly because its escape velocity (about 2.4 km/s) is low.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only &nbsp; (b) 1 and 3 only &nbsp; (c) 2 and 3 only &nbsp; (d) 1, 2 and 3

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer: (b) 1 and 3 only**

**Q47.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Heat & Thermodynamics</span>

Explain the physics of the greenhouse effect. Why do only some gases in the atmosphere act as greenhouse gases, and how does human activity strengthen the effect? (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** The greenhouse effect is the warming of the Earth's surface because some atmospheric gases absorb and re-emit the heat radiated by the Earth. It is natural and necessary: without it the average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about +15 °C.

**Key points:**

- **Shortwave in, longwave out:** By Wien's law, the hot Sun (surface about 5,800 K) emits mostly visible and near-infrared light, which passes through the air. The cooler Earth (about 288 K) re-radiates the energy as long-wave infrared (peak near 10 μm).
- **Selective absorbers:** Water vapour, CO₂, methane, nitrous oxide and ozone absorb this infrared because their molecular vibrations change their electric dipole. N₂ and O₂ (about 99% of dry air) are symmetric two-atom molecules and do not absorb it.
- **Re-emission:** The gases radiate energy in all directions, part of it back down, so the surface warms until outgoing and incoming energy balance again.
- **Human enhancement:** Burning fossil fuels has raised CO₂ from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to over 420 ppm. Methane (rice paddies, livestock, leaks) is about 28 times stronger than CO₂ per tonne over 100 years.
- **Feedbacks:** A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, and melting ice lowers albedo, so the warming is amplified.
- **A precise point:** A glass greenhouse stays warm mainly because it stops convection, not only because it traps infrared, so the name is an analogy.

**Conclusion:** The physics is settled; the policy task is cutting emissions (India's net-zero target of 2070) and adapting to heat waves, which already hitBihar.

**Q65.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Heat & Thermodynamics</span>

Explain how a refrigerator or air conditioner transfers heat from a cooler space to a warmer one without violating the laws of thermodynamics. Discuss the energy and environmental issues linked with India's rising demand for cooling. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** Heat flows naturally from hot to cold. A refrigerator or AC reverses this by using work, which the second law permits (Clausius statement).

**Working (vapour-compression cycle):**

- **Evaporator:** The liquid refrigerant at low pressure evaporates inside the cabinet or room, taking in latent heat and cooling it.
- **Compressor:** It compresses the vapour, raising its pressure and temperature. This is where electrical work is used.
- **Condenser:** The hot vapour gives up heat through coils at the back (or the outdoor unit) and turns back into liquid.
- **Expansion valve:** Pressure drops suddenly, the refrigerant cools, and the cycle repeats.

- Cooling demand is rising fast with heat waves, urbanisation and incomes, adding to peak electricity load.

- The **India Cooling Action Plan (2019)** was among the first such national plans. It aims to cut cooling demand by 20–25% by 2037–38 through efficient equipment, building design and skilled technicians.

**Q91.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Gravitation & Space</span>

How does a satellite stay in orbit? Explain orbital velocity and escape velocity, and discuss the main types of Earth orbits and the purposes for which India uses them. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** A satellite is a body that keeps falling towards the Earth but moves sideways so fast that the ground curves away beneath it. Gravity supplies the centripetal force needed for this circular motion.

**Orbital velocity:** v = √(GM/r). Close to the surface it is about 7.9 km/s, and it becomes smaller for higher orbits. It does not depend on the satellite's mass. A higher orbit also means a longer period (Kepler's third law).

**Escape velocity:** √2 times the orbital velocity at the same place, about 11.2 km/s from the surface. A spacecraft must reach it (with help from gravity assists and orbit-raising manoeuvres) to leave Earth for the Moon, Mars or beyond, as Chandrayaan and Mangalyaan did step by step.

**Types of orbits and Indian uses:**
- **Low Earth orbit (about 200–2,000 km):** short periods (about 90 minutes); space stations and human spaceflight (Gaganyaan is planned for about 400 km).
- **Sun-synchronous polar orbit (about 500–900 km):** passes over each place at the same local time; used by Cartosat, Resourcesat, Oceansat and RISAT for mapping, crops and disasters. PSLV is the workhorse for this orbit.
- **Medium and inclined geosynchronous orbits:** used for navigation satellites.
- **Geostationary orbit (about 35,786 km above the equator):** 24-hour period; INSAT/GSAT for TV, telecom and weather.
- **Lagrange points:** gravity-balanced points for observatories.

**Conclusion:** Choosing the right orbit is a physics problem with direct development pay-offs, from weather warnings in Bihar's flood season to banking, navigation and broadband.

Mass is the amount of matter in a body; it is a scalar, measured in kilograms, and is the same everywhere. <details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

- In a flowing fluid, where speed is higher the pressure is lower (conservation of energy).
- An aeroplane wing (aerofoil) makes air flow faster over its top, giving lift (together with the downward deflection of air, Newton's third law).
- Other uses: carburettors, atomisers and sprayers, venturi meters to measure flow; it also explains roofs lifting in cyclones and the danger of standing close to a fast train.

<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

To move in a circle, a body needs a centripetal force towards the centre, equal to mv²/r. By leaning inward, the cyclist makes the reaction of the road act at an angle; its horizontal part supplies the centripetal force, together with friction, and keeps him from toppling outward. The angle of lean is given by tan θ = v²/(rg), so a higher speed or a sharper curve needs a larger lean.

**Q143.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Physics in Technology</span>

How does a radar find the distance and speed of an aircraft, and how does stealth technology make an aircraft hard to detect? (answer in about 70 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

**Radar** (RAdio Detection And Ranging) sends out pulses of radio waves or microwaves and catches the echo. Distance = (speed of light × time taken) ÷ 2; the **Doppler shift** in the echo's frequency gives the target's speed. **Stealth** reduces the aircraft's **radar cross-section**: flat, angled surfaces and hidden engines reflect waves away from the radar, and **radar-absorbing materials** soak up the rest. Engine heat (infrared signature) is also reduced. Examples: B-2, F-35; India's planned AMCA.

</div>
<details class="qa-a">

**Answer: (a) Behind him**

Once the coin leaves the hand, no horizontal force acts on it, so by inertia it keeps the horizontal speed the train had at that moment.
