**Q4.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Polity</span>

"Public Interest Litigation (PIL) has been a powerful tool for justice in India, but it has also been misused." Critically examine. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** PIL relaxes the traditional rule of *locus standi*. Any public-spirited person can move the Supreme Court (Art. 32) or a High Court (Art. 226) for people who cannot approach the court themselves. It was developed in the late 1970s and 1980s by Justices P. N. Bhagwati and V. R. Krishna Iyer, and settled in *S. P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981).

**Achievements:**
- **Rights of the poor and prisoners:** *Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1979) led to the release of thousands of undertrials held in Bihar jails for longer than their maximum sentence. It made speedy trial part of Article 21.
- **Epistolary jurisdiction:** letters and postcards were treated as petitions, as in the Bhagalpur blindings case.
- **Environment:** the M. C. Mehta cases (Ganga pollution, CNG for Delhi buses).
- **Gender justice and workers:** the Vishaka guidelines (1997) on sexual harassment, and the bonded labour cases (*Bandhua Mukti Morcha*, 1984).
- **Governance:** the right to food case (*PUCL*, 2001) led to mid-day meals in schools.

**Concerns:**
- Frivolous, publicity or "paise" interest litigation clogs courts that already have huge pending cases.
- Charges of **judicial overreach** into executive and policy matters, which strains the separation of powers.
- Courts often cannot enforce or monitor complex orders.

**Safeguards:** In *State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal* (2010), the Supreme Court laid down guidelines: check the petitioner's bona fides, and impose costs on frivolous petitions.

**Conclusion:** PIL should stay a tool for the voiceless, used with judicial restraint. Strong legal aid and Lok Adalats, and quicker executive action, would reduce the need for it.

**Q14.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Environment</span>

How do chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) destroy stratospheric ozone? Why does the "ozone hole" appear over Antarctica in spring? (answer in about 70 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

CFCs from old refrigerants and aerosols are stable, so they drift up into the stratosphere. There, UV rays break them and release **chlorine atoms**. Each chlorine atom acts as a catalyst and can destroy thousands of ozone (O₃) molecules. Over Antarctica, the very cold winter polar vortex forms **polar stratospheric clouds**. Reactions on these clouds store "active" chlorine, which is released when sunlight returns in **spring (September–October)**. Ozone then falls below 220 Dobson Units (the "hole"). The result is more UV-B at the surface: skin cancer, cataract and harm to plankton. Recovery over Antarctica is expected around 2066.

</details>

**Q18.** <span class="qa-tag">GMA · GMA</span>

A train 150 m long runs at 54 km/h. In the same direction on a parallel track, another train 100 m long runs at 36 km/h. How long will the faster train take to pass the slower one completely?

(a) 10 seconds &nbsp; (b) 25 seconds &nbsp; (c) 50 seconds &nbsp; (d) 45 seconds

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer: (c) 50 seconds**

The trains move in the same direction, so relative speed = 54 − 36 = 18 km/h = 18 × 5/18 = 5 m/s.

Distance to be covered = sum of the lengths = 150 + 100 = 250 m.

Time = 250 ÷ 5 = **50 seconds**.

(Had they run in opposite directions: relative speed 90 km/h = 25 m/s, so the time would be 10 s — the trap option.)

**Q19.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Economy</span>

"Retail participation in India's stock markets has grown rapidly, but so have the risks to small investors." Examine this statement and the role of SEBI. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** Since 2020, India's equity market has become far more retail-driven. Demat accounts rose from about 4 crore (2020) to around 20 crore by 2025, and monthly SIP inflows into mutual funds crossed ₹25,000 crore in 2025.

**Drivers of growth:**
- Cheap smartphones and data, e-KYC, UPI-linked payments and discount brokers.
- Low bank deposit rates and a long equity rally.
- SIPs that make regular investing easy. Small towns now give a growing share of new investors.

**Benefits:** household savings move into productive capital. Domestic investors balance out the selling by foreign portfolio investors. Companies find it easier to raise capital through IPOs.

**Risks:**
- **Derivatives speculation:** a SEBI study (2024) found that about 93% of individual futures-and-options traders lost money in FY22–FY24, with combined losses of about ₹1.8 lakh crore.
- "Finfluencers", tips on social media and pump-and-dump schemes in small and SME stocks.
- Low financial literacy, especially in states like Bihar, which remain under-represented among investors relative to their population.

**SEBI's response:**
- In November 2024 it raised the minimum futures-and-options contract size, limited weekly expiries to one benchmark index per exchange and required upfront collection of option premiums.
- Rules for finfluencers, closer scrutiny of SME IPOs, and faster settlement (T+1).
- Investor-education drives and grievance redress (SCORES, online dispute resolution).

**Conclusion:** Wider participation strengthens the market only if investors are informed. Continued regulation, financial literacy (including in Hindi and regional languages) and nudging people towards long-term investing are the way forward.

**Q67.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Polity</span>

Discuss the main devices of parliamentary control over the executive in India. Why is it often said that this control has weakened in recent decades? (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** In a parliamentary system the executive is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)). Parliament enforces this through daily questioning, debate, financial control and committees.

**Devices of control:**
- **Question Hour** (first hour of sitting): starred questions (oral, with supplementaries), unstarred (written) and short-notice questions. **Zero Hour** (an Indian practice since the 1960s) lets members raise urgent issues.
- **Motions:** adjournment motion (Lok Sabha only, implies censure), calling-attention, censure and **no-confidence motions** (need the support of 50 members). A no-confidence motion removes the whole ministry.
- **Financial control:** voting on demands for grants, cut motions, and audit through the CAG's reports.
- **Committees:** the Public Accounts Committee, Estimates Committee, Committee on Public Undertakings, and the 24 Departmentally Related Standing Committees (since 1993), which examine Bills and budgets in detail.

**Why control has weakened:**
- **Fewer sittings:** the Lok Sabha met about 120 days a year in the 1950s, but only about 55–70 days in recent years.
- **Less committee scrutiny:** the share of Bills sent to committees fell from 60–70% (14th–15th Lok Sabha) to under 20% (17th) (PRS data).
- **Disruptions** wash out Question Hour. Most demands for grants pass without discussion through the "guillotine".
- Strong majorities and the anti-defection whip reduce independent scrutiny by members.

**Way forward:** fix a minimum number of sitting days, refer major Bills to committees by default, set aside time for the Opposition, and strengthen research support for members.

**Conclusion:** Accountability to Parliament is the core of the Westminster model. Reviving these devices is essential for good governance.

**Q119.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Environment</span>

"Climate finance is the weakest link in the global climate regime." Discuss with reference to the outcomes of COP29 and COP30 and India's position. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** Climate finance means money flowing, mainly from developed to developing countries, to cut emissions (mitigation) and cope with impacts (adaptation, loss and damage). It rests on the UNFCCC principle of **common but differentiated responsibilities (CBDR-RC)**.

**Track record:**
- At Copenhagen (2009), developed countries promised **USD 100 billion a year by 2020**. It was reached only in 2022, and largely as loans, not grants.
- **COP29, Baku (2024)** adopted the **New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG)**: developed countries are to lead in mobilising at least **USD 300 billion a year by 2035**, with a call to scale up all flows to USD 1.3 trillion. India called the figure too small and objected to how it was adopted.
- The **Loss and Damage Fund** was operationalised at COP28, Dubai (2023), but pledges are small.
- **COP30, Belém (November 2025)** called for at least **tripling adaptation finance by 2035** and launched the **Tropical Forest Forever Facility**, but a fossil-fuel phase-out roadmap was left out of the final text.

**Why finance is the weak link:**
- Pledges are voluntary, and there is no agreed definition of climate finance.
- The money is loan-heavy, which adds to the debt of poor countries.
- Adaptation gets far less money than mitigation.
- Access through bodies like the Green Climate Fund is slow.

**India's position and action:** India insists on public, grant-based finance and on technology transfer. At home it issues **sovereign green bonds** and is building a carbon credit trading scheme. Climate-vulnerable states like Bihar (floods and heatwaves) need adaptation money most.

**Conclusion:** Without predictable, affordable finance, NDCs cannot be met. Reform of multilateral development banks and a clear definition of climate finance are urgent before COP31 in Antalya.
