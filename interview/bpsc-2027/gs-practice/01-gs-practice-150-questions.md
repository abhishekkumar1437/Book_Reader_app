**Q1.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Bihar</span>

Why is north Bihar so prone to floods? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

About **73%** of Bihar's area is flood-affected, mostly in the north. Reasons: (1) Himalayan rivers from Nepal — **Kosi, Gandak, Bagmati, Kamla-Balan, Mahananda** — bring huge water during heavy rain in their upper catchments; (2) they carry heavy **silt**, which raises riverbeds; (3) the plain is very **flat**, so water spreads and drains slowly; (4) rivers like the Kosi **shift course** (the 2008 Kusaha breach); (5) **embankment breaches** and blocked drainage; (6) the **Ganga backwater** effect when the Ganga is in spate.

**Q6.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Bihar</span>

Discuss the role of Bihar in the Quit India Movement of 1942. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** After the "Do or Die" call of 8 August 1942, Bihar saw one of the most intense risings in India; Viceroy Linlithgow called the August rising the most serious rebellion since 1857, with Bihar and eastern UP at its centre.

**Key points**
- **Arrests and the Secretariat firing:** Rajendra Prasad was arrested at Sadaquat Ashram on 9 August. On **11 August 1942**, students tried to hoist the tricolour on the Patna Secretariat; firing ordered by DM W. G. Archer killed **seven students** — Umakant Prasad Sinha, Ramanand Singh, Satish Prasad Jha, Jagatpati Kumar, Devipada Choudhary, Rajendra Singh and Ramgovind Singh.
- **Mass action:** rail lines, telegraph wires and police stations were attacked; British control collapsed for days in parts of Saran, Muzaffarpur, Bhagalpur, Munger and Patna.
- **Repression:** aerial machine-gunning of crowds, collective fines and mass arrests.
- **Underground movement:** **Jayaprakash Narayan** escaped from Hazaribagh jail (9 November 1942) and organised the **Azad Dasta** from the Nepal Terai; JP and Lohia were freed from Hanuman Nagar jail in 1943.
- **Women and peasants:** Tara Rani Srivastava hoisted the flag at Siwan after her husband was shot; students, socialists and Kisan Sabha workers formed the core.

**Significance:** The rising showed that British rule rested on force alone, built leaders such as JP and Karpoori Thakur, and gave Bihar's later politics its socialist stream.

**Conclusion:** Bihar converted Gandhi's call into a people's revolt; the Shaheed Smarak at Patna remains its symbol.

**Q8.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · History</span>

"The Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22) turned the Indian national movement into a mass movement." Examine. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** Launched by Gandhi on 1 August 1920 and adopted by the Congress at Calcutta (September 1920) and Nagpur (December 1920), the movement joined the Khilafat issue, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and the Rowlatt Act into one national campaign for Swaraj.

**Key points:**

1. **New social base:** peasants, workers, students, women and traders joined for the first time on a large scale; the movement was no longer limited to the educated middle class.
2. **Hindu–Muslim unity:** the alliance with the Khilafat leaders (Ali brothers) brought Muslims into the national movement in large numbers.
3. **Programme anyone could follow:** boycott of foreign cloth, courts, councils and government schools; bonfires of foreign cloth; khadi and charkha; Tilak Swaraj Fund (over ₹1 crore).
4. **National education:** Jamia Millia Islamia, Kashi Vidyapith, Gujarat Vidyapith and **Bihar Vidyapith** (Patna, 1921, at Sadaqat Ashram founded by Mazharul Haque; Rajendra Prasad was a key figure).
5. **Organisation:** the Nagpur session reorganised the Congress into linguistic provincial committees with a 4-anna membership, making it a mass party.
6. **Limits:** the movement was withdrawn after the **Chauri Chaura** violence (5 February 1922); Swaraj was not won "within a year", and Hindu–Muslim unity later weakened.

**Conclusion:** Though withdrawn, the movement removed the fear of the Raj, spread nationalism to villages and gave the Congress the mass base it used in the Civil Disobedience and Quit India movements.

Explain the President's ordinance-making power and the limits on it. (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

Under **Article 123**, the President can issue an ordinance when **either House is not in session** and immediate action is needed, on the advice of the Union Cabinet. Limits: (1) it has the same force as an Act but must be laid before Parliament and **lapses six weeks after Parliament reassembles** (maximum life about six months and six weeks); (2) it can be made only on subjects Parliament can legislate on; (3) it is open to **judicial review** — in **Krishna Kumar Singh (2017)** the Supreme Court held repeated re-promulgation a "fraud on the Constitution".

**Q17.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Geography</span>

What are Western Disturbances? How do they affect weather and agriculture in north India? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

Western Disturbances are **extra-tropical low-pressure systems** that start over the **Mediterranean region** and travel east, carried by the subtropical westerly jet stream, mainly in **winter (December–February)**. They bring **snow** to the Himalaya and light **winter rain** to Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, western UP and sometimes Bihar. This rain is very useful for **rabi crops**, especially wheat. But strong ones also bring hailstorms, cold waves and fog that damage crops and disrupt transport.

**Q22.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Polity</span>

Critically analyse the provisions for National Emergency under Article 352 and the safeguards added by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** Article 352 lets the President proclaim a National Emergency if the security of India or any part is threatened by **war, external aggression or armed rebellion**. It has been used three times: 1962 (China war), 1971 (Pakistan war) and 1975 ("internal disturbance").

**Effects:**

1. The federal structure becomes unitary: the Centre can give directions to states and Parliament can make laws on State List subjects (Art. 353, 250).
2. Lok Sabha's term can be extended by one year at a time.
3. Art. 19 is suspended automatically (Art. 358) and other rights can be suspended by Presidential order (Art. 359).

**44th Amendment safeguards (after the misuse of 1975–77):**

1. "Internal disturbance" replaced by **"armed rebellion"**.
2. The President acts only on the **written advice of the Union Cabinet**.
3. Approval by **both Houses within one month** (earlier two months) by **special majority**; renewal every **six months**.
4. The **Lok Sabha can revoke** it by a simple majority; one-tenth of members can ask for a special sitting.
5. **Arts. 20 and 21 can never be suspended**; Art. 19 is suspended only for war or external aggression, not armed rebellion.
6. The 38th Amendment's bar on judicial review was removed; **Minerva Mills (1980)** confirmed the proclamation can be challenged.

**Criticism:** Emergency still concentrates great power in the Centre, and judicial review is limited to mala fide or irrelevant grounds.

**Conclusion:** The 44th Amendment converted Art. 352 from a tool open to misuse into a power with strong checks — no emergency has been declared since 1977.

[Read more](/interview/bpsc-2027/polity/centre-state-relations-and-emergency)

</details>

**Q24.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Economy</span>

Distinguish between revenue deficit, fiscal deficit and primary deficit. Which one best shows the government's fresh borrowing need? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

- **Revenue deficit** = revenue expenditure − revenue receipts; it shows borrowing used for day-to-day spending.
- **Fiscal deficit** = total expenditure − (revenue receipts + non-debt capital receipts such as loan recoveries and disinvestment). It equals the **total borrowing** need of the year, so it is the best measure.
- **Primary deficit** = fiscal deficit − interest payments; it shows borrowing for current needs, excluding past debt.

Budget 2026–27 targets a fiscal deficit of 4.3% of GDP.

**Q120.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Economy</span>

Critically examine how effective the flexible inflation targeting framework has been in controlling inflation in India. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** The RBI Act was amended in 2016 to give the RBI a legal mandate of **flexible inflation targeting (FIT)**: CPI (combined) inflation of **4% with a band of ±2%**, set by the Union Government. A six-member **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** (three RBI members including the Governor, who has a casting vote, and three external members) fixes the repo rate.

**Achievements:**

1. **Clear anchor:** inflation expectations became more stable compared with the 2010–13 period of near double-digit inflation.
2. **Transparency and accountability:** published MPC minutes; if inflation stays outside the band for three straight quarters, the RBI must explain the reasons to the Government.
3. **Flexibility:** it allowed deep rate cuts in 2020 (COVID) and rate changes in both directions later, including the hike to 5.50% in October 2026.

**Limitations:**

1. **Supply-side food shocks** (vegetables, pulses, monsoon failure) are not controlled by interest rates; food still has a large weight (36.75% in the new CPI, base 2024).
2. **Weak transmission:** deposit and loan rates adjust slowly; informal credit lies outside the system.
3. **Fiscal dominance:** high government borrowing and fuel tax/subsidy decisions affect prices.
4. **Imported inflation** from crude oil and a weaker rupee.
5. **Growth trade-off** during slowdowns.

**Conclusion:** FIT has improved credibility, but it works best with supply-side steps — better storage, buffer stocks, agricultural market reforms and fiscal discipline. Bihar, where food is a large share of household spending, gains most from stable food prices.

**Q144.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Geography</span>

Discuss the factors that explain the location of the sugar industry in India. Why has it shifted from north India to the peninsular states? Suggest how Bihar can revive its sugar industry. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Intro:** Sugar is India's second-largest agro-based industry after cotton textiles. India is among the world's top two sugar producers, and UP and Maharashtra lead.

**Location factors:**
- **Raw-material based:** cane is bulky, loses weight and sucrose within 24–48 hours of cutting, so mills must be close to the fields.
- Tropical climate, assured irrigation, fertile alluvial or black soils.
- Cheap labour, transport and nearby markets.

**Why the shift to the south (Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu):**
- Tropical cane has **higher sucrose content** and higher yield per hectare.
- **Longer crushing season** (about 6–7 months against about 4 in the north).
- Strong **cooperative mills** (Maharashtra), newer and bigger plants, better management.
- Coastal ports help exports.

**Bihar angle:** In the 1950s Bihar had many sugar mills in West Champaran, Gopalganj, Siwan and East Champaran, helped by the calcareous Balsundari soil. Most closed due to old machinery, low recovery, floods and dues to farmers. Revival steps: modernise and reopen mills; use high-yield varieties; make ethanol from cane and molasses (Bihar's **Ethanol Production Promotion Policy, 2021** was the first such state policy); co-generation of power from bagasse; pay farmers on time.

**Conclusion:** Cane is perishable, so sugar must stay a "field-side" industry. Ethanol blending gives north India, and Bihar especially, a chance to make the industry profitable again.
