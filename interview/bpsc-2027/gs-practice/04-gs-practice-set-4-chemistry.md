**Q2.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Matter & Its Nature</span>

A mixture of crushed ice and common salt is used to set kulfi and ice cream, and salt is spread on icy roads in cold countries. Explain the principle behind both. (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

Both use the **depression of the freezing point**, a colligative property: a dissolved solute lowers the freezing point of a solvent, the more so the more particles it adds. Salt dissolves in the thin film of water on ice, so the ice keeps melting even below 0 °C, taking its latent heat from the surroundings; an ice–salt mixture can cool to about −21 °C, cold enough to freeze kulfi. On roads, salty water stays liquid below 0 °C, so ice does not form.

**Q4.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Matter & Its Nature</span>

Distinguish between a true solution, a colloid and a suspension, with one example of each. (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

- **True solution:** particles smaller than about 1 nm; homogeneous, transparent, pass through filter paper, never settle, no Tyndall effect — salt or sugar in water.
- **Colloid:** particles about 1–1,000 nm; look homogeneous but are heterogeneous, pass through filter paper, do not settle, show the Tyndall effect — milk, ink, fog.
- **Suspension:** particles larger than about 1,000 nm; opaque, settle on standing, can be filtered off — muddy water, chalk in water.

**Q6.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Chemical Bonding</span>

Why does common salt dissolve readily in water but not in kerosene? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

Salt is an ionic solid (Na⁺ and Cl⁻ held in a lattice). Water is a **polar** solvent with a very high dielectric constant (about 80): the partly negative oxygen ends attract Na⁺ and the partly positive hydrogen ends attract Cl⁻. The energy released by this hydration overcomes the lattice energy and pulls the ions apart. Kerosene is **non-polar**, cannot attract ions, so salt stays undissolved — "like dissolves like". For the same reason grease dissolves in kerosene, not in water.

**Q8.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Chemical Bonding</span>

Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon, yet diamond is used in cutting tools and graphite as a lubricant and in electrodes. Explain on the basis of their structure. (answer in about 70 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

In **diamond**, each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four others in a rigid three-dimensional tetrahedral network. This makes it the hardest natural substance — ideal for cutting tools — and, with no free electrons, an electrical insulator. In **graphite**, each carbon is bonded to only three others in flat hexagonal layers. The layers are held by weak forces and slide over one another, so graphite is soft and slippery (lubricant, pencil "lead"). The fourth electron of each carbon is free to move, so graphite conducts electricity and is used for electrodes.

**Q10.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Metals & Metallurgy</span>

Why are gold and platinum called noble metals? How does aqua regia manage to dissolve gold? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

Gold and platinum are at the bottom of the reactivity series. They do not react with air, water or ordinary acids, so they do not tarnish or corrode and are found free (native) in nature. That makes them ideal for jewellery, coins, electronics contacts and catalysts. **Aqua regia** is concentrated HCl and HNO₃ in a 3:1 ratio. Nitric acid oxidises the gold, and chloride ions immediately lock the gold ions into a stable complex, so the gold keeps dissolving.

**Q14.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Chemistry in Technology</span>

How does a three-way catalytic converter reduce pollution from petrol vehicles? Why can such a vehicle not use leaded petrol? (answer in about 70 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

A catalytic converter is a honeycomb in the exhaust pipe coated with **platinum, palladium and rhodium**. It does three jobs at once: oxidises carbon monoxide to CO₂, oxidises unburnt hydrocarbons to CO₂ and water, and reduces nitrogen oxides to harmless nitrogen. Lead from leaded petrol coats the metal surface and **"poisons" the catalyst**, making it useless — one reason leaded petrol was phased out in India by 2000. BS-VI fuels also have very low sulphur, which protects the catalyst.

</details>

**Q23.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Metals & Metallurgy</span>

Distinguish between roasting and calcination in metallurgy, with one example of each. (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

Both steps heat an ore to turn it into an oxide, which is easier to reduce. **Roasting** heats a **sulphide** ore strongly in a good supply of **air**, for example 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂. **Calcination** heats a **carbonate** (or hydrated) ore in a limited supply of air or none at all, so that CO₂ or water is driven off, for example ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂. **Q25.** <span class="qa-tag">Short answer · Non-metals & Their Compounds</span>

Why is chlorine added to drinking water supplies? What are disinfection by-products, and why is the dose of chlorine controlled? (answer in about 60 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer:**

In water, chlorine forms hypochlorous acid (HOCl). HOCl is a strong oxidising agent that kills the bacteria and viruses behind cholera, typhoid and dysentery. A small **residual chlorine** is left in the pipes so that water stays safe until it reaches the tap. Too much chlorine can react with organic matter in the water and form **disinfection by-products** such as trihalomethanes (for example chloroform), which may cause cancer over long use. So the dose is kept just above the "chlorine demand" of the water.

</details>

**Q36.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Matter & Its Nature</span>

What are colloids? Explain their main properties and examine their importance in nature, industry and daily life. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** A colloid is a heterogeneous mixture in which particles of size about 1–1,000 nm (the dispersed phase) are spread through a dispersion medium. They are bigger than molecules in a true solution but too small to settle like a suspension.

**Types (with examples):** aerosol — fog, smoke; emulsion — milk, butter; gel — jelly, curd; foam — shaving cream; sol — paint, ink.

**Properties:**

- **Tyndall effect:** colloidal particles scatter light, so a beam becomes visible — sunbeams in a forest, headlights in fog.

- **Water purification:** alum (Al³⁺) coagulates negatively charged clay particles in muddy water.
- **Delta formation:** salts in seawater coagulate river silt at the mouth — as in the Ganga–Brahmaputra delta.
- **Pollution control:** Cottrell electrostatic precipitators in thermal plants (such as NTPC Kahalgaon and Barh in Bihar) remove charged fly-ash and smoke particles.
- **Medicine:** alum or ferric chloride stops bleeding by coagulating blood; many medicines and injections are colloidal for easy absorption.
- **Food and cosmetics:** milk, ice cream, mayonnaise, creams and lotions.
- **Weather:** clouds are aerosols; cloud seeding with silver iodide is tried for artificial rain.

**Conclusion:** Colloid science links everyday experiences to large industries, from dairy and paints to nanomedicine and clean air.

**Q42.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Non-metals & Their Compounds</span>

The Haber–Bosch process has been called the invention that feeds half the world. Explain the process and examine its importance and its environmental costs. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** Plants need nitrogen, but they cannot use the N₂ that makes up 78% of the air. In 1909 Fritz Haber found a way to combine atmospheric nitrogen with hydrogen to make ammonia, and Carl Bosch scaled it up for industry. Both later won Nobel Prizes, Haber in 1918 and Bosch in 1931.

**The process**
- N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. The reaction is reversible and exothermic.
- By Le Chatelier's principle, **high pressure** (about 200 atm) favours ammonia, because the volume of gas falls. **Low temperature** would also favour it, but makes the reaction too slow. So a compromise of about **400–450 °C** is used, with an **iron catalyst** (promoted by K₂O and Al₂O₃).

**Importance**
- Ammonia is the base for urea, ammonium sulphate and ammonium phosphates. It is also oxidised to nitric acid (Ostwald process) for fertilisers and explosives.
- Synthetic nitrogen fertilisers are estimated to support food for nearly half of humanity. They made the Green Revolution possible.
- India has revived urea plants at Gorakhpur, Sindri and Barauni (Bihar) to cut imports.

**Environmental costs**
- The process uses about 1–2% of the world's energy, mostly natural gas to make the hydrogen, and is a major CO₂ source.
- Too much fertiliser causes nitrate in groundwater, which is linked to "blue baby syndrome". Run-off causes eutrophication of lakes.
- Nitrous oxide from fertilised fields is a powerful greenhouse gas and also depletes ozone.

**Conclusion:** Green ammonia (made with green hydrogen), balanced fertiliser use, nano-fertilisers and soil testing can keep the benefits of Haber–Bosch while reducing its costs.

**Q46.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Acids, Bases & Salts</span>

"pH is a small number with a large role." Explain the importance of pH in the human body, agriculture and the environment. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** pH = −log[H⁺] measures how acidic or basic a solution is. At 25 °C the scale runs from 0 to 14, with 7 neutral. Each unit is a tenfold change in H⁺ concentration, so small shifts in pH matter a lot.

**Human body**
- **Blood** is kept between pH 7.35 and 7.45 by buffers such as the bicarbonate system. Going outside this range (acidosis or alkalosis) can be fatal.

**Environment and daily life**
- Rain with pH below 5.6 is called acid rain. Lakes below about pH 5 lose most of their fish.
- BIS drinking-water standard IS 10500 sets pH 6.5–8.5.
- Pickles and other preserved foods last because a low pH stops microbes from growing.

**Conclusion:** From digestion to crop yield to safe water, keeping pH in the right range is basic to health, food security and the environment.

**Q58.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Periodic Table & Elements</span>

The periodic table is often called the "map of chemistry". Trace its evolution from Döbereiner to the modern long form, and explain how periodic trends help predict the properties and uses of elements. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** The periodic table arranges all 118 known elements so that elements with similar properties fall in the same column. The UN marked its 150th anniversary by declaring 2019 the International Year of the Periodic Table.

**Evolution:**

- **Döbereiner's triads (1829):** groups of three similar elements (Li, Na, K; Cl, Br, I) where the middle atomic mass is about the average of the other two.
- **Newlands' law of octaves (1865):** every eighth element resembled the first, but it worked only up to calcium.
- **Mendeleev (1869):** periodic law based on atomic mass; he corrected wrong masses, left gaps and correctly predicted gallium, scandium and germanium. Weaknesses: position of hydrogen, isotopes, and pairs such as argon–potassium.
- **Moseley (1913):** X-ray spectra showed that atomic number is the basis; the modern periodic law followed.
- **Long form:** 18 groups and 7 periods, divided into s, p, d and f blocks according to the orbital being filled; the seventh period was completed when elements 113, 115, 117 and 118 were named in 2016.

**Trends and their use:**

- Atomic size, ionisation energy, electronegativity and metallic character change regularly, so the properties of an element can be predicted from its position.
- Elements of one group behave alike: sodium is being tried in place of lithium in batteries; elements next to silicon (germanium, gallium, arsenic) give semiconductors.
- Trends guide the search for superheavy elements and new materials.

**Conclusion:** From 63 elements in 1869, the table has grown into a tool for predicting chemistry and designing materials.

**Q121.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Chemical Bonding</span>

"Hydrogen bonding is a weak force, yet life on Earth depends on it." Discuss. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** A hydrogen bond is an attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to a highly electronegative atom (F, O or N) and a lone pair on another such atom. At roughly 5–40 kJ/mol, it is about one-tenth as strong as a covalent bond, but there are vast numbers of them.

**Water's unusual properties:**

- **Liquid at room temperature:** H₂S, a heavier molecule, is a gas, while H₂O boils at 100 °C because its molecules are linked by hydrogen bonds. Without them, Earth would have no liquid oceans.
- **High heat of vaporisation:** evaporating sweat removes much heat, cooling the body; large water bodies moderate climate.
- **Universal solvent:** water forms hydrogen bonds with sugars, amino acids and ions, carrying nutrients in blood and sap.
- **Cohesion and adhesion:** together they help water rise as a continuous column in the xylem of tall trees.

**Molecules of life:**

- **DNA:** the two strands pair by hydrogen bonds (A–T two, G–C three); being weak, they can unzip for replication and transcription and then re-form.
- **Proteins:** hydrogen bonds hold the α-helix and β-sheet shapes; enzymes bind their substrates through them. Heat breaks these bonds, which is why fever or cooking denatures proteins.
- **Cellulose:** hydrogen bonds between chains make cotton fibres and wood strong.

**Other examples:** HF boils far higher than HCl; nylon fibres get their strength from hydrogen bonds.

**Conclusion:** A force individually weak but collectively powerful, hydrogen bonding gives water its life-supporting properties and lets the molecules of heredity and metabolism be both stable and flexible.

**Q137.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Chemical Reactions</span>

Corrosion is often called a "silent thief" of national wealth. Explain the chemistry of corrosion and examine the methods used to prevent it. (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** Corrosion is the slow eating away of a metal by reaction with its surroundings. Examples are rusting of iron, green patina on copper and black tarnish on silver. A NACE International study (2016) put the global cost at about US$2.5 trillion a year, roughly 3.4% of world GDP. It also said 15–35% of this could be saved with methods already known.

**Chemistry:** Rusting is **electrochemical**. At anodic spots iron is oxidised: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻. At cathodic spots oxygen is reduced: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. The products combine and oxidise further to hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust), which flakes off and exposes fresh metal. Moisture, dissolved salts (coastal air, sea water), acidic gases such as SO₂, and impurities in the metal all speed it up.

**Prevention**
- **Barrier coatings:** paint, oil, grease, enamel or plastic keep air and water away.
- **Galvanisation:** a zinc coating protects iron even when scratched, because zinc corrodes first. **Tinning** is used for food cans, and chromium electroplating for taps.
- **Alloying:** stainless steel contains at least 10.5% chromium, which forms a thin self-healing Cr₂O₃ film.
- **Cathodic protection:** sacrificial magnesium or zinc blocks are fixed to ship hulls, buried pipelines and jetties, or a small impressed current is applied.
- **Inhibitors and design:** chemicals in boilers and cooling water, good drainage, and avoiding contact between two different metals.

**Indian example:** The 1,600-year-old Iron Pillar at Delhi has not rusted. Its phosphorus-rich iron forms a thin protective film, a lesson for modern corrosion-resistant steel.

**Conclusion:** With India investing heavily in bridges, railways, pipelines and ports, building corrosion protection in from the design stage saves money and prevents disasters.

Why is the groundwater of many districts along the Ganga in Bihar contaminated with arsenic? State its health effects and two ways to provide safe water. (answer in about 70 words)

**Answer:**

The arsenic is **geogenic** (natural). **Q143.** <span class="qa-tag">Explanatory · Metals & Metallurgy</span>

Explain the chemistry of making steel from iron ore. Why is the steel sector hard to decarbonise, and what steps has India taken towards "green steel"? (about 250 words)

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Model answer (outline)**

**Introduction:** India is the world's second-largest producer of crude steel. The National Steel Policy 2017 aims for 300 million tonnes of capacity by 2030–31. The first steel plants were in the old Bihar region: Tata Steel at Jamshedpur (1907) and Bokaro.

**Chemistry of steel-making**
- **Blast furnace:** iron ore (haematite, Fe₂O₃) is reduced by carbon monoxide from coke: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. Limestone removes silica as slag. The product is **pig iron**, with about 4% carbon.
- **Basic oxygen furnace:** oxygen is blown through molten pig iron to burn off extra carbon and impurities, giving **steel** (usually well below 2% carbon). Alloying elements such as chromium, nickel or manganese are added as needed.
- **Other routes:** India is the world's largest producer of **sponge iron (DRI)**, made by reducing ore in solid form, mostly with coal. **Electric arc furnaces** melt scrap or DRI.

**Why it is hard to decarbonise:** Carbon is not just the fuel; it is the **chemical reducing agent**, so CO₂ is a built-in product. Steel causes about 7–9% of global CO₂ emissions, and India's coal-heavy route emits more per tonne than the world average.

**India's steps**
- The **Green Steel Taxonomy** (Ministry of Steel, December 2024) calls steel "green" if it emits less than 2.2 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of finished steel, with star ratings for lower levels.
- The Steel Scrap Recycling Policy (2019) promotes scrap-based furnaces.
- Pilots of hydrogen-based DRI, in which hydrogen reduces ore and gives water instead of CO₂, run under the National Green Hydrogen Mission. Renewable power, energy efficiency and carbon capture are being promoted alongside.

**Conclusion:** India's steel demand will keep growing with infrastructure. A gradual shift to scrap, hydrogen and clean power can make that growth compatible with net zero by 2070.

</details>

**Q147.** <span class="qa-tag">Numerical · Acids, Bases & Salts</span>

The pH of gastric juice in the stomach is about 2, while that of black coffee is about 5. How many times greater is the hydrogen ion concentration of gastric juice than that of black coffee?

(a) 3 times &nbsp; (b) 30 times &nbsp; (c) 300 times &nbsp; (d) 1,000 times

</div>
<details class="qa-a">
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer: (d) 1,000 times**

pH = −log[H⁺], so [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ mol/L.

- Gastric juice: [H⁺] = 10⁻² mol/L
- Black coffee: [H⁺] = 10⁻⁵ mol/L
- Ratio = 10⁻² ÷ 10⁻⁵ = 10³ = **1,000**

Each drop of one pH unit means ten times more H⁺. A difference of 3 units is therefore 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 times, not 3 times.
