This chapter covers point 3(i) of Section A of the syllabus, "Water relations: transpiration, translocation" — NCERT Class 11 "Transport in Plants" (old edition) and B.Sc. physiology.
In UP PGT, expect **numerical questions on water potential**, **theory–proposer matching** (Dixon-Joly, Münch, Bose, Levitt), **sequence of events in stomatal opening** and "which statement is correct" questions from here.

> **In simple words:** Water always flows **from higher to lower water potential** — soil (highest) → root → stem → leaf → air (lowest). Water evaporates from the leaves as vapour (transpiration), which pulls the "column" of water in the xylem upward — like drinking through a straw. Food (sugar) flows in the phloem from the "source" (leaf) to the "sink" (root, fruit) because of pressure.

## 1. Basic phenomena

| Phenomenon | Definition | Example/note |
|---|---|---|
| **Diffusion** | Passive movement of molecules from higher to lower concentration | No energy; **Graham's law** — rate ∝ 1/√density |
| **Facilitated diffusion** | Through carrier/channel proteins, along the concentration gradient | **Aquaporins** — water channels |
| **Active transport** | Against the gradient, uses ATP | Pumps; symport, antiport, uniport |
| **Imbibition** | Swelling of **colloidal solids** by absorbing water | Dry seeds, wooden doors swell in the rains; **imbibition pressure** is huge — in ancient times wooden pegs were used to split rocks. Water-absorbing capacity: **agar > pectin > protein > starch > cellulose** |
| **Osmosis** | Movement of **solvent (water)** across a **semipermeable membrane** from lower solute concentration (higher Ψw) to higher solute concentration (lower Ψw) | **Thistle funnel experiment**, potato osmoscope; **Pfeffer** — measured osmotic pressure |
| **Reverse osmosis** | Applying pressure greater than the osmotic pressure to move solvent in the opposite direction | Water purification, desalination |

> **Common mistake:** In osmosis it is **water** that moves, not the solute; and water moves **from the solution with lower solute concentration (higher water potential)** to **the one with higher concentration (lower water potential)**.

## 2. Water potential (Ψw)

Term by **Slatyer & Taylor (1960)**. Water potential = the **chemical potential (free energy)** of water compared with pure water. **Ψw of pure water = 0** (at standard temperature and pressure) — the maximum; Ψw of a solution is **negative**. Unit — **pascal (MPa)**; 1 MPa = 10 bar ≈ 9.87 atm.

<div class="formula">

**Ψw = Ψs + Ψp** (+ Ψg gravity, + Ψm matric — usually ignored in a cell)

- **Ψs (solute/osmotic potential)** — always **negative**; Ψs = −iCRT (van 't Hoff) — i = ionisation constant, C = molar concentration, R = 0.0083 L·MPa/mol·K, T = kelvin.
- **Ψp (pressure potential)** — **positive** in a turgid cell; **zero** in a plasmolysed cell; **negative in the xylem because of tension**.
- **Matric potential (Ψm)** — dominant in imbibition (dry seeds, soil).

**Example:** 0.1 M sucrose, 27°C (300 K): Ψs = −1 × 0.1 × 0.0083 × 300 ≈ **−0.25 MPa**.
**Example:** Cell A: Ψs = −0.8, Ψp = +0.3 → Ψw = −0.5 MPa; cell B: Ψs = −0.6, Ψp = +0.3 → Ψw = −0.3 MPa → **water moves from B to A** (−0.3 to −0.5).

</div>

**Older terminology (in B.Sc. books):** **Diffusion pressure deficit (DPD)** or **suction pressure** = **osmotic pressure (OP) − turgor pressure (TP)**; **DPD = −Ψw**. In a fully turgid cell TP = OP, DPD = 0; in a fully flaccid cell TP = 0, DPD = OP.

### Tonicity and plasmolysis

| External solution | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Hypotonic** — Ψw higher outside | Water enters → **turgidity**; the wall prevents bursting |
| **Isotonic** | No net movement → **flaccid** |
| **Hypertonic** — Ψw lower outside | Water leaves → **plasmolysis**: the protoplast shrinks away from the wall; the space between wall and membrane fills with the **external solution** (the wall is fully permeable) |

- **Incipient plasmolysis** — the protoplast starts to pull away from the corners; at this point **Ψp = 0, Ψw = Ψs**; the osmotic pressure of cell sap is measured this way.
- **Deplasmolysis** — on placing the cell back in a hypotonic solution.
- Uses: **pickles, jams, preservation with salt-sugar** — plasmolysis of microbes; plants wilt when too much fertiliser is added in the field (**"fertiliser burn"**).

## 3. Water absorption by roots

**Pathways:**
1. **Apoplast** — cell walls and intercellular spaces, non-living, fast, unregulated; **stopped at the Casparian strip (suberin) of the endodermis**.
2. **Symplast** — from protoplast to protoplast, through **plasmodesmata**.
3. **Transmembrane** — including vacuoles.

> **Remember:** At the endodermis water **has to enter the symplast** — this is where the plant controls **the selection of minerals** (which ions, how much). **Passage cells** help here.

**Active vs passive absorption (Renner):**

| | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Force | In the root itself — **root pressure**, active accumulation of ions | In the leaf — **transpiration pull** |
| Contribution | Small (~4%) | **Most (~96%)** |
| When | Night, high humidity, low transpiration | Day |

**Root pressure:** Positive pressure in the xylem due to active accumulation of ions — **Stephen Hales** measured it on the cut stem of a grapevine; normally only **a few bars (~1–2 bar)** — not enough to lift water in tall trees; **almost absent in gymnosperms**. Evidence — **bleeding/exudation** from a cut stem; **guttation** — at night/early morning, **drops of liquid water** at leaf margins through **hydathodes** — **grasses, tomato, colocasia (*Colocasia*), water hyacinth**. The pores of hydathodes are **always open** (guard cells do not control them); guttation water contains **minerals and organic substances** (not pure water).

**Mycorrhiza** — fungal hyphae increase absorption of water and minerals (especially **phosphorus**); seeds of ***Pinus*** do not establish properly without mycorrhiza.

## 4. Ascent of sap

The upward movement of water in the xylem from root to leaf — up to trees over 100 metres tall (*Sequoia*, *Eucalyptus regnans*).

| Class | Theory | Proposer | Objection/status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vital** | **Relay pump theory** | **Godlewski, 1884** | Living parenchyma and rays pump — refuted by **Strasburger (1891)**, who showed ascent of sap even in stems killed by poison/heat |
| | **Pulsation theory** | **Jagadish Chandra Bose, 1923** | Pulsation of cells of the inner cortical layer — using an **electric probe**; not accepted |
| **Root pressure** | Root pressure theory | **Priestley** | Pressure too low; absent in gymnosperms; root pressure is low in summer (when ascent of sap is greatest) |
| **Physical** | Capillarity | **Boehm, 1809** | Only up to ~1 metre; vessels are not open capillaries |
| | Imbibition theory | **Sachs, 1878** | Water rises through the lumen, not the wall |
| | **Cohesion–tension / transpiration pull** | **Dixon & Joly, 1894** | **Universally accepted** |

### Cohesion–tension theory

1. **Cohesion** — **hydrogen bonds** between water molecules → high **tensile strength** (up to hundreds of atm; experimentally estimated up to ~30 MPa).
2. **Adhesion** — between water and the xylem wall (lignocellulose).
3. **Continuous water column** — unbroken from leaf to root.
4. Transpiration from the leaf mesophyll cells → their Ψw falls → water is pulled from neighbouring cells and finally from the xylem → **tension (negative pressure) in the xylem** → the whole column is pulled up.
- **Evidence:** **stem diameter decreases** at midday (dendrometer); air is sucked into a cut stem.
- **Problem:** **Cavitation** — air bubbles (**embolism**) break the column; overcome by the **pits** of tracheids, many parallel vessels, and repair by root pressure at night.

> **Memory trick:** "**D**ixon-**J**oly = **D**rink **J**uice through a straw" — cohesion-tension pull; "**Go**dlewski — **Go**-between relay", "**Bo**se — **Bea**ting (pulsation)".

## 5. Transpiration

Loss of water **as water vapour** from the aerial parts of the plant. A plant loses **~99% of the absorbed water in transpiration**; only ~1% is used in metabolism/growth. **Curtis** — "**a necessary evil**".

| Type | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Stomatal** | **~90–95%** |
| Cuticular | ~5–10% (more in shade plants) |
| Lenticular | ~0.1% (important in deciduous trees in winter) |

**Significance:** driving force for the ascent of sap, mineral transport, **cooling of the leaf (evaporative cooling)**, maintaining cell shape/turgidity.
**Measurement:** **Ganong's potometer** — actually measures **the rate of water absorption** (≈ transpiration); **cobalt chloride paper** — **blue (dry) → pink (moist)**; **weighing (balance) method** — loss of weight; **bell jar experiment**.

**Factors:** **temperature ↑ → transpiration ↑**; **humidity ↑ → ↓**; **wind speed** — a gentle breeze ↑ (removes the layer of still air), strong wind → stomata close → ↓; **light ↑ → stomata open → ↑**; **soil water ↓ → ↓**; CO₂ ↑ → stomata close → ↓. Internal — number/position of stomata, cuticle, **sunken stomata**, hairs, leaf rolling (bulliform), **root/shoot ratio**.

**Antitranspirants:** **phenyl mercuric acetate (PMA)** — partial closure of stomata; **ABA**; **CO₂**; silicone oils, low-viscosity waxes (form a film); **kaolin** (reflective); **aspirin** (in some experiments).

## 6. Stomata — structure and mechanism

**Structure:** Two **guard cells** — **kidney-shaped** in dicots, **dumbbell-shaped** in grasses; **inner wall (towards the pore) thick and inelastic, outer wall thin and elastic**; cellulose **microfibrils arranged radially (radial micellation)** — on becoming turgid the outer wall is pulled outward and the pore opens. Guard cells have **chloroplasts** (other epidermal cells do not); surrounded by **subsidiary cells**.

### Types of stomata (Metcalfe & Chalk, based on subsidiary cells)

| Type | Subsidiary cells | Example families |
|---|---|---|
| **Anomocytic (ranunculaceous)** | Irregular, like epidermal cells | Ranunculaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Malvaceae |
| **Anisocytic (cruciferous)** | 3, one smaller | **Brassicaceae**, Solanaceae |
| **Paracytic (rubiaceous)** | 2, parallel to the guard cells | **Rubiaceae**, Magnoliaceae |
| **Diacytic (caryophyllaceous)** | 2, at right angles to the guard cells | **Caryophyllaceae**, Acanthaceae (*Dianthus*) |
| **Graminaceous** | Dumbbell guard cells + 2 parallel subsidiary cells | **Poaceae** |

**Distribution of stomata (Loftfield):** apple type (lower surface only), potato type (more on the lower), oat type (equal on both), water lily type (upper only), *Potamogeton* type (no stomata/vestigial).

### Theories of opening and closing

| Theory | Proposer | Gist |
|---|---|---|
| **Photosynthesis theory** | **von Mohl, 1856** | Chloroplasts of the guard cells make sugar and lower Ψs — insufficient (chloroplasts not very active) |
| **Starch–sugar interconversion** | **Lloyd (1908)**, **Sayre (1923)**; modified by **Steward (1964)** | By day photosynthesis lowers CO₂ → **pH rises (~7)** → **phosphorylase** converts starch → glucose-1-phosphate → Ψ falls → stomata open; the reverse at night (pH ~5) — **objection:** guard cells of monocots (onion) have no starch; the change is too slow |
| **Active K⁺ transport theory** | **Imamura (1943)**, **Fujino (1959/1967)**; extended by **Levitt (1974)**, **Raschke** | **Accepted modern theory** |

### K⁺ (potassium ion) theory — sequence of events

<div class="formula">

**Opening (day, blue light):**
1. **Blue light** → **phototropin/zeaxanthin** receptors activated.
2. The plasma membrane **H⁺-ATPase (proton pump)** is activated → **H⁺ pumped out** → **hyperpolarisation** of the membrane, internal pH rises.
3. **K⁺ enters** through voltage-gated channels (from the subsidiary cells); to balance the charge **Cl⁻ enters** and **malate²⁻ is formed** (starch → PEP → **PEP carboxylase** → oxaloacetate → malate).
4. K⁺ + malate/Cl⁻ into the vacuole → **Ψs and Ψw fall** → water enters (osmosis) → guard cells become turgid → **stoma opens**.

**Closing (darkness / water stress):** Under water stress **ABA** is made in the leaf → **Ca²⁺ rises** in the guard cell → **anion channels open (Cl⁻, malate out)** → depolarisation → **K⁺ leaves (outward K⁺ channels)** → water leaves → **stoma closes**. H⁺-ATPase is inhibited.

</div>

> **Exam angle:** **In CAM plants stomata open at night** (scotoactive) — water conservation. **Blue light is the most effective for stomatal opening**; a **high internal CO₂ concentration** closes stomata; **ABA = "stress hormone"** — closes stomata.

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** In bright sunlight and moderate humidity, the guard cells of a plant actively accumulate K⁺, Cl⁻ and malate. Most likely sequence of events? (A) Solute concentration falls → stomata open → water loss increases (B) Water potential rises → water leaves → stomata close → CO₂ entry falls (C) **Water potential falls → water enters the guard cells → stomata open → CO₂ diffusion increases** (D) Turgor rises → transpiration falls → cooling falls
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(C)** — more solute lowers Ψs and Ψw, water enters, and turgor opens the stoma.


**Transpiration–photosynthesis compromise:** If stomata open for photosynthesis, water is lost. **Water use efficiency (WUE)** — **C₄ plants fix CO₂ with about twice the efficiency of C₃** (for the same water loss); **CAM the highest**. **Transpiration ratio** (water lost ÷ dry matter formed): C₃ ~ 500–1000, C₄ ~ 250–350, CAM ~ 50–100 (approximate ranges).

## 7. Absorption and transport of minerals (in brief)

- Ions are absorbed **actively** (ATP, carriers) — by root hair cells; some passively (free space/Donnan equilibrium).
- Minerals move up **in the xylem**; **remobilisation** through the phloem — **N, P, K, Mg are mobile** (deficiency symptoms in older leaves), **Ca, S, Fe, B less mobile** (in young leaves) — Chapter 7.

## 8. Translocation in phloem

**Source** — where food is made/stored (mature leaf; storage organs in spring); **sink** — where it is used/stored (root, fruit, seed, young leaves, meristems). Source and sink can change with season → **transport in phloem is bidirectional** (up and down); **in xylem it is unidirectional** (upward).

**Phloem sap:** mainly **sucrose** (non-reducing, hence stable during transport), amino acids, hormones, some minerals (K⁺), RNA, some viruses.

### Pressure-flow/mass-flow hypothesis — **Ernst Münch, 1930**

1. **Phloem loading at the source:** sucrose moves **actively** (**H⁺–sucrose symport**, using the proton gradient made by H⁺-ATPase) from the companion cell into the sieve tube.
2. **Ψs and Ψw of the sieve tube fall** → water enters **by osmosis from the neighbouring xylem** → **turgor pressure (Ψp) rises**.
3. **Unloading at the sink:** sucrose leaves (actively/passively) → Ψw rises there → water leaves (back into the xylem).
4. The **pressure gradient** between source (high pressure) and sink (low pressure) makes the whole solution flow by **mass flow**.

> **Common mistake:** Loading and unloading are **active (ATP)**, but **the flow of solution itself in the sieve tube is passive** (pressure gradient). Sieve tube cells must stay **alive** — a semipermeable membrane is needed to maintain pressure.

**Evidence:**
- **Girdling/ringing experiment** — **Malpighi**: when a ring of bark (including phloem) is removed from the stem, **the part above the ring swells** (food accumulates), and the roots below eventually starve; since the xylem is intact the leaves stay green for some time.
- **Aphid stylet technique** — **Kennedy & Mittler (1953)**: when the aphid's mouthpart is cut, sap keeps flowing out of the sieve tube **under pressure** — evidence of sap collection and pressure.
- **Radioactive ¹⁴CO₂** tracer.
- **Objection:** **bidirectional movement** in the same sieve tube is not explained by pressure flow; **P-protein** resistance at sieve plates.

**Other theories:** **protoplasmic streaming/cyclosis** — **Hugo de Vries (1885)**, Curtis; **electro-osmotic** — **Spanner (1958)**; **transcellular strand** — **Thaine (1962)**.

> **Exam angle:** The 2026 paper asked about the **"limiting factor"** (Liebig's law) through a crop scenario (see Chapters 7 and 9). A possible question from here in the same style: "At midday in bright sun and dry wind a plant wilts even though the soil is moist — best explanation?" — **the rate of transpiration exceeds absorption; tension in the xylem rises; ABA closes the stomata**.

## 2026-style practice questions (statements, sequence, matching, Assertion–Reason)

The questions below are written in the style of the 2026 paper (they are not actual previous questions). Solve them yourself first, then check the answer and reason.

**Q.1 (statement combination).** (I) The water potential of pure water is maximum (zero). (II) Pressure potential in the xylem can be negative. (III) At incipient plasmolysis Ψw = Ψp. (IV) Solute potential is always negative. Correct — (A) I, II, IV (B) I, III (C) II, III, IV (D) All
**Answer: (A)** — III is wrong: at incipient plasmolysis **Ψp = 0, so Ψw = Ψs**.

**Q.2 (matching).** (a) Relay pump (b) Pulsation (c) Cohesion-tension (d) Pressure flow — (I) Dixon and Joly (II) Münch (III) Godlewski (IV) J.C. Bose
**Answer:** a-III, b-IV, c-I, d-II.

**Q.3 (sequence).** Order of stomatal closure under water stress — (I) ABA synthesis (II) Rise of Ca²⁺ in the guard cell (III) Efflux of anions and K⁺ (IV) Loss of turgor
**Answer:** I → II → III → IV.

**Q.4 (Assertion–Reason).** A: Guttation is greater at night. R: At night transpiration is low while root pressure continues.
**Answer:** **Both true, R is the correct explanation.**

**Q.5 (situation).** A complete ring of bark was removed from a stem; a few weeks later the bark above the ring swelled and eventually the plant died. Explanation? — **With the phloem cut, sugar could not reach the roots (it accumulated above); the roots starved; the leaves stayed green for a while because the xylem was intact.**

## Traps and exceptions

- **A potometer measures water absorption, not transpiration.**
- **Guttation is through hydathodes (liquid) — transpiration through stomata (vapour)**; hydathodes are not regulated.
- **In plasmolysis the space between wall and membrane holds the external solution**, not air or water.
- **Ψp can be negative in the xylem**; **Ψs is never positive**.
- **Ψw of pure water = 0 is the maximum**; an option with "water potential greater than zero" is wrong.
- **Sucrose (non-reducing)** moves in the phloem; glucose is not the main translocated sugar.
- **J.C. Bose — pulsation theory** (ascent of sap) — and the **crescograph** (measuring growth).
- **Stephen Hales — "father of plant physiology"**, measured root pressure and transpiration (Vegetable Staticks, 1727).
- **In the starch–sugar theory pH rises by day** (less CO₂); in the K⁺ theory the **pumping out of H⁺** also raises the internal pH of the guard cell.
- During stomatal opening **K⁺ moves from the subsidiary cells into the guard cells** (not the reverse).

## Quick revision

- Ψw = Ψs + Ψp; pure water Ψw = 0; Ψs = −iCRT.
- Water moves from higher Ψw to lower Ψw.
- At incipient plasmolysis Ψp = 0.
- DPD = OP − TP = −Ψw.
- Imbibition capacity: agar > pectin > protein > starch > cellulose.
- Apoplast stops at the Casparian strip.
- Guttation — hydathodes; grasses, tomato, colocasia.
- Relay pump — Godlewski; pulsation — J.C. Bose; capillarity — Boehm; imbibition — Sachs.
- Cohesion-tension — Dixon and Joly (1894) — universally accepted.
- Cavitation/embolism — breaking of the water column.
- ~99% of absorbed water is transpired; "necessary evil" — Curtis.
- Cobalt chloride — blue to pink.
- K⁺ theory — Fujino/Levitt; blue light → H⁺-ATPase → K⁺ in → malate.
- Starch-sugar — Sayre 1923, Steward 1964.
- ABA → Ca²⁺ → K⁺ out → stomata close.
- Anisocytic — Cruciferae; diacytic — Caryophyllaceae; paracytic — Rubiaceae.
- PMA — antitranspirant.
- Pressure flow — Münch 1930; aphid stylet; girdling — Malpighi.
- Phloem bidirectional, xylem unidirectional; sucrose is the main sugar.
