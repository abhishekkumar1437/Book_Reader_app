This chapter is the first part of point 7 of Section A of the syllabus — "concept; habitat & niche; ecosystem structure/function, stability, carrying capacity, food chain/web, energy flow, pyramids, biomes; population & community; biogeochemical cycles; succession" — together with soil, which was asked twice in the 2026 paper. The level runs from NCERT 12 "Organisms and Populations" and "Ecosystem" to B.Sc. ecology (niche theory, IVI, Raunkiaer, r-K selection).
**In the 2026 paper ecology was the largest single botany section (10 questions)** — all in the "situation-based explanation" style: limiting factors, temperature and metabolism, resource partitioning, distribution patterns, energy flow, soil profile and erosion.

> **In simple words:** Ecology = the study of the relationships between organisms and their surroundings. The **habitat** is an organism's "address", the **niche** its "profession". All the organisms of an area (biotic) and their surroundings (abiotic) together form an **ecosystem**, in which energy flows **in one direction** from the sun to plants, then to herbivores and carnivores, while nutrients keep moving in **cycles**. Over time a bare rock or a pond slowly turns into a forest — this is **succession**.

**Levels of organisation:** organism → **population** → **community** → **ecosystem** → **landscape** → **biome** → **biosphere**.

### Laws of limiting factors

| Law | Gist |
|---|---|
| **Liebig's law of the minimum (1840)** | Growth is limited by the **essential nutrient/factor** that is available **in the smallest amount relative to need** |
| **Blackman's law of limiting factors (1905)** | The **rate** of a process affected by many factors is set by the slowest/scarcest factor (Chapter 5) |
| **Shelford's law of tolerance (1913)** | The distribution of an organism is controlled by **both the minimum and maximum limits** of a factor (range of tolerance); **both deficiency and excess** are limiting; the optimum range lies in between |

- By range of tolerance — **eury-** (wide) and **steno-** (narrow): **eurythermal/stenothermal** (temperature), **euryhaline/stenohaline** (salinity), euryhydric, euryphagic (food).
- **Odum** combined the two laws into the "**combined concept of limiting factors**".

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** (Question 15, given in full in Chapter 5) — growth held back by deficiency of one micronutrient despite adequate macronutrients → **Liebig's law**; option (D) gave Shelford's law **in a wrong form** ("growth is maximum when all factors are highest") — in Shelford's law excess is also limiting.

### Abiotic factors — temperature

- The rate of most biochemical reactions **roughly doubles for every 10°C** (**Q₁₀ ≈ 2**, **van 't Hoff rule**) — up to a limit; above it enzymes denature.
- **Ectotherms** — fish, amphibians, invertebrates — body temperature and metabolism change with the environment.
- **Bergmann's rule** — warm-blooded animals of cold regions are larger; **Allen's rule** — in cold regions ears, tails and limbs are shorter (less heat loss).

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** Main reason for the exponential rise in metabolic rate with rising temperature in aquatic ecosystems? (A) **Acceleration of enzyme-mediated biochemical reactions due to greater molecular motion** (B) Change in osmotic balance due to falling salinity (C) Higher density of water (D) Increase in the solubility of O₂ in water
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(A)** — (D) is the reverse: **the solubility of O₂ falls as temperature rises** (which is why fish face O₂ stress in warm water).

**Other abiotic factors:** light (photoperiodism; **heliophytes** and **sciophytes**), water (**hydrophytes, mesophytes, xerophytes**, halophytes), soil (section 13), topography (altitude, slope).

## 2. Habitat and niche

- **Habitat** — the physical place where an organism lives ("address"). **Microhabitat** — under a leaf, under a stone.
- **Niche** — the **functional role of an organism in the community** and all its requirements (food, space, time, tolerance) — its "profession".

| Concept | Proposed by | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Spatial/habitat niche** | **Joseph Grinnell (1917)** | Microhabitat |
| **Trophic/functional niche** | **Charles Elton (1927)** | Role in the food chain |
| **Multidimensional/n-dimensional hypervolume** | **G.E. Hutchinson (1957)** | **Fundamental niche** — the whole range possible without competitors; **realized niche** — the (smaller) part actually used after competition |

- **Gause's competitive exclusion principle (1934)** — **two species with the same niche cannot coexist indefinitely**; *Paramecium aurelia* and *P. caudatum* — *P. aurelia* won.
- **Resource partitioning — MacArthur (1958):** **five warbler species** on the same tree avoid competition by feeding on different parts/heights.
- **Connell (1961) — barnacles of Scotland:** the larger *Balanus* excludes the smaller *Chthamalus* from the lower intertidal zone → the realized niche of *Chthamalus* is smaller than its fundamental niche.
- **Character displacement** — the difference in beak size of Darwin's finches increases when they live together.
- **Niche overlap** → competition; **territoriality** → regulates **intraspecific** competition.

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** In a structured community two closely related herbivores use overlapping food. Over time one starts feeding on a different part of the same plant, while the members of each species show territorial spacing. Which explanation links the inter- and intraspecific interactions? (A) Niche shift is mutualism; territorial spacing is due to cooperation (B) **Resource partitioning reduces interspecific competition, while territoriality limits intraspecific competition** (C) Both are due to commensalism (D) The food shift is parasitism
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(B)**.

**Ecological equivalents** — unrelated species with similar niches in different geographical regions (kangaroos of Australia and the grazing ungulates of other continents). **Ecotone** — the transition zone between two communities (forest–grassland, mangroves); **edge effect** — the number and density of species are higher in the ecotone.

## 3. Ecosystem — structure and function

**Structure:**
- **Abiotic** — inorganic substances (C, N, CO₂, H₂O), organic compounds (proteins, carbohydrates, humus — link biotic and abiotic), climatic factors.
- **Biotic** — **producers (autotrophs)**, **consumers** (primary — herbivores, secondary, tertiary), **decomposers/microconsumers** (bacteria, fungi — saprotrophs).
- **Stratification** — vertical layers (tree-shrub-herb-ground in a forest).

**Function (four main components — NCERT):** (1) **productivity**, (2) **decomposition**, (3) **energy flow**, (4) **nutrient cycling**.

### Productivity

<div class="formula">

- **Primary production** — the **biomass** produced by producers per unit area over a period of time (g m⁻² or kcal m⁻²); **productivity** = the rate (g m⁻² yr⁻¹).
- **Gross primary productivity (GPP)** — the total rate of production of organic matter in photosynthesis.
- **Net primary productivity (NPP) = GPP − R** (R = respiratory loss of producers) — **available to heterotrophs**.
- **Secondary productivity** — the rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers.
- **Annual NPP of the biosphere ≈ 170 billion tonnes (dry weight)**; **the oceans (70% of the area) contribute only ~55 billion tonnes** (NCERT).
- Light: **~50% of incident solar radiation is PAR**; plants capture only **2–10%** of PAR — and all life runs on this.

</div>

**Order of productivity (per unit area, high → low):** **tropical rain forest, coral reef, estuary, marsh** > temperate forest > cropland > grassland > lake > **open ocean, desert, tundra** (lowest). The open ocean is a "biological desert" because of nutrient shortage.

### Decomposition

**Detritus** — dead leaves, bark, flowers, animal remains, faeces.
**Steps:** **fragmentation** — detritivores (**earthworm**) → **leaching** — water-soluble inorganic nutrients go into the soil → **catabolism** — bacterial-fungal enzymes → **humification** — dark, decay-resistant, colloidal **humus** (nutrient reservoir) → **mineralisation** — slow breakdown of humus releasing inorganic nutrients.
**Rate:** **warm-moist conditions, more N and water-soluble sugars** → fast; **more lignin, chitin, cold, anaerobic** → slow (which is why **peat** builds up in bogs).

## 4. Energy flow, food chains and webs

- **Energy flow is unidirectional** (sun → producers → consumers; lost as heat) — obeys the **first and second laws of thermodynamics**; nutrients are **cyclic**.
- **Lindeman's 10% law** — at each trophic level only **about 10%** of the energy of the previous level passes to the next (the rest goes to respiration, heat, undigested parts). That is why **food chains are usually limited to 4–5 trophic levels**.

| Food chain | Starts with | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Grazing food chain (GFC)** | Living green plants | Dominant in aquatic ecosystems |
| **Detritus food chain (DFC)** | Dead organic matter (detritus) | **In terrestrial ecosystems more energy flows through the DFC** (NCERT) |
| Parasitic food chain | Host → parasite → hyperparasite | Pyramid of numbers inverted |

**Food web** — interconnected food chains; the more complex the web, the greater the **stability** (alternative routes).

**Ecological efficiencies (B.Sc.):** **assimilation efficiency**, **production efficiency**, **Lindeman efficiency** (= assimilation at level n ÷ assimilation at level n−1).

## 6. Ecosystem stability, homeostasis and carrying capacity

- Two components of **stability** — **resistance** — the ability to remain unchanged during a disturbance; **resilience** — the ability to return to the original state after a disturbance.
- **Diversity–stability hypothesis** — **MacArthur (1955)**; long-term experiments of **David Tilman** (Cedar Creek, Minnesota) — **plots with more species showed less year-to-year variation in total biomass** and higher productivity.
- **Rivet popper hypothesis — Paul Ehrlich** — keep removing rivets (species) from an aeroplane; losing a few may not matter, but if more/key (wing) rivets are removed the plane crashes.
- **Ecosystem homeostasis** — self-regulation through **negative feedback** (cybernetic system); **positive feedback** increases the deviation (e.g. in eutrophication).
- **Carrying capacity (K)** — the **maximum population** a habitat can support over a long period; set by resources (food, space, water). The **ecological footprint** — human demand compared with the Earth's **biocapacity**.

## 8. Attributes of a population

<div class="formula">

**Change in population density:** Nₜ₊₁ = Nₜ + [(B + I) − (D + E)] — B = births (**natality**), D = **mortality**, I = **immigration**, E = **emigration**.
**Exponential growth (J-shaped):** dN/dt = rN; Nₜ = N₀eʳᵗ — unlimited resources.
**Logistic growth (S-shaped) — Verhulst-Pearl:** dN/dt = rN (K − N)/K — K = carrying capacity; **more realistic**.
**r = intrinsic rate of natural increase** — Norway rat 0.015, flour beetle 0.12; **human population of India (1981) 0.0205** (NCERT).

</div>

- **Age structure / age pyramid** — **expanding (triangular — more pre-reproductive individuals)**, **stable (bell-shaped)**, **declining (urn-shaped)**.
- **Sex ratio**.
- **Survivorship curves (Pearl):** **Type I** (low early mortality, high in old age) — **humans, large mammals**; **Type II** (constant mortality) — **birds, *Hydra***, lizards; **Type III** (very high early mortality) — **oysters, fish, most plants** (many offspring).
- **Density-dependent factors** (competition, predation, disease) and **density-independent factors** (weather, floods, fire).
- **Allee effect** — growth rate falls even at very low density (difficulty finding mates).
- **r-selected and K-selected (MacArthur and Wilson, 1967):** r — small, mature early, many small offspring, little parental care, unstable environment (insects, weeds); K — large, mature late, few offspring, more care, stable environment (elephant, humans, trees).

### Distribution/dispersion patterns

| Pattern | Cause | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Random** | Uniform environment, no attraction/repulsion | Rare — some invertebrates on the forest floor, plants from wind-dispersed seeds |
| **Uniform** | **Intense intraspecific competition**, territoriality, **allelopathy** | Desert shrubs (*Larrea*), nesting seabirds, orchards |
| **Clumped** — **the most common** | **Patchy distribution of resources**, **limited dispersal**, **social/behavioural aggregation** | Herds, plants around a parent tree, insects on host plants |

## 9. Population interactions

| Interaction | Species A | Species B | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mutualism** | + | + | **Lichens** (fungus + alga), **mycorrhiza**, *Rhizobium*–legumes, **fig–wasp**, *Yucca*–moth, orchid *Ophrys*–bee (pseudocopulation — a kind of "cheating") |
| **Competition** | − | − | **Gause's *Paramecium***, Connell's barnacles, goats and the Abingdon tortoise on the Galapagos, flamingos and fish in South American lakes (for zooplankton) |
| **Predation** | + | − | Lion-deer; **herbivory is also predation**; control of ***Opuntia*** (prickly pear) in Australia by the **cactus-feeding moth (*Cactoblastis cactorum*)** — biological control; plant defences — thorns, **cardiac glycosides** of *Calotropis*, nicotine, caffeine, quinine |
| **Parasitism** | + | − | *Cuscuta*, liver fluke (two intermediate hosts — snail, fish); **brood parasitism** — **the cuckoo lays eggs in the crow's nest** |
| **Commensalism** | + | 0 | **Orchid on a mango branch (epiphyte)**, barnacles on whales, **cattle and the cattle egret**, sea anemone and clownfish |
| **Amensalism** | − | 0 | Suppression of bacteria by *Penicillium* (antibiotic), **allelopathy** (*Juglans* — juglone, *Eucalyptus*, *Parthenium*) |

**Community-level concepts:** **keystone species** — a disproportionately large effect (**Paine, 1966 — the starfish *Pisaster***; removing it led to the loss of 10+ species); **dominant species**; **flagship** (tiger), **umbrella** species; **indicator species** — **lichens (SO₂)**.

## 10. Attributes of a community

- **Analytical (quantitative):** **frequency** = quadrats in which the species occurred ÷ total quadrats studied × 100; **density** = total individuals ÷ total quadrats; **abundance** = total individuals ÷ quadrats in which it occurred; **cover/basal area**.
- **Importance Value Index (IVI)** = **relative density + relative frequency + relative dominance** (maximum **300**) — **Curtis and McIntosh (1950/51)**.
- **Raunkiaer's law of frequency (1934):** frequency classes A (1–20%) > B > C ≤ D < E (81–100%) — a "J-shaped" distribution — in a homogeneous community.
- **Raunkiaer's life forms** — by the position of perennating buds: **phanerophytes** (trees — buds high up; more in the tropics), **chamaephytes** (near the ground), **hemicryptophytes** (at the soil surface; temperate), **cryptophytes** (underground — rhizomes, bulbs), **therophytes** (pass the unfavourable season as seeds — **annuals**; deserts). **Biological spectrum** = their percentages.
- **Diversity:** **species richness** + **evenness**; indices — **Shannon-Wiener (H′)**, **Simpson (D)**; **α (within one community), β (between communities, along a gradient), γ (whole region) diversity — Whittaker**.
- **Synthetic characters** — presence, constance, fidelity.

## 11. Biogeochemical cycles

**Gaseous cycles** — reservoir in the atmosphere/hydrosphere — **C, N, O**; **sedimentary cycles** — reservoir in the **Earth's crust (rocks)** — **P, S**, Ca, K. Sedimentary cycles are slower and more sensitive to disturbance.

### Carbon cycle

- Carbon is **~49% of the dry weight** of organisms; **~71% of total global carbon is dissolved in the oceans** (NCERT) — CO₂ regulation.
- **~4 × 10¹³ kg of carbon is fixed** every year by photosynthesis (NCERT).
- Return — respiration, decomposition, **burning of fossil fuels, deforestation, volcanoes**; storage — fossil fuels, **limestone (CaCO₃)**, sediments.

### Phosphorus cycle

- Reservoir — **phosphate rocks**; into the soil by **weathering** → plants (H₂PO₄⁻) → animals (bones, teeth, ATP, nucleic acids) → returned by decomposition by **phosphate-solubilising bacteria**.
- **NCERT: compared with the carbon cycle, (i) the input of P from the atmosphere through rain is negligible, (ii) gaseous exchange of P between plants/animals and the atmosphere is negligible** (no P is released in respiration). It is lost to the sea as sediment — **guano** of seabirds is a route of return.

### Sulphur cycle

Reservoir — rocks (gypsum, pyrite), atmosphere (SO₂ — volcanoes, fossil fuels); plants take up **SO₄²⁻** (cysteine, methionine); decomposition gives H₂S; **sulphur bacteria** — *Beggiatoa*, *Thiobacillus* (oxidation), *Desulfovibrio* (sulphate-reducing, anaerobic).

### Nitrogen cycle — see Chapter 7. Water cycle — evaporation, transpiration (**evapotranspiration**), condensation, precipitation, infiltration, runoff; driven by solar energy.

## 12. Ecological succession

The **gradual and predictable** change of communities in an area until a **climax community** forms that is in equilibrium with the environment.
- **Pioneer species** → **seral stages** — the whole series = a **sere** → **climax**.
- **Primary succession** — where life never existed before (**bare rock, cooled lava, a new pond, sand dune**) — **very slow** (soil formation takes hundreds to thousands of years).
- **Secondary succession** — where life was destroyed but soil remains (**abandoned field, burnt/felled forest, flooded land**) — **fast**.
- **Autogenic** — the community itself changes the environment; **allogenic** — external factors.
- **Climax theories:** **monoclimax — Clements (1916)** — the climax of a region is decided by **climate** alone; **polyclimax — Tansley** — also by soil, topography etc.; **climax pattern — Whittaker (1953)**.
- As succession proceeds — **species diversity, biomass, soil depth and humus, nutrient retention and complexity of the food web increase**; **net productivity (P/R ratio)** is >1 at first and ≈1 at climax.
- **Both hydrosere and xerosere reach a climax under mesic (medium-water) conditions.**

### Hydrosere — from a pond/lake

| Stage | Species |
|---|---|
| 1. **Phytoplankton (pioneers)** | Blue-green algae, diatoms, green algae |
| 2. **Submerged rooted** | ***Hydrilla***, ***Vallisneria***, *Potamogeton*, *Utricularia*, *Chara* |
| 3. **Floating (rooted, floating leaves)** and free-floating | ***Nymphaea***, ***Nelumbo*** (lotus), ***Trapa***; *Pistia*, *Lemna*, *Wolffia*, *Azolla* |
| 4. **Reed-swamp (amphibious)** | ***Typha***, ***Phragmites***, ***Scirpus***, *Sagittaria* |

| 6. **Woodland/scrub** | *Salix*, *Populus*, *Alnus*, *Cephalanthus* |
| 7. **Climax forest** | According to climate (tropical rain forest/deciduous/temperate) |

## 13. Soil

### Soil formation (pedogenesis)

- Soil science = **pedology**; the Russian **Dokuchaev** — father of modern soil science.
- **Weathering:** **physical** (temperature changes, frost shattering, wind-water), **chemical** (hydration, hydrolysis, oxidation, carbonation), **biological** (lichens, roots, earthworms).
- **Five factors of soil formation (Jenny, 1941 — "CLORPT"):** **climate (Cl), organisms (O), relief/topography (R), parent material (P), time (T)**. ~2.5 cm of topsoil takes hundreds to a thousand years to form.
- **Residual/sedentary** — remains where formed; **transported** — **alluvial (water)**, **aeolian/loess (wind)**, glacial (glaciers), **colluvial (gravity)**.

### Soil profile — horizons

| Horizon | Name | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **O** | Organic horizon | Leaf litter (O₁ — undecomposed, O₂ — partly decomposed humus); distinct in forests, usually absent in cropland |
| **A** | **Topsoil** | **Rich in humus, dark, the most biological activity and roots**; **zone of eluviation (leaching)** |
| E | Eluvial horizon | Light-coloured, clay-iron-humus washed out (distinct in podzols) |
| **B** | **Subsoil** | **Zone of accumulation (illuviation)** — **clay, Fe and Al oxides, CaCO₃** washed down from above accumulate; little humus |
| **C** | Weathered parent material | Partly broken rock, almost no biological activity |
| **R** | Bedrock | Unweathered |

### Soil texture, water and properties

| Particle | Diameter (international/ISSS system) |
|---|---|
| **Gravel** | > 2 mm |
| **Coarse sand** | 2–0.2 mm |
| **Fine sand** | 0.2–0.02 mm |
| **Silt** | 0.02–0.002 mm |
| **Clay** | **< 0.002 mm** (colloidal; highest water retention and cation exchange) |

(In the USDA system sand is 2–0.05 mm, silt 0.05–0.002 mm.)

- **Loam** — a balanced mixture of sand, silt and clay (~40:40:20) — **best for agriculture**. Classes from the **soil texture triangle**.
- **Soil water:** **gravitational water** (drains quickly, unavailable), **capillary water** (**the main water available to plants**), **hygroscopic water** (tightly held by particles, unavailable), chemically bound water. **Field capacity** — water held after gravitational water drains (~−0.03 MPa); **permanent wilting point** (~−1.5 MPa) — **available water = field capacity − wilting point**. Water retention: **clay > loam > sand**; aeration and drainage: sand > loam > clay.
- **Soil pH** — most nutrients are most available at pH 6–7.5 (Chapter 7). **Cation exchange capacity (CEC)** — higher in clay and humus.
- **Soil organisms** — earthworm ("**farmer's friend**", studied by **Darwin**), bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes (the smell of soil — **geosmin**).

### Major soils of India (ICAR classification)

| Soil | Region | Features | Crops |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alluvial** — **the most extensive (~40%+)** | Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra plains, coastal deltas; **almost all of UP** | **Fairly rich in potash and lime, poor in N, P and humus**; very fertile; **khadar (new alluvium)** — river floodplains, a new layer every year, more fertile, fine; **bangar (old alluvium)** — higher ground beyond floods, contains **kankar (calcareous nodules)**, less fertile | Wheat, rice, sugarcane, pulses |
| **Black/regur** | Deccan lava plateau — Maharashtra, Gujarat, MP; **in UP, Bundelkhand (mar, kabar)** | **Montmorillonite** clay — swells when wet, deep **cracks** when dry ("**self-ploughing**"); **rich in Fe, lime, Mg, K; poor in N, P, humus**; high water retention | **Cotton** ("black cotton soil"), soybean, sorghum |
| **Red and yellow** | Eastern-southern peninsula, **Bundelkhand (parwa, rakar)** | Red from **iron oxide**; yellow when hydrated; poor in N, P, humus | Groundnut, pearl millet, pulses |
| **Laterite** | High-temperature, heavy-rainfall regions — Kerala, Karnataka, Odisha, Meghalaya | **Intense leaching** washes out silica → **rich in Fe and Al oxides; poor in N, P, K, humus**; used for bricks (later = brick) | Cashew, tea, coffee, rubber |
| **Arid/desert** | Rajasthan | Sandy, saline, negligible humus, kankar | Pearl millet, guar with irrigation |
| **Saline-alkaline (usar/reh/kallar/thur)** | **UP, Punjab, Haryana**, Rajasthan | Sodium salts; **white crust (reh)** on the surface; due to poor drainage, over-irrigation and waterlogging | (after reclamation) rice, dhaincha, berseem |
| **Peaty and marshy** | Kerala, Sundarbans, coastal | Very high organic matter, acidic | Rice |
| **Mountain/forest** | Himalaya | Humus-rich, acidic | Tea, fruits |

**Geographical sequence of soils in UP (north to south):** **Bhabar** (at the foot of the Shiwaliks, pebbles and boulders, highly permeable — rivers disappear) → **Terai** (marshy, fine, moist; sal forests; now farmed) → **Ganga-Yamuna plain (bangar and khadar)** → **southern plateau — Bundelkhand** (red and black soils — **mar, kabar, parwa, rakar**).

### Saline and alkaline (usar) soil and its reclamation

| | **Saline** | **Alkaline/sodic** |
|---|---|---|
| Salts | Soluble **NaCl, Na₂SO₄**, CaCl₂ | **Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃**; high exchangeable **Na⁺** (ESP > 15) |
| pH | < 8.5 | **> 8.5** (usually 9–10.5) |
| Electrical conductivity (EC) | > 4 dS/m | Low/medium |
| Appearance | White salt crust | Black alkali (dissolved humus), hard, impermeable |

**Reclamation:**
- **Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O)** — **the main amendment for alkaline (sodic) soil**: Ca²⁺ replaces exchangeable Na⁺ → Na₂SO₄ is formed, which is **leached by water**; also **pyrite (FeS₂)**, sulphur, pressmud (sugar mills).
- **Saline soil** — **good drainage (subsurface drains)** and **leaching with plenty of water**.
- **Green manure — dhaincha (*Sesbania aculeata*)**, organic manure; **salt-tolerant crops** — barley, rice, beet, berseem, cotton; trees — ***Prosopis juliflora***, *Acacia nilotica*, *Eucalyptus*, *Terminalia arjuna*.
- **Institutions:** **Central Soil Salinity Research Institute (CSSRI), Karnal** — regional centre at **Lucknow**; for usar reclamation in UP, the **UP Bhumi Sudhar Nigam (UP Land Development Corporation)**.

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** Statements on soil properties, profile and management — (I) Laterite soils are rich in iron and aluminium oxides but poor in N, P and humus. (II) The B-horizon is the main zone of organic accumulation and biological activity. (III) Saline and alkaline soils can be reclaimed by drainage and gypsum. (IV) Alluvial soils are generally fertile because of their mineral content and favourable texture. Correct statements? (A) II and III only (B) I, II, III and IV (C) **I, III and IV only** (D) I and II only
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(C)** — statement II is wrong: organic accumulation and biological activity are greater in the **A-horizon (topsoil)**; the B-horizon is the zone of **illuviation**.

### Soil erosion and conservation

- **Erosion** — removal of topsoil. **Water erosion:** **sheet** → **rill** → **gully** → **ravines** — **the ravines of the Chambal and Yamuna (Agra, Etawah, Auraiya, Jalaun — UP)**; **wind erosion** — arid regions (Thar).
- **Causes:** **deforestation, overgrazing, unscientific ploughing (along the slope), leaving land bare after harvest**, shifting (jhum) cultivation, mining.
- **Conservation measures:** **contour ploughing**, **terracing**, **strip cropping**, **cover crops**, **mulching**, **shelter belts (windbreaks)**, **check dams**, bunding, **afforestation**, controlled grazing, crop rotation.

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** Why does soil erosion increase after ploughing and harvesting? (A) **Removal of the vegetation cover exposes the soil directly to the action of wind and water** (B) Rise in soil salinity (C) Rise in microbial activity (D) Decrease in soil pore size
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(A)** — roots bind the particles and leaves absorb the impact of raindrops; when they are removed, erosion follows.

## Traps and exceptions

- **"Ecosystem" — Tansley (1935)**, "Ecology" — Haeckel; "Biocoenosis" — Möbius.
- **The energy pyramid is never inverted**; the biomass pyramid is inverted in the sea; the pyramid of numbers is inverted in a tree/parasitic chain.
- **In terrestrial ecosystems more energy flows through the detritus chain**, not the grazing chain.
- **The oceans cover 70% of the area yet give only ~55 billion tonnes of NPP** (total ~170) — nutrient-limited.
- **The phosphorus cycle has almost no gaseous phase** — a sedimentary cycle.
- **The solubility of O₂ in water falls as temperature rises.**
- **Shelford — both deficiency and excess** are limiting; Liebig — only the minimum.
- **Fundamental niche > realized niche** (because of competition).
- **Clumped distribution is the most common**, random is rare; uniform — intense competition/allelopathy.
- **Brood parasitism** — cuckoo (parasite) and crow (host).
- **An epiphytic orchid is a commensal, not a parasite** (it only takes support).
- **The B-horizon is the zone of illuviation, the A-horizon of eluviation and biological activity.**
- **Khadar is new, bangar old** (kankar-bearing) alluvium.
- **Alkaline soil → gypsum; acidic soil → lime.**
- **Secondary succession is faster than primary**; both hydrosere and xerosere move towards a mesic climax.

## Quick revision

- Liebig 1840 — minimum; Shelford 1913 — tolerance; Blackman 1905.
- Grinnell — spatial niche; Elton — functional; Hutchinson — n-dimensional (fundamental and realized).
- Gause — competitive exclusion (*Paramecium*); MacArthur — resource partitioning in warblers; Connell — barnacles.
- NPP = GPP − R; biosphere ~170 billion tonnes; oceans ~55.
- Only 2–10% of PAR is captured.
- Decomposition: fragmentation → leaching → catabolism → humification → mineralisation.
- Lindeman 1942 — 10% law.
- Sea — inverted biomass pyramid; energy pyramid always upright.
- Tilman — stability from diversity; Ehrlich — rivet popper.
- dN/dt = rN(K−N)/K — logistic; K = carrying capacity.
- Survivorship: I humans, II birds/*Hydra*, III oysters/fish.
- Clumped distribution most common.
- *Opuntia* — biological control by *Cactoblastis* (Australia).
- Paine — keystone species *Pisaster*.
- IVI = relative density + frequency + dominance (maximum 300).
- Raunkiaer — life forms; therophytes — deserts; phanerophytes — tropics.
- ~71% of global carbon in the oceans; carbon — 49% of the dry weight of organisms.
- Clements — monoclimax; Tansley — polyclimax; Whittaker — climax pattern.
- Hydrosere: phytoplankton → submerged → floating → reed → sedge → woodland → forest.
- Xerosere: crustose lichen → foliose lichen → moss → herbs → shrubs → forest.
- Factors of soil formation — Jenny's CLORPT; clay < 0.002 mm.
- Khadar — new alluvium; bangar — old, kankar; usar-reh — reclaimed with gypsum.
