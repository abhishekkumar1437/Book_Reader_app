This chapter starts from NCERT Class 11 "Locomotion and Movement" and goes up to B.Sc./M.Sc. level points — sarcomere bands, excitation–contraction coupling, the role of ATP in the cross-bridge cycle, types of muscle fibres and their energy sources. In UP PGT 2026 an "integrative" question linked muscle contraction with excretion (uricotelism), so the mechanism must be understood along with its reasons.

> **In simple words:** Movement is a general property of living things — streaming of cytoplasm, beating of cilia, bending of limbs. When movement carries the whole organism from one place to another, it is called locomotion. Every locomotion is movement, but not every movement is locomotion. In muscle, two kinds of protein filaments — thin actin and thick myosin — slide over each other and shorten the muscle; this needs Ca²⁺ ions and ATP.

English chapter for more depth: [Muscles, Skeleton and Movement](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/physiology/muscles-skeleton-and-movement).

## 1. Types of movement

| Type | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Amoeboid movement | Pseudopodia — cytoplasmic streaming and microfilaments (actin) | *Amoeba*, white blood cells (macrophages, neutrophils), migrating embryonic cells |
| Ciliary movement | Cilia and flagella — 9+2 microtubule arrangement, dynein ATPase | *Paramecium*, tracheal epithelium, oviduct (Fallopian tube), sperm (flagellum) |
| Muscular movement | Sliding of actin and myosin | Movement of limbs, jaws, tongue; locomotion |

**Theories of amoeboid movement:**

- **Sol–gel theory** — Hyman (1917) and Mast (1926): at the posterior end the plasmagel (ectoplasm) contracts and pushes the plasmasol forward; on reaching the front, the sol turns back into gel.
- Modern view: actin polymerisation at the leading edge pushes the pseudopodium forward and myosin-II contracts the rear end.

**Cilia vs flagella:**

| Feature | Cilia | Flagella |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Very many | Few (1 to a few) |
| Length | Short (5–10 µm) | Long (up to ≈150 µm) |
| Movement | Oar-like, coordinated — metachronal rhythm | Undulating (wave-like) |
| Base | Basal body — 9+0 triplet arrangement | Basal body 9+0 |

> **Remember:** Axoneme = 9+2 (9 peripheral doublets + 2 central singlets); basal body and centriole = 9+0 triplets (9 triplets, no microtubule in the centre). Dynein arms use ATP to slide microtubules over one another. In Kartagener syndrome (primary ciliary dyskinesia), a dynein defect causes infertility and respiratory infections.

## 2. Types of muscle tissue

| Feature | Skeletal | Cardiac | Smooth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Other names | Striated, voluntary | Striated, involuntary | Unstriated, involuntary, visceral |
| Cell shape | Long, cylindrical, unbranched | Branched | Spindle-shaped |
| Nucleus | Multinucleate (syncytium), peripheral | 1 (sometimes 2), central | 1, central |
| Intercalated disc | No | Yes (gap junctions + desmosomes) | No |
| T-tubule | At the A–I junction (2 per sarcomere) | At the Z-line (1 per sarcomere) | Absent (caveolae) |
| Source of Ca²⁺ | Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) | SR + extracellular | Mainly extracellular |
| Ca²⁺ binds to | Troponin-C | Troponin-C | Calmodulin → MLCK |
| Fatigue | Quick | Does not fatigue | Slow; sustained contraction |
| Tetanus | Possible | No (long refractory period) | — |

> **Exam angle:** Cardiac muscle cannot be tetanised because its absolute refractory period (≈250 ms) is almost equal to the whole contraction period. This is a favourite point in statement-based questions.

## 3. Structure of skeletal muscle — from large to small

Muscle → fascicle (surrounded by perimysium) → muscle fibre (= one cell, surrounded by endomysium) → myofibril → sarcomere → myofilaments (actin, myosin).

- Connective tissue covering the whole muscle: **epimysium**; it continues into the tendon and attaches to bone.
- Membrane of the muscle fibre: **sarcolemma**; cytoplasm: **sarcoplasm**; endoplasmic reticulum: **sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)** — the store of Ca²⁺.
- **Triad** = 1 T-tubule + 2 terminal cisternae. In cardiac muscle, a **diad**.

| Structure | What it is | During contraction |
|---|---|---|
| Z-line (Z-disc, *Zwischenscheibe*) | Boundary of the sarcomere; thin filaments are anchored here; α-actinin | Come closer |
| I-band (isotropic, light) | Only thin filaments; Z-line in the middle | Shortens |
| A-band (anisotropic, dark) | Full length of the thick filaments (thin ones partly overlap) | **Length unchanged** |
| H-zone (*Hensen's zone* / helle) | Central part of the A-band with only thick filaments | Shortens or disappears |
| M-line (*Mittelscheibe*) | Middle of the H-zone; holds the thick filaments together (myomesin) | Fixed |

- Resting sarcomere length ≈ **2.0–2.5 µm** (maximum force at ≈ 2.0–2.2 µm — length–tension relationship).
- Each thick filament is surrounded by about 6 thin filaments, and each thin filament by 3 thick filaments (hexagonal arrangement, vertebrate skeletal muscle).
- **Titin** — the largest known protein; runs from the Z-line to the M-line and gives the muscle elasticity (a spring). **Nebulin** sets the length of the thin filament. **Dystrophin** links actin to the sarcolemma.

### Molecular organisation of the myofilaments

| Filament | Components |
|---|---|
| Thin filament (≈7 nm) | F-actin (double helix of G-actin monomers) + tropomyosin (in the groove) + troponin complex |
| Thick filament (≈15 nm) | About 300 myosin-II molecules; each = 2 heavy chains + 4 light chains; **heavy meromyosin (HMM)** = head (ATPase + actin-binding site) and arm; **light meromyosin (LMM)** = tail |

**Three subunits of troponin:** troponin-C (binds Ca²⁺), troponin-I (inhibits the actin–myosin interaction), troponin-T (binds tropomyosin). In a heart attack (myocardial infarction), a rise of **cardiac troponin (cTnI/cTnT)** in the blood is a diagnostic marker.

> **Memory trick:** "C = Calcium, I = Inhibit, T = Tropomyosin". For the bands — "**A**nisotropic is **A**lways the same; **I** and **H** shrink."

## 4. Sliding Filament Theory

- Proposed by: **A.F. Huxley & R. Niedergerke** and **H.E. Huxley & Jean Hanson** — the papers of both teams were published together in *Nature* in **1954**.
- Basic statement: the filaments do not change length during contraction; the thin filaments slide over the thick filaments towards the centre of the sarcomere (M-line).
- Result: the Z–Z distance decreases; the I-band and H-zone shorten; the A-band stays the same.

## 5. Excitation–Contraction Coupling

1. The impulse of the motor neuron reaches the **neuromuscular junction (NMJ)** / **motor end plate**.
2. Acetylcholine (ACh) is released → **nicotinic ACh receptors** of the sarcolemma (ligand-gated Na⁺/K⁺ channels) open → end-plate potential → action potential on the sarcolemma.
3. The action potential travels inward along the **T-tubules** → the **DHP receptor (dihydropyridine receptor, L-type Ca²⁺ channel, voltage sensor)** mechanically opens the **ryanodine receptor (RyR1)** of the SR.
4. Ca²⁺ moves from the SR into the sarcoplasm (from ≈10⁻⁷ M to ≈10⁻⁵ M).
5. Ca²⁺ + **troponin-C** → tropomyosin shifts → the **active sites** on actin are exposed.
6. Myosin heads bind to actin and form **cross-bridges** → power stroke.
7. Relaxation: ACh is broken down by **acetylcholinesterase**; the **SERCA pump** (Ca²⁺-ATPase) uses ATP to send Ca²⁺ back into the SR (where it binds calsequestrin); tropomyosin again covers the active sites.

> **In simple words:** The nerve "presses the button" (ACh), the electricity (action potential) goes inside through the T-tubule, the SR's "Ca²⁺ store" opens, Ca²⁺ opens the troponin "lock", and myosin pulls actin.

## 6. Cross-bridge cycle and the three roles of ATP

| Step | Event |
|---|---|
| 1. ATP binding | ATP binds to the myosin head → the head **detaches** from actin |
| 2. ATP hydrolysis | ATP → ADP + Pi (both stay on the head); head in the "cocked" (high-energy, 90°) state |
| 3. Cross-bridge formation | The head binds to an exposed site on actin |
| 4. Power stroke | When Pi is released the head tilts to 45° and pulls the thin filament towards the M-line; then ADP is released |
| 5. Rigor state | The head stays attached to actin until a new ATP arrives |

**Three jobs of ATP:** (i) detaching the myosin head from actin, (ii) energising ("cocking") the head, (iii) returning Ca²⁺ to the SR through the SERCA pump (relaxation). The Na⁺/K⁺ pump also runs on ATP.

> **Remember:** **Rigor mortis** — after death ATP production stops, so myosin heads cannot detach from actin; the muscles stiffen (begins in ≈3–4 hours, maximum at ≈12 hours, ends after 24–48 hours due to protein breakdown). That is, rigor is due to a **lack** of ATP, not an excess.

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** A desert vertebrate minimises water loss during nitrogen excretion and shows rapid skeletal-muscle contraction while running. Which explanation links both adaptations?
> (A) Ureotelism does not need water conservation, while sarcomeres shorten by Ca²⁺-independent contraction
> (B) Uricotelism removes nitrogen with minimal water, while ATP-driven actin–myosin sliding produces contraction
> (C) Ammonotelism reduces water loss, while muscle contraction depends on ATP-independent actin polymerisation
> (D) Urea is excreted in solid form, while contraction occurs by passive elastic recoil
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(B)** — uric acid is almost insoluble and is excreted in semi-solid form with very little water; contraction is Ca²⁺-dependent, ATP-driven sliding. ## 7. Types and properties of muscle contraction

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Threshold stimulus | Minimum stimulus needed for contraction |
| All-or-none law | Applies to a **single muscle fibre** (and one motor unit), not to the whole muscle — in the whole muscle force increases by recruitment of more motor units |
| Motor unit | One motor neuron + all its muscle fibres; small in eye muscles (≈10 fibres), large in the thigh (thousands) |
| Simple twitch | One stimulus → one contraction; latent period (≈10 ms, in frog gastrocnemius), contraction period (≈40 ms), relaxation period (≈50 ms) — total ≈0.1 s |
| Summation | Contractions add up with repeated stimuli |
| Tetanus | Sustained, smooth maximal contraction with very rapid stimuli (incomplete/complete tetanus); different from the **disease "tetanus"** (toxin tetanospasmin of *Clostridium tetani*) |
| Treppe (staircase effect) | Progressively stronger contractions with equal stimuli (rise in Ca²⁺ and temperature) |
| Muscle fatigue | Fall in ATP/glycogen and build-up of Pi and H⁺ (lactic acid) with repeated contraction; the **NMJ** fatigues first (in a laboratory preparation) |
| Muscle tone (tonus) | Slight partial contraction even at rest |
| Isometric | Length constant, tension increases — pushing a wall, maintaining posture |
| Isotonic | Tension constant, length changes — lifting a weight (concentric and eccentric) |

## 8. Red and white muscle fibres

| Feature | Red / slow oxidative (Type I) | White / fast glycolytic (Type IIb/IIx) |
|---|---|---|
| Myoglobin | High (red colour) | Low |
| Mitochondria | Many | Few |
| Blood capillaries | Dense | Few |
| SR | Less developed | Well developed (fast Ca²⁺ cycling) |
| Energy | Aerobic oxidation | Anaerobic glycolysis (lactic acid) |
| Contraction | Slow, sustained | Fast, powerful, fatigues quickly |
| Examples | Postural back muscles, flight muscles of flying birds (pigeon), marathon runners | Extrinsic eye muscles, breast muscle of chicken, sprinters |

Type IIa = fast oxidative (intermediate).

> **Exam angle:** Myoglobin has a higher O₂ affinity than haemoglobin (hyperbolic curve, P₅₀ ≈ 1–3 mmHg) — so it acts as an O₂ store in muscle. Diving mammals (whales, seals) have very high myoglobin.

## 9. Energy sources of muscle and the Cori cycle

| Source | Duration | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Stored ATP | 2–3 s | Very little |
| Creatine phosphate (phosphagen) | ≈10 s | Creatine kinase: CP + ADP → creatine + ATP; in invertebrates **arginine phosphate** |
| Anaerobic glycolysis | ≈1–2 minutes | 2 ATP per glucose; lactic acid |
| Aerobic respiration | Long duration | ≈30–32 ATP per glucose (older books 36–38) |

**Cori cycle** — Carl & Gerty Cori (Nobel 1947): **lactate** formed by glycolysis in muscle goes through the blood to the **liver** → glucose by gluconeogenesis → back to muscle through the blood. The liver spends 6 ATP while the muscle gains 2 ATP — so the cycle has a net energy cost.

**Oxygen debt (EPOC — Excess Post-exercise Oxygen Consumption):** extra O₂ after exercise — for clearing lactate, rebuilding CP and ATP, and reloading myoglobin with O₂.

**Glucose–alanine cycle:** alanine from muscle goes to the liver to form glucose, and its nitrogen goes into urea.

## 11. Joints

| Type | Movement | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Fibrous / immovable (synarthrosis)** | No movement | Sutures between skull bones; tooth–jaw (gomphosis); lower end of tibia–fibula (syndesmosis) |
| **Cartilaginous / slightly movable (amphiarthrosis)** | Limited movement | Intervertebral discs between vertebrae; pubic symphysis; first rib–sternum (synchondrosis) |
| **Synovial / freely movable (diarthrosis)** | Free movement; synovial cavity and fluid (hyaluronic acid) | See below |

**Subtypes of synovial joints:**

| Subtype | Axis | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ball and socket | Multiaxial | Shoulder (humerus–glenoid cavity of scapula), hip (femur–acetabulum) |
| Hinge | Uniaxial | Elbow, knee, between fingers (interphalangeal) |
| Pivot | Uniaxial (rotation) | Atlas–axis (shaking the head "no"); proximal radius–ulna |
| Gliding (plane) | Sliding | Between carpals, between tarsals |
| Saddle | Biaxial | Carpometacarpal joint of the thumb (trapezium–first metacarpal) |
| Ellipsoid / condyloid | Biaxial | Wrist (radiocarpal), metacarpophalangeal |

> **Common mistake:** The atlas–skull (occipital condyle) joint is ellipsoid — nodding "yes"; the atlas–axis joint is a pivot — shaking "no". The knee is mainly a hinge joint (some books say "modified hinge").

**Ligament vs tendon:** tendon = joins muscle to bone, inelastic, white fibrous (collagen); ligament = joins bone to bone, elastic (yellow fibrous, more elastin).

## 12. Antagonistic muscles and levers

- **Antagonistic muscles:** biceps (bends the elbow, flexor) vs triceps (straightens it, extensor).
- Other pairs: adductor (towards the body axis) vs abductor; rotator; depressor vs levator.
- **Levers:** first class — balancing the head on the atlas; second class — rising on the toes; third class (most common) — lifting the forearm by the biceps at the elbow.

## 13. Disorders of the musculoskeletal system

| Disorder | Cause / features |
|---|---|
| Myasthenia gravis | Autoimmune — antibodies against **nicotinic ACh receptors at the NMJ**; fatigue, drooping eyelid (ptosis), weakness; treated with anticholinesterases (neostigmine, pyridostigmine) |
| Muscular dystrophy | Genetic; **Duchenne (DMD)** — X-linked recessive, defect in the **dystrophin** gene (the largest human gene, ≈2.4 Mb); in boys; pseudohypertrophy of the calves |
| Tetany | **Low blood Ca²⁺** (hypocalcaemia, often hypoparathyroidism) → severe muscle spasms; carpopedal spasm, Trousseau and Chvostek signs |
| Arthritis | Inflammation of joints; **osteoarthritis** — wear of cartilage (age); **rheumatoid arthritis** — autoimmune, inflammation of the synovial membrane |
| Gout | Deposition of **uric acid** (monosodium urate) crystals in joints; often the big toe; treated with allopurinol (xanthine oxidase inhibitor) |
| Osteoporosis | Loss of bone mass, brittleness; oestrogen deficiency after menopause, Ca²⁺/vitamin D deficiency |
| Rickets / osteomalacia | Vitamin D deficiency — rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults |
| Cramp | Sudden painful involuntary contraction (dehydration, electrolyte imbalance) |
| Slipped disc | The nucleus pulposus of an intervertebral disc bulges out — pressure on a spinal nerve |
| Botulism | *Clostridium botulinum* toxin blocks ACh release → flaccid paralysis |
| Curare poison | Competitive blocker of ACh receptors → paralysis (muscle relaxant in surgery) |

## Traps and exceptions

- During contraction the **A-band length does not change**; the I-band and H-zone shorten. The actin and myosin filaments themselves also do not change length.
- The all-or-none law applies to a single fibre/motor unit, not to the whole muscle.
- Smooth muscle has no troponin; Ca²⁺ binds **calmodulin** and activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK) — the control is myosin-based, not actin-based.
- Rigor mortis is due to the **absence** of ATP — ATP is needed to detach cross-bridges, not to form them.
- ATP hydrolysis occurs **before** the power stroke (the head is cocked); the power stroke is linked to Pi release.
- Mammalian red blood cells have no mitochondria — they too get energy only from glycolysis (another source of lactate in the Cori cycle).
- The hyoid bone does not form a joint with any other bone.
- Some books count the ear ossicles in the skull and write skull = 28; NCERT keeps skull 22 and ear ossicles 6 separately.
- "Tetanus" (physiological state) and "tetanus" (bacterial disease) are different.
- Myasthenia gravis is due to anti-receptor antibodies; in Lambert–Eaton syndrome the antibodies are against presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels.

## Quick revision

- Movement is a property of all organisms; locomotion = movement with change of place.
- Axoneme 9+2; basal body/centriole 9+0 triplets; motor protein dynein.
- Sarcomere = the part between two Z-lines; resting length ≈2.0–2.5 µm.
- A-band = thick filaments (constant); I-band = only thin; H-zone = only thick.
- Sliding filament theory: Huxley–Hanson and Huxley–Niedergerke, 1954.
- Thin filament = actin + tropomyosin + troponin (C, I, T).
- Myosin head (HMM) has the ATPase and actin-binding site.
- Triad = T-tubule + 2 terminal cisternae.
- DHP receptor (voltage sensor) → ryanodine receptor → Ca²⁺ from SR.
- Ca²⁺ binds troponin-C and exposes the active sites.
- The SERCA pump returns Ca²⁺ to the SR and causes relaxation.
- ATP: detaching cross-bridges, cocking the head, Ca²⁺ pump.
- Rigor mortis = lack of ATP.
- Red fibres: myoglobin, mitochondria, aerobic, slow; white: glycolytic, fast, fatigue.
- Cori cycle: muscle lactate → liver glucose → muscle (the Coris, Nobel 1947).
- Creatine phosphate gives ≈10 s of energy; arginine phosphate in invertebrates.
- 206 bones = axial 80 + appendicular 126.
- Vertebrae: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 1 sacrum, 1 coccyx = 26.
- Ribs: true 7 pairs, false 3, floating 2.
- Ball and socket: shoulder, hip; pivot: atlas–axis; saddle: carpometacarpal of thumb.
- Myasthenia gravis: autoantibodies against ACh receptors.
- Duchenne muscular dystrophy: dystrophin, X-linked.
- Tetany: Ca²⁺ deficiency; gout: uric acid crystals.
- No tetanus in cardiac muscle (long refractory period).

## Practice questions (with answers)

1. Which band keeps its length unchanged during muscle contraction? — **A-band** (it is the full length of the thick filaments).
2. In which year was the sliding filament theory proposed? — **1954** (Huxley–Hanson and Huxley–Niedergerke).
3. In skeletal muscle, Ca²⁺ binds to which protein? — **Troponin-C** (shifts tropomyosin).
4. Ca²⁺-binding protein in smooth muscle? — **Calmodulin** (activates MLCK).
5. Which part of myosin has ATPase activity? — **Head (heavy meromyosin)**.
6. Cause of rigor mortis? — **Lack of ATP** (cross-bridges cannot detach).
7. Pump that returns Ca²⁺ to the SR? — **SERCA (Ca²⁺-ATPase)** (uses ATP).
8. Components of a triad? — **1 T-tubule + 2 terminal cisternae** (skeletal muscle).
9. Which fibres look red because of high myoglobin? — **Type I slow oxidative** (aerobic).
10. Where is lactate converted to glucose in the Cori cycle? — **Liver** (gluconeogenesis).
11. How many bones in the axial skeleton of an adult human? — **80** (appendicular 126).
12. Total number of vertebrae (adult)? — **26** (sacrum and coccyx fused).
13. Type of the atlas–axis joint? — **Pivot** (turning the head).
14. Joint between the carpal and metacarpal of the thumb? — **Saddle** (biaxial).
15. What kind of joint are the skull sutures? — **Fibrous (immovable)**.
16. In myasthenia gravis, antibodies are against what? — **Nicotinic ACh receptors** (NMJ).
17. Defective protein in Duchenne muscular dystrophy? — **Dystrophin** (X-linked recessive).
18. Tetany is due to deficiency of which ion? — **Ca²⁺** (hypocalcaemia).
19. What is deposited in gout? — **Uric acid (urate) crystals**.
20. Why can cardiac muscle not be tetanised? — **Long absolute refractory period** (almost equal to the contraction period).
