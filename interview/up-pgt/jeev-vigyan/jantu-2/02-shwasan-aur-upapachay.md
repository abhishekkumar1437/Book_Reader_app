This chapter takes NCERT Class 11 "Breathing and Exchange of Gases" and the animal side of "Respiration in Plants" up to B.Sc. (Guyton) level — the chemistry of gas transport, shifts of the oxygen dissociation curve (ODC), chemoreceptors and metabolic pathways. UP PGT 2026 asked an "integrated" question linking hyperventilation at **high altitude** with haemoglobin affinity — that is, regulation of breathing and gas transport must be understood together.

> **In simple words:** Respiration has two parts — **breathing**, i.e. moving air into and out of the lungs (a physical process), and **cellular respiration**, i.e. burning food with oxygen inside the cell to make ATP (a chemical process). In between, blood carries O₂ from the lungs to the tissues and CO₂ from the tissues to the lungs. Gases always diffuse from **higher partial pressure to lower partial pressure**.

English chapter for more depth: [Respiration and Gas Transport](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/physiology/respiration-and-gas-transport).

## 1. Respiratory organs of different animals

| Respiratory surface | Animals | Special |
|---|---|---|
| General body surface (diffusion) | Sponges, coelenterates, flatworms, *Amoeba* | No special organ |
| Moist skin (cutaneous) | Earthworm, frog (in water and during hibernation) | Dense capillaries in the skin |
| Tracheal system | Insects (cockroach) | O₂ goes directly to the tissues; role of blood negligible; spiracles — 10 pairs in the cockroach |
| Gills | Fish, *Palaemon*, *Pila*, tadpole | Dissolved O₂ in water; in fish **counter-current exchange** — water and blood flow in opposite directions |
| Book lungs | Spider, scorpion | — |
| Lungs | Reptiles, birds, mammals, adult frog | Positive-pressure breathing in the frog (buccal pumping) |
| Lungs + air sacs | **Birds** | 9 air sacs (in most); **unidirectional** airflow in the **parabronchi**; a given volume of air leaves only after two breathing cycles; **no alveoli**; cross-current exchange |

> **Exam angle:** The bird lung is more efficient than the mammalian lung — unidirectional flow, no residual "stale" air, cross-current exchange. This meets the high energy demand of flight and migration.

## 2. Human respiratory system

**Route:** External nostrils → nasal cavity → pharynx → **larynx ("voice box"; thyroid cartilage = "Adam's apple")** → trachea (C-shaped rings of **hyaline cartilage** — open at the back) → primary bronchi → secondary and tertiary bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles → respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → **alveoli**.

- **Conducting part:** from the nose to the terminal bronchioles — filters, warms and moistens the air; no gas exchange; this is the **dead space (~150 mL)**.
- **Respiratory part:** alveoli and their ducts — the actual exchange.
- About 300 million alveoli, surface ~70–100 m²; thickness of the diffusion membrane ~0.5 µm (much less than a millimetre).
- **Alveolar cells:** type-I pneumocytes (flat, ~95% of the surface, exchange), **type-II pneumocytes — secrete surfactant** (mainly **dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine, DPPC**) — lowers surface tension and keeps alveoli from collapsing; alveolar macrophages ("dust cells").
- Premature babies lack surfactant → **respiratory distress syndrome (hyaline membrane disease)**; in the foetus surfactant begins to form at ~24–28 weeks and is sufficient by 34–35 weeks.
- The lungs are covered by a double **pleura**; between the layers is pleural fluid (reduces friction). The right lung has 3 lobes, the left 2 (a **cardiac notch** for the heart).
- Thoracic cavity — vertebral column dorsally, sternum ventrally, ribs laterally, **diaphragm** below — an air-tight chamber.

## 3. Mechanism of breathing

| | Inspiration | Expiration |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **Active** (muscle contraction) | Normally **passive** (muscle relaxation, elastic recoil of the lungs) |
| Diaphragm | Contracts → flattens, moves down (volume ↑ along the vertical axis) | Relaxes → dome-shaped, moves up |
| External intercostal muscles | Contract → ribs and sternum move up and forward (volume ↑ along the dorso-ventral axis) | Relax |
| Intrapulmonary pressure | **Lower** than atmospheric (~ −1 mmHg) → air flows in | **Higher** than atmospheric (~ +1 mmHg) → air flows out |

- **Forced expiration:** internal intercostal muscles and abdominal muscles become active.
- **Intrapleural pressure** is always negative (~ −4 mmHg at rest) — keeps the lungs expanded; a hole in the chest lets air in and the lung collapses (**pneumothorax**).
- Boyle's law (P × V = constant) — when volume increases, pressure decreases.
- Healthy adult: 12–16 breaths/minute; newborn ~40–60.

## 4. Pulmonary volumes and capacities

| Quantity | Definition | Normal value (NCERT) |
|---|---|---|
| Tidal volume (TV) | Air in/out in normal breathing | ~500 mL |
| Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) | Extra air forcibly inspired after a normal inspiration | 2500–3000 mL |
| Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) | Extra air forcibly expired after a normal expiration | 1000–1100 mL |
| Residual volume (RV) | Air remaining even after forced expiration | 1100–1200 mL |
| Inspiratory capacity (IC) | TV + IRV | ~3000–3500 mL |
| Functional residual capacity (FRC) | ERV + RV | ~2100–2300 mL |
| **Vital capacity (VC)** | TV + IRV + ERV — maximum expiration after a maximum inspiration | ~3500–4500 mL |
| Total lung capacity (TLC) | VC + RV | ~5000–6000 mL |

<div class="formula">

**Minute ventilation** = TV × respiratory rate = 500 × 12 = **6000 mL/minute** (6–8 L)

**Alveolar ventilation** = (TV − dead space) × rate = (500 − 150) × 12 = **4200 mL/minute**

</div>

> **Common mistake:** A spirometer cannot measure **residual volume (RV)** — so FRC and TLC cannot be measured directly either (helium dilution or body plethysmography is needed). Vital capacity is higher in athletes and mountain dwellers; lower in women than in men.

> **B.Sc. point:** Shallow rapid breathing reduces alveolar ventilation (the dead space stays the same) — so deep slow breathing is more efficient.

## 5. Exchange of gases — partial pressures (mm Hg)

| Gas | Atmosphere | Alveoli | Deoxygenated blood | Oxygenated blood | Tissues |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| O₂ | 159 | 104 | 40 | 95 | 40 |
| CO₂ | 0.3 | 40 | 45 | 40 | 45 |

- O₂ gradient: alveoli → blood = 104 − 40 = **64 mm Hg**; CO₂ gradient: blood → alveoli = 45 − 40 = **only 5 mm Hg**.
- Yet CO₂ diffuses easily because its **solubility is 20–25 times** that of O₂.
- Factors affecting diffusion — partial pressure gradient, solubility, membrane thickness (Fick's law: proportional to surface area, inversely proportional to thickness).

## 6. Transport of oxygen

- **~97%** of O₂ is carried in RBCs as **oxyhaemoglobin**; **~3%** is dissolved in plasma.
- One haemoglobin molecule (2α + 2β chains, 4 haems, each with Fe²⁺) binds at most **4 O₂**; 1 g Hb ≈ **1.34 mL O₂**; Hb ~15 g/100 mL → ~20 mL O₂/100 mL blood.
- Under normal resting conditions every 100 mL of oxygenated blood delivers ~**5 mL O₂** to the tissues (NCERT).
- Binding of O₂ does not oxidise Fe²⁺ (it is oxygenation, not oxidation); the Fe³⁺ form, **methaemoglobin**, cannot carry O₂.

### Oxygen–Haemoglobin Dissociation Curve (ODC)

% saturation of Hb (y-axis) against pO₂ (x-axis) — **sigmoid (S-shaped)**, because binding of the first O₂ raises the affinity of the remaining haems (**cooperative binding** — T (tense) → R (relaxed) state). **P₅₀** (pO₂ at 50% saturation) is normally ~**26–27 mm Hg**.

| Curve shifts to the **right** (affinity ↓, O₂ released easily) | Curve shifts to the **left** (affinity ↑) |
|---|---|
| pCO₂ ↑ | pCO₂ ↓ |
| H⁺ ↑ (pH ↓) — **Bohr effect** | pH ↑ (alkalinity) |
| Temperature ↑ (exercising muscle) | Temperature ↓ |
| **2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG / DPG)** ↑ (high altitude, anaemia) | 2,3-BPG ↓ (stored blood) |
| — | **Foetal haemoglobin (HbF, α₂γ₂)** — binds 2,3-BPG poorly |
| — | **Myoglobin** (single chain, hyperbolic curve, P₅₀ ~1–3) |
| — | **Carbon monoxide (CO)** |

> **Remember — Bohr effect (1904):** When CO₂ and H⁺ rise, the O₂ affinity of Hb falls. In the tissues (high CO₂) O₂ is released — which is exactly what is useful. "CADET, face Right": **C**O₂, **A**cid, **D**PG, **E**xercise, **T**emperature → right shift.

> **Exam angle:** Because of the higher affinity of HbF, O₂ passes from the mother's blood to the foetal blood. The affinity of CO for Hb is ~**200–250 times** that of O₂ — **carboxyhaemoglobin** forms, and the curve shifts left so that even the remaining O₂ is not released to the tissues.

## 7. Transport of carbon dioxide

| Form | Proportion (approx.) |
|---|---|
| **Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)** — in plasma (formed in RBCs) | ~70% |
| **Carbaminohaemoglobin** (bound to amino groups of Hb) | ~20–25% |
| Dissolved in plasma | ~7% |

**Reaction in the RBC** (enzyme **carbonic anhydrase** — one of the fastest known enzymes, contains Zn²⁺):

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

- In the tissues: HCO₃⁻ moves from the RBC into the plasma and, to maintain electrical neutrality, **Cl⁻ moves in** — the **chloride shift (Hamburger shift)**. H⁺ is buffered by Hb.
- In the lungs: the reverse — **reverse chloride shift**, CO₂ is released.
- Every 100 mL of deoxygenated blood delivers ~**4 mL CO₂** to the alveoli (NCERT).

> **Remember — Haldane effect:** Deoxygenated (deoxy) Hb binds more CO₂ and H⁺; in the lungs binding of O₂ releases CO₂ and H⁺ from Hb. **Bohr effect = CO₂ affects O₂ transport; Haldane effect = O₂ affects CO₂ transport** — the two complement each other.

## 8. Regulation of respiration

| Centre | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory rhythm centre** — dorsal respiratory group (DRG, mainly inspiration) and ventral respiratory group (VRG, forced breathing) | **Medulla oblongata** | Generates the basic rhythm (B.Sc.: the actual rhythm generator is the **Pre-Bötzinger complex** of the VRG) |
| **Pneumotaxic centre** | **Pons** (upper) | Shortens the duration of inspiration and so increases the rate |
| Apneustic centre | Pons (lower) | Prolonged inspiration (B.Sc. level) |
| **Chemosensitive area (central chemoreceptors)** | Ventral surface of the medulla, near the rhythm centre | Highly sensitive to **CO₂ and H⁺** (in the cerebrospinal fluid); **not to O₂** |
| **Peripheral chemoreceptors** — **carotid body** (glossopharyngeal nerve IX) and **aortic body** (vagus X) | At the bifurcation of the common carotid and on the aortic arch | Mainly to **pO₂ ↓** (< ~60 mm Hg); also to CO₂ ↑ and H⁺ ↑ |
| **Hering–Breuer reflex** | Stretch receptors of the lungs → vagus | Prevents over-inflation |

> **Exam angle:** Under normal conditions the strongest stimulus for breathing is **blood CO₂ (as H⁺)**, not O₂ (NCERT: "the role of O₂ is quite insignificant"). Lack of O₂ is sensed only by the peripheral chemoreceptors, and only when it is severe.

### What happens at high altitude

1. Atmospheric pO₂ falls (pressure falls; the O₂ percentage stays at 21%) → arterial pO₂ ↓ (**hypoxia**).
2. The **carotid and aortic bodies** sense the fall in pO₂ and signal the medulla → **hyperventilation**.
3. Hyperventilation removes more CO₂ → pCO₂ ↓ → pH ↑ (**respiratory alkalosis**) → ODC initially shifts left.
4. **Acclimatization (a few days to weeks):** 2,3-BPG ↑ in RBCs (ODC shifts right — O₂ released to the tissues), the kidneys excrete HCO₃⁻ to correct pH, **erythropoietin** ↑ → RBCs ↑ (**polycythaemia**), capillaries and mitochondria ↑.
5. Without acclimatization — **mountain sickness**: headache, nausea; severe forms — HAPE (pulmonary oedema), HACE (cerebral oedema).

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** On rapid ascent to high altitude, hyperventilation occurs and oxygen transport changes. Which explanation links regulation of breathing to the changes in gas transport?
> (A) Central chemoreceptors directly sense decreased oxygen and increase ventilation, while CO₂ transport remains unchanged
> (B) Carotid and aortic chemoreceptors sense low oxygen tension and stimulate ventilation; the reduced CO₂ alters pH and haemoglobin affinity
> (C) Peripheral chemoreceptors sense increased oxygen and suppress ventilation, while affinity decreases for release in the lungs
> (D) Altitude does not affect regulation of breathing; transport changes only after Hb concentration rises
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(B)** — lack of O₂ is sensed by the peripheral (carotid/aortic) chemoreceptors, not the central ones; hyperventilation lowers pCO₂, raises pH, and affinity rises (the reverse of the Bohr effect).

## 9. Respiratory disorders

| Disorder | Description |
|---|---|
| Asthma | Inflammation and spasm of the bronchioles (often type-I hypersensitivity) → wheezing |
| Emphysema | Alveolar walls destroyed → surface area ↓; main cause is smoking |
| Bronchitis | Inflammation of the bronchi, cough with sputum |
| Occupational diseases | Silicosis (stone quarries), asbestosis, anthracosis (coal — "black lung"), byssinosis (cotton) — pulmonary fibrosis |
| Pneumonia | Fluid in the alveoli (*Streptococcus pneumoniae*) |
| CO poisoning | Carboxyhaemoglobin, cherry-red colour |

**Types of hypoxia:** hypoxic (high altitude — arterial pO₂ ↓), anaemic (Hb ↓, CO), stagnant/circulatory (slow blood flow, heart failure), histotoxic (**cyanide** blocks cytochrome oxidase; enough O₂ in the blood but it cannot be used).

## 10. Cellular respiration and metabolism

**Metabolism** = **anabolism** (building up, energy-consuming) + **catabolism** (breaking down, energy-releasing). Cellular respiration is catabolic.

| Stage | Site | Key points | Net yield (per glucose) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glycolysis (EMP pathway** — Embden, Meyerhof, Parnas) | Cytoplasm | Glucose (6C) → 2 pyruvate (3C); 10 steps; O₂ not needed; rate-limiting enzyme **phosphofructokinase-1** | 2 ATP (4 made − 2 used) + 2 NADH |
| **Link reaction** | Mitochondrial matrix | Pyruvate → acetyl-CoA + CO₂; **pyruvate dehydrogenase complex** (5 cofactors: TPP, lipoic acid, CoA, FAD, NAD⁺) | 2 NADH + 2 CO₂ |
| **Krebs cycle / TCA / citric acid cycle** (Hans Krebs, 1937) | Mitochondrial matrix | Acetyl-CoA (2C) + oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C); 2 decarboxylations | 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, 2 GTP/ATP, 4 CO₂ |
| **Electron transport system (ETS) + oxidative phosphorylation** | **Inner membrane** of the mitochondria | Complexes I–IV, final electron acceptor **O₂** (→ H₂O); **ATP synthase (complex V, F₀–F₁)**; **chemiosmotic theory (Peter Mitchell, Nobel 1978)** | NADH ≈ 2.5 ATP, FADH₂ ≈ 1.5 ATP (new values) |

<div class="formula">

**ATP balance sheet (per glucose, aerobic):** old/NCERT values — **38 ATP** (prokaryotes) / 36 (eukaryotes, glycerol-phosphate shuttle); new values (P/O ratios 2.5 and 1.5) — **~30–32 ATP**.

**Anaerobic (fermentation):** only **2 ATP** per glucose.

</div>

> **Common mistake:** NCERT gives 38 ATP but also says this is "theoretical". If a question says "latest estimate", choose 30–32.

**Components of the ETS (in order):** NADH → complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) → **ubiquinone (CoQ)** → complex III (cytochrome bc₁) → **cytochrome c** (mobile carrier) → complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase, contains Cu and Fe) → O₂. FADH₂ enters CoQ through complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) — hence less ATP.

**ETS inhibitors (B.Sc.):** rotenone and amytal — complex I; antimycin A — complex III; **cyanide, CO, azide** — complex IV; oligomycin — ATP synthase; **2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP)** and **thermogenin (UCP-1, brown fat)** — **uncouplers**: energy becomes heat, not ATP — heat production in hibernation and in newborns.

### Fermentation

| Type | Reaction | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Lactic acid fermentation | Pyruvate → **lactate** (lactate dehydrogenase; NADH → NAD⁺) | Skeletal muscle in strenuous exercise, RBCs (no mitochondria), *Lactobacillus* |
| Alcoholic fermentation | Pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO₂ → **ethanol** | Yeast, some bacteria |

Lactate formed in muscle is converted to glucose in the liver and returned — the **Cori cycle** (details in [Movement and Locomotion](/interview/up-pgt/jeev-vigyan/jantu-2/gati-aur-chalan)).

### Respiratory Quotient (RQ)

RQ = volume of CO₂ released ÷ volume of O₂ consumed

| Substrate | RQ |
|---|---|
| Carbohydrate (glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O) | **1.0** |
| Fat (tripalmitin) | **~0.7** |
| Protein | **~0.8–0.9** |
| Organic acids (oxalic, malic) | **> 1** (oxalic = 4) |
| Anaerobic respiration | **∞** (O₂ zero) |
| Conversion of fat to carbohydrate (germinating oilseeds) / mixed diet | < 1 / ~0.85 |

### Other metabolic pathways (B.Sc. level)

- **Glycogenesis** (glucose → glycogen, insulin), **glycogenolysis** (glycogen → glucose, glucagon/adrenaline), **gluconeogenesis** (amino acids, lactate, glycerol → glucose; mainly liver and renal cortex; increased by cortisol).
- **Pentose phosphate pathway (HMP shunt):** NADPH (fatty acid synthesis, glutathione in RBCs) and ribose-5-phosphate; G6PD deficiency → haemolytic anaemia.
- **β-oxidation of fatty acids:** in mitochondria (entry by the carnitine shuttle); each cycle gives 2C (acetyl-CoA) + 1 NADH + 1 FADH₂; palmitate (16C) gives **7 cycles, 8 acetyl-CoA**, net ~**106 ATP** (new values; old 129).
- **Ketone bodies:** acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, acetone — made in the liver during fasting/uncontrolled diabetes; **ketosis**.
- **Protein catabolism:** **transamination** (coenzyme PLP — vitamin B₆; ALT/AST) and **oxidative deamination** (glutamate dehydrogenase) → NH₃ → urea cycle (Chapter 04).
- **Amphibolic pathway:** the Krebs cycle takes part in both catabolism and anabolism (its intermediates are used for amino acids, haem, etc.).

## Traps and exceptions

- **Expiration is normally passive**; only forced expiration is active.
- **Central chemoreceptors do not sense O₂** — they sense CO₂/H⁺; lack of O₂ is sensed by peripheral chemoreceptors.
- **The pneumotaxic centre is in the pons**, the rhythm centre in the medulla.
- **The CO₂ gradient is only 5 mm Hg**, yet diffusion is fast — solubility is 20–25 times higher.
- **Most CO₂ is carried as bicarbonate** (~70%), not in the carbamino form.
- **In carbaminohaemoglobin CO₂ binds the amino groups of globin**, not the iron of haem; CO binds the iron of haem.
- **HbF shifts the curve to the left**, 2,3-BPG to the right.
- **The myoglobin curve is hyperbolic**, not sigmoid.
- **Birds have no alveoli** — parabronchi and air capillaries; no exchange in the air sacs.
- **RBCs have no mitochondria** — they make ATP only by glycolysis and do not use the O₂ they carry.
- **A spirometer cannot measure RV.**
- **The Krebs cycle directly makes only 1 ATP (GTP) per turn**; most ATP comes from the ETS.
- **The cartilage rings of the trachea are C-shaped** (open at the back — they let the oesophagus expand).

## Practice questions (with answers)

1. What is the strongest chemical stimulus for normal breathing? — **Rise in blood CO₂ / H⁺** (central chemoreceptors).
2. Vital capacity is the sum of? — **TV + IRV + ERV**.
3. Which volume cannot be measured with a spirometer? — **Residual volume (RV)**.
4. Which cell makes surfactant? — **Type-II alveolar cell (pneumocyte)**.
5. In the Bohr effect, which way does the ODC shift? — **To the right** (affinity decreases).
6. Why does foetal haemoglobin have higher affinity? — **It binds 2,3-BPG poorly** (γ-chains).
7. Enzyme in RBCs that converts CO₂ to H₂CO₃? — **Carbonic anhydrase** (contains Zn).
8. By what other name is the chloride shift known? — **Hamburger shift**.
9. What is the Haldane effect? — **Binding of O₂ lowers the CO₂ affinity of Hb** (deoxy Hb carries more CO₂).
10. Where is the pneumotaxic centre? — In the **pons**.
11. Who senses hypoxia at high altitude? — **Carotid and aortic bodies** (peripheral chemoreceptors).
12. What is the pO₂ in the alveoli? — **104 mm Hg**.
13. In what form is most CO₂ carried? — **Bicarbonate (~70%)**.
14. What kind of airflow do birds have? — **Unidirectional, in the parabronchi**.
15. RQ of fat? — **About 0.7**.
16. What type of hypoxia does cyanide cause? — **Histotoxic** — inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase.
17. Who proposed the chemiosmotic theory? — **Peter Mitchell** (Nobel 1978).
18. Rate-limiting enzyme of glycolysis? — **Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1)**.
19. Protein for heat production in brown fat? — **Thermogenin (UCP-1)**, an uncoupler.
20. How many cycles in the β-oxidation of palmitic acid? — **7 cycles, 8 acetyl-CoA**.
