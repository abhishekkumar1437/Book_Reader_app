The syllabus line "Hormones and pheromones; vitamins" starts from NCERT 11's "Chemical Coordination and Integration" and goes up to B.Sc./M.Sc. level — mechanism of hormone action, classes of pheromones, insect endocrinology and the coenzyme forms of vitamins. UP PGT 2026 asked about glucocorticoids and hCG, and also three questions on animal behaviour (innate behaviour, the sequence of bird migration, integration of migration–communication–instinct), so a section on behaviour has been added here.

> **In simple words:** Hormones are chemical messengers that travel from a gland into the blood and act on distant organs of **the same organism**. Pheromones are chemicals released outside the body that change the behaviour or physiology of **other members of the same species** — for example, the male silkworm moth being attracted by the scent of the female. Vitamins are organic nutrients needed in small amounts; most of them form coenzymes, and the body cannot make them in sufficient quantity.

For depth: [Endocrine System and Hormones](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/physiology/endocrine-system-and-hormones).

## 1. Hormones — definition and chemical nature

- The word "hormone" — **E.H. Starling, 1905**; the first hormone discovered was **secretin** — **Bayliss & Starling, 1902**.
- Father of endocrinology — **Thomas Addison** (described Addison's disease); some books regard Berthold (1849, testis transplant in the cockerel) as the first experimenter.
- NCERT definition: hormones are non-nutrient chemicals which act as intercellular messengers and are produced in trace amounts.

| Chemical class | Examples | Solubility / receptor |
|---|---|---|
| Peptide/protein | Insulin, glucagon, pituitary hormones, ADH, oxytocin, PTH, calcitonin, hCG | Water-soluble → membrane receptor |
| Steroid (from cholesterol) | Cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone, ecdysone, calcitriol | Lipid-soluble → intracellular (nuclear/cytoplasmic) receptor |
| Amino acid derivative (iodothyronines) | T₃, T₄ (from tyrosine) | Behave like lipid-soluble → nuclear receptor |
| Amino acid derivative (catecholamines) | Adrenaline, noradrenaline (from tyrosine), melatonin (from tryptophan) | Water-soluble → membrane receptor |
| Eicosanoids (semi-local) | Prostaglandins (from arachidonic acid) | Local hormones |

> **Remember:** Thyroxine is an amino-acid derivative but acts through a **nuclear receptor** — a favourite trap in statement questions. Adrenaline is also derived from tyrosine but acts through membrane receptors (α, β adrenergic).

## 2. Mechanism of hormone action

**(a) Membrane receptor — second messenger:**

1. The hormone (first messenger) binds to a G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) in the membrane.
2. Gs-protein (GTP-bound α subunit) → **adenylate cyclase** activated → ATP → **cAMP**.
3. cAMP → **protein kinase A (PKA)** activated → phosphorylation of target proteins → cellular response.
4. Phosphodiesterase breaks cAMP into AMP and ends the signal (caffeine inhibits PDE and raises cAMP).
5. **Amplification** — one hormone molecule causes thousands of molecules to be activated.

- Discovery of cAMP: **Earl W. Sutherland** — working on glycogen breakdown in liver by adrenaline; **Nobel 1971**.
- Other second messengers: **IP₃** and **DAG** (phospholipase C pathway; IP₃ → Ca²⁺ from ER; DAG → protein kinase C), **Ca²⁺–calmodulin**, **cGMP** (ANP, nitric oxide).
- The receptor of **insulin** is a **tyrosine kinase** (not a GPCR).
- Discovery of G-proteins: **Gilman and Rodbell**, Nobel 1994.

**(b) Intracellular receptor (steroid, thyroid):** the hormone crosses the membrane → binds a receptor in the cytoplasm/nucleus → the hormone–receptor complex binds the **hormone response element (HRE)** on DNA → gene expression (mRNA → protein) changes. The effect is slow but long-lasting.

**Feedback:** mostly negative — e.g. when T₃/T₄ rise, TRH and TSH fall; when cortisol rises, CRH and ACTH fall. Examples of **positive feedback** — oxytocin during childbirth (Ferguson reflex), oestrogen before ovulation → LH surge.

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** Which statement correctly describes the action of glucocorticoids during stress?
> (A) They convert proteins into glucose through the liver and kidneys
> (B) They directly increase insulin secretion
> (C) They increase glycogen storage in muscles
> (D) They inhibit all glucose-synthesis pathways
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(A)** — cortisol breaks down muscle protein and sends amino acids to the liver (and kidney), where **gluconeogenesis** takes place; it is an insulin antagonist.

**Stress response:** immediate — sympathetic system + adrenal medulla (adrenaline: glycogenolysis, heart rate); long-term — **HPA axis** (CRH → ACTH → cortisol): gluconeogenesis, lipolysis, immunosuppression. Hans Selye — **General Adaptation Syndrome**: Alarm → Resistance → Exhaustion.

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** The main function of hCG is —
> (A) to initiate ovulation (B) to maintain the corpus luteum (C) to stop embryonic growth (D) to form the placenta
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(B)** — hCG (LH-like) maintains progesterone secretion from the corpus luteum in early pregnancy; it is the basis of the pregnancy test. In detail: [Frog development, embryonic membranes and placenta](/interview/up-pgt/jeev-vigyan/jantu-2/medhak-vikas-bhroon-jhilli-aparaa-punarjanan).

## 4. Invertebrates — insect hormones

| Hormone | Source | Function |
|---|---|---|
| PTTH (prothoracicotropic hormone / "brain hormone") | Neurosecretory cells of the brain; released from corpora cardiaca | Stimulates the prothoracic gland |
| **Ecdysone (moulting hormone)** — steroid; active form 20-hydroxyecdysone | Prothoracic gland | Moulting and metamorphosis; "puffs" on polytene chromosomes |
| **Juvenile hormone (JH, neotenin)** — terpenoid | Corpora allata | Maintains larval characters; high JH → larval moult; low JH + ecdysone → pupa; JH absent → adult; oogenesis in adults |
| Bursicon | Neurosecretory cells | Hardening and darkening of the cuticle after moulting (sclerotisation) |
| Eclosion hormone | Brain | Emergence of the adult from the pupa |

> **Exam angle:** "High JH + ecdysone = larva to larva; low JH + ecdysone = pupa; zero JH + ecdysone = adult." JH analogues (methoprene) are called "third-generation insecticides" in pest control — the idea of Carroll Williams. In crustaceans the **X-organ–sinus gland** of the eyestalk produces moult-inhibiting hormone (MIH), and the **Y-organ** produces ecdysone.

## 5. Pheromones

- Term: **Karlson & Lüscher, 1959** — Greek *pherein* (to carry) + *hormon* (to excite).
- First chemically identified pheromone: **bombykol** — sex attractant of the female silkworm moth *Bombyx mori*; **Adolf Butenandt, 1959** (extracted from about 5 lakh (500,000) female glands). The male detects extremely low concentrations with its feathery (pectinate) antennae.
- **Hormone vs pheromone:** hormone — endocrine, into the blood, acts on the same organism; pheromone — exocrine, released outside, acts on **other** members of the same species.
- Related terms — **semiochemicals** = all signalling chemicals; these include **interspecific allelochemicals** — allomones (benefit the emitter, e.g. the stench of a skunk), kairomones (benefit the receiver, e.g. a parasite recognising the smell of its host), synomones (benefit both, e.g. floral scent and pollinators).

### Two main classes based on function

| Class | Effect | Speed | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Releaser** | Immediate change in **behaviour** (through the nervous system) | Fast, short-lived | Sex attractants, alarm, trail marking |
| **Primer** | Long-term **physiological/endocrine** change | Slow, lasting | Queen substance, Bruce–Whitten effects |

(Some authors also recognise a third class, "signaller", and a fourth, "modulator".)

### Types based on the message

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Sex attractant | Bombykol (*Bombyx mori*); **disparlure** of the gypsy moth; muscalure of the housefly; **androstenone** of the boar (in male saliva; female adopts the "standing" posture) — pest monitoring with pheromone traps |
| Alarm | **Isopentyl acetate** of the honeybee sting (banana-like smell); (E)-β-farnesene of aphids; **Schreckstoff** of fishes — discovered by Karl von Frisch in minnows |
| Trail | Ants, termites — trail to the food source |
| Aggregation | Bark beetles — mass attack; aggregation pheromone of cockroaches (in faeces) |
| Queen substance | **9-ODA (9-oxo-2-decenoic acid)** from the mandibular gland of the honeybee queen — inhibits ovary development in workers, prevents rearing of new queens (primer) and attracts drones during the mating flight (releaser) |
| Territorial / marking | Urine of dogs and cats, preorbital gland of deer, urine spraying by tigers |
| Species recognition / nest-mate | Colony odour of ants and termites |
| Nasonov | Honeybee workers — gathering the swarm |

### Primer effects in mammals (mostly in mice)

| Effect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Bruce effect** — Hilda Bruce, 1959 | In a newly pregnant female mouse, the smell of an **unfamiliar male** makes implantation fail (abortion/pregnancy block) |
| **Whitten effect** | The urine odour of a male **synchronises** the oestrous cycles of a group of females, with oestrus on about the third day |
| **Lee–Boot effect** | In females housed together without a male, the oestrous cycle is **suppressed**/lengthened (pseudopregnancy/anoestrus) |
| **Vandenbergh effect** | Male odour brings **early puberty** in young females |
| McClintock effect (1971) | Synchronisation of menstrual cycles in women living together — disputed by later studies |

> **Memory trick:** **B**ruce = **B**lock (pregnancy block); **W**hitten = **W**ith male — sync; **L**ee–Boot = **L**adies alone — suppressed; **V**andenbergh = **V**ery early puberty.

**Detection of pheromones:** in mammals (and reptiles), the **Jacobson's organ / vomeronasal organ (VNO)** — at the base of the nasal septum; signals go to the **accessory olfactory bulb** → amygdala → hypothalamus. Snakes flick their tongue to carry odour particles to the VNO. The **Flehmen behaviour** of horses/cats (curling the upper lip) is to carry odour to the VNO. In adult humans the VNO is regarded as vestigial.

## 6. Vitamins

The word "vitamine" — **Casimir Funk, 1912**; work on beri-beri — **Christiaan Eijkman** and Hopkins (Nobel 1929).

| Vitamin | Chemical name | Active/coenzyme form and function | Deficiency disease | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Retinol | Retinal — rhodopsin; epithelial health | Night blindness, xerophthalmia, Bitot's spots, keratomalacia | Carrot (β-carotene), liver, fish oil |
| **D** | Calciferol (D₃ cholecalciferol) | 1,25-dihydroxy D₃ (calcitriol) — a hormone; intestinal Ca²⁺ absorption; made in skin by UV-B | Rickets (children), osteomalacia (adults) | Sunlight, fish oil |
| **E** | Tocopherol | Antioxidant (membrane lipids) | Sterility in rats, RBC breakdown (haemolysis) | Vegetable oils, wheat germ |
| **K** | Phylloquinone (K₁), menaquinone (K₂) | γ-carboxylation of clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X) | Failure of blood clotting (bleeding in newborns) | Green leaves, gut bacteria |
| **B₁** | Thiamine | **TPP** (thiamine pyrophosphate) — pyruvate dehydrogenase, transketolase | **Beri-beri**, Wernicke–Korsakoff | Cereal husk, yeast |
| **B₂** | Riboflavin | **FAD, FMN** — oxidation-reduction | Angular stomatitis (cheilosis), glossitis | Milk, egg |
| **B₃** | Niacin (nicotinic acid) | **NAD⁺, NADP⁺** | **Pellagra** (4D — dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia, death); also made from tryptophan | Groundnut, meat |
| **B₅** | Pantothenic acid | **Coenzyme A (CoA)** | Burning feet syndrome | Almost all foods |
| **B₆** | Pyridoxine | **PLP** (pyridoxal phosphate) — transamination, decarboxylation | Convulsions, peripheral neuritis, anaemia | Cereals, meat |
| **B₇** | Biotin (vitamin H) | Carboxylation (pyruvate carboxylase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase) | Dermatitis; **avidin** of raw egg binds it | Liver, gut bacteria |
| **B₉** | Folic acid | **THF** (tetrahydrofolate) — one-carbon transfer, DNA synthesis | **Megaloblastic anaemia**, neural tube defects in the foetus | Green leaves |
| **B₁₂** | Cyanocobalamin (contains **cobalt**) | Methylcobalamin, deoxyadenosylcobalamin | **Pernicious anaemia** — due to lack of gastric **intrinsic factor**; nerve damage | Only animal foods and bacteria |
| **C** | Ascorbic acid | Collagen synthesis (prolyl hydroxylase), antioxidant, iron absorption | **Scurvy** (bleeding gums) | Amla (richest), citrus fruits |

> **Remember:** Fat-soluble **A, D, E, K** (stored in liver/fat; excess is toxic — hypervitaminosis A, D); the rest are water-soluble (lost in urine; B₁₂ is the exception, stored in the liver for years). Humans, guinea pigs, apes and fruit bats cannot make vitamin C (lack of gulonolactone oxidase). The only vitamin containing a metal — B₁₂ (Co).

## 7. Animal Behaviour

**Ethology** = the study of behaviour in the natural environment. Founders **Konrad Lorenz, Niko Tinbergen, Karl von Frisch** — **Nobel 1973** (Physiology or Medicine).

**Tinbergen's four questions:** causation (mechanism), development (ontogeny), function/adaptive value, evolutionary history (phylogeny). Proximate cause vs ultimate cause.

### (a) Innate behaviour

Genetically programmed, nearly **uniform (stereotyped)** in all members of the species, correct the very first time without experience.

| Type | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Taxis | Oriented movement **towards** (+) or away from (−) the **direction** of the stimulus | Positive phototaxis of *Euglena*; chemotaxis of sperm; negative phototaxis of cockroach |
| Kinesis | **Non-directional** — the intensity of the stimulus changes the **rate** of movement or rate of turning, not the direction | Woodlice (*Porcellio*) move fast in dry places and slowly in moist places — so they collect in moist places |
| Reflex | Automatic response of a body part to a single stimulus | Knee jerk, blinking |
| Instinct | Complex, chain-like behaviour without learning | Nest building, spider web, migration, courtship dance |
| Fixed Action Pattern (FAP) | Started by a **sign stimulus (releaser)**; once started it runs to completion | Egg-rolling by the greylag goose (completes the action even if the egg is removed — Lorenz and Tinbergen); male **stickleback** fish attacks any model with a red belly (Tinbergen); herring gull chick pecking at the red spot on the parent's beak |

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** Which statement describes the characteristic of innate behaviour?
> (A) Behaviour acquired by trial-and-error learning
> (B) Behaviour that is genetically programmed and expressed uniformly among individuals
> (C) Behaviour showing random variation among individuals without a pattern
> (D) Behaviour that changes continuously with experience and environment
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(B)**.

### (b) Learned (acquired) behaviour

| Type | Description | Examples / scientist |
|---|---|---|
| Habituation | Decline in response to a repeated harmless stimulus — the simplest learning | Snail stops withdrawing after repeated touching; *Aplysia* (Kandel, Nobel 2000) |
| Imprinting | Rapid, almost irreversible learning during a **sensitive/critical period** of life | Goslings/ducklings follow the first moving object after hatching (Lorenz himself) — **Konrad Lorenz**; home-stream odour imprinting in salmon |
| Classical conditioning | Association of a neutral stimulus (bell) + unconditioned stimulus (food) → salivation at the bell alone | **Ivan Pavlov** (dog; Nobel 1904 — for digestion) |
| Operant / instrumental conditioning | The consequence of a behaviour (reward/punishment) changes its frequency; trial and error | **B.F. Skinner** (Skinner box — rat presses a lever); Thorndike (law of effect, puzzle-box cat) |
| Insight learning | Sudden solution of a new problem based on previous experience | **Wolfgang Köhler** — chimpanzee "Sultan" got the banana by stacking boxes/joining sticks |
| Latent learning | Learned without immediate reward, shown later | Exploration of a maze by rats (Tolman) |
| Imitation / social learning | Learning by watching others | Japanese macaque "Imo" washing sweet potatoes; blue tits opening milk-bottle caps |

### (c) Communication

- Channels: visual (peacock's feather display, light flashes of fireflies), auditory (bird song, croaking of frogs — vocal sacs), chemical (pheromones), tactile (antenna contact in the honeybee dance), electrical (electric fishes).
- **Honeybee dance — Karl von Frisch:**
  - **Round dance:** food is near (less than ≈ 50–100 m) — only "food is nearby", no direction.
  - **Waggle dance:** food is far; figure-of-8 shape; on the vertical comb, the **angle of the straight "waggle run" from the direction of gravity = angle of the food from the direction of the sun**; **duration/tempo of the waggle = distance**; odour tells the type of food.
  - Some books also mention an intermediate "sickle dance".

### (d) Courtship, sexual behaviour and parental care (brief)

- **Courtship:** species recognition, mate choice (sexual selection — female choice), endocrine synchronisation; examples — zig-zag dance of the stickleback, peacock's feathers, "penguin dance" of the great crested grebe (Julian Huxley).
- Amphibian examples of parental care (details in Zoology I): Darwin's frog *Rhinoderma darwinii* — tadpoles in the male's vocal sac; Surinam toad *Pipa* — in pockets on the female's back; midwife toad *Alytes* — male carries egg strings on the hind legs.

### (e) Bird Migration — physiology

**Sequence of migration (preparation → flight):**

1. Change in **photoperiod** is sensed by the eyes/deep-brain photoreceptors and the **pineal–melatonin** rhythm.
2. **Hypothalamus–pituitary** activation → GnRH → gonadotropins and gonadal hormones, prolactin, corticosterone, thyroid hormones — the **hormonal readiness** for migration.
3. **Hyperphagia** → fat deposition (subcutaneous and abdominal; some small songbirds store fat up to 50% of body weight or more) — **premigratory fattening**.
4. **Adjustment of metabolic rate and muscle efficiency** — hypertrophy of flight muscle (pectoralis), increase in mitochondria and fat-oxidation enzymes, enlargement of the heart; shrinking of digestive organs (in some species).
5. **Migratory restlessness (Zugunruhe)** — night-time restlessness in caged migratory birds, hopping in the direction of migration (Emlen funnel experiment).
6. **Start of directional flight** — on receiving environmental cues (weather, wind direction).
7. During flight, **mobilisation of stored fat (lipid mobilisation)** — fat is the densest energy store (≈ 9 kcal/g, plus metabolic water); refuelling at "stopovers" along the way.

**Cues for navigation:**

| Cue | Evidence / scientist |
|---|---|
| Sun compass — time-compensation of the sun's position by the internal clock | **Gustav Kramer** — starlings changed orientation when the apparent direction of the sun was shifted with mirrors |
| Star compass — the sky rotating around the Pole Star | **Stephen Emlen** — indigo bunting, planetarium experiment |
| Geomagnetic field (magnetic compass) | **Wolfgang Wiltschko** — European robin; inclination compass; **cryptochrome** in the retina (radical pair) and magnetite in the beak (disputed) |
| Polarised light, landmarks, smell (seabirds), infrasound | — |

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** In long-distance migration, birds show coordinated physiological and behavioural changes. Arrange the sequence of preparation and migratory flight — (I) mobilisation of stored lipids for long energy demand (II) accumulation of fat reserves through hyperphagia (III) start of directional flight using environmental cues (IV) adjustment of metabolic rate and muscle efficiency for long flight.
> (A) II → IV → I → III (B) II → I → IV → III (C) IV → II → I → III (D) I → II → IV → III
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(A)** — "III at the end" is common to all the options in the paper; the logical order is fat accumulation (II) → body adjustment (IV) → lipid mobilisation (I) → directional flight (III). Note — in reality lipid mobilisation happens during flight, so this question is about choosing the "most appropriate" of the given options.

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** A migratory bird uses photoperiod cues for seasonal movement, shows visual and auditory courtship displays and returns to specific breeding sites. Which explanation links communication, sexual behaviour, migration and instinct?
> (A) Instincts are unchangeable, migration is free of hormonal control, courtship displays do not affect reproduction
> (B) Migration is entirely learned, courtship displays have no adaptive value, instinct does not affect site fidelity
> (C) Sexual behaviour is independent of environmental cues, migration depends only on resources, communication is only chemical
> (D) Photoperiod triggers endocrine control of migration, courtship signals help in mate choice, instinct guides orientation and homing
> Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(D)**.

**Types of migration (brief):** latitudinal (north–south; Siberian crane — Keoladeo, now almost vanished), altitudinal (Himalayan birds come down in winter), longitudinal. Arctic tern — longest annual migration (more than ≈ 70,000 km). Bar-tailed godwit — longest non-stop flight (Alaska → New Zealand, ≈ 11,000–13,000 km). Studied by bird ringing/banding — in India by **Salim Ali** and BNHS.

## Traps and exceptions

- ADH and oxytocin are **made** in the hypothalamus; the posterior pituitary only **stores/releases** them.
- Thyroxine looks like a water-soluble amino-acid derivative, but acts through a nuclear receptor.
- The insulin receptor is a tyrosine kinase, not the cAMP pathway.
- Glucocorticoids do not increase insulin — they are insulin antagonists (hyperglycaemic).
- PTH raises blood Ca²⁺, calcitonin lowers it; vitamin D acts like a hormone (steroid).
- A pheromone is an **intraspecific** signal; chemicals acting on other species are allomones/kairomones/synomones.
- Queen substance 9-ODA is both a primer and a releaser.
- Bruce effect = pregnancy block (unfamiliar male); Whitten = synchronisation; Lee–Boot = suppression; Vandenbergh = early puberty.
- Taxis is direction-based; in kinesis there is no direction, only the rate of movement changes.
- Imprinting happens in a critical period and is almost irreversible, yet it is **learned** behaviour (driven by an innate tendency).
- Vitamins D and K are made by gut bacteria/in the skin, yet are still called vitamins; niacin can be made from tryptophan.
- B₁₂ is water-soluble yet stays stored in the liver.

## Practice questions (with answers)

1. Who discovered cAMP as a second messenger? — **Earl Sutherland** (Nobel 1971).
2. Which hormone acts through a nuclear receptor — adrenaline or thyroxine? — **Thyroxine**.
3. What type of receptor is the insulin receptor? — **Tyrosine kinase**.
4. Disease caused by GH excess in adults? — **Acromegaly**.
5. Where is ADH synthesised? — **Hypothalamus** (supraoptic/paraventricular nuclei).
6. Hormone that raises blood Ca²⁺? — **Parathormone** (PTH).
7. Cushing's syndrome is due to excess of? — **Cortisol** (glucocorticoid).
8. Hormone that maintains the larval stage in insects? — **Juvenile hormone** (corpora allata).
9. Bombykol is the pheromone of which animal? — **Silkworm moth *Bombyx mori*** (female sex attractant).
10. Pregnancy block in a mouse due to the smell of an unfamiliar male is called? — **Bruce effect**.
11. Synchronisation of the oestrous cycles of females by male odour? — **Whitten effect**.
12. Queen substance of the honeybee queen? — **9-ODA** (suppresses worker ovaries).
13. Accessory olfactory organ that detects pheromones? — **Jacobson's (vomeronasal) organ**.
14. NAD⁺ is made from which vitamin? — **Niacin (B₃)**.
15. Pernicious anaemia is due to deficiency of? — **B₁₂** (lack of intrinsic factor).
16. Avidin of raw egg binds which vitamin? — **Biotin (B₇)**.
17. Who demonstrated imprinting in ducklings? — **Konrad Lorenz**.
18. The red belly of the stickleback fish is the sign stimulus for which behaviour? — **Fixed action pattern (attack)** (Tinbergen).
19. In the waggle dance, the distance of food is indicated by? — **Duration of the waggle run** (direction by the angle).
20. What is the night-time restlessness of a caged migratory bird called? — **Zugunruhe**.
