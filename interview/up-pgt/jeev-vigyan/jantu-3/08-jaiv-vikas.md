This chapter takes Unit 11 "Organic Evolution" — **concept, origin of life, Lamarck, Darwin, evidences, Neo-Darwinism and the synthetic theory, variations and human evolution** — from NCERT 12 ("Evolution") up to B.Sc./M.Sc. level (Hardy-Weinberg calculations, types of speciation, megaevolution). The 2026 paper had **5 questions** from this unit — homologous organs, insecticide resistance, macro- versus megaevolution, Kettlewell's data and the biological species concept — making it the highest-scoring theory section of Zoology.

> **In simple words:** Life on Earth arose about 4 billion years ago from simple chemicals and slowly changed into the diversity we see today. Darwin explained that the members of every population differ slightly from one another; the differences that help an organism survive in its environment and leave more offspring keep increasing over generations (natural selection). Today we know that these differences come from mutation and recombination, and that evolution means a change in gene frequencies in a population.

## 1. Origin of life

| Fact | Value (NCERT/modern) |
|---|---|
| Age of the universe (Big Bang) | ≈ **13.7–13.8 billion years** (NCERT: 20 billion — an old estimate) |
| Age of the Earth | ≈ **4.5–4.6 billion years** |
| Appearance of life | ≈ **500 million years after** the Earth formed, about **4 billion years ago** (NCERT); oldest fossils — **stromatolites** ≈ 3.5 billion years |
| Early atmosphere | **Reducing** — CH₄, NH₃, H₂, water vapour, CO₂; **no free O₂** |
| Accumulation of O₂ | From photosynthesis by cyanobacteria — the "**Great Oxidation Event**" ≈ 2.4 billion years ago |

### 1.1 Theories

| Theory | Main idea / scientist |
|---|---|
| **Special creation** | God created all organisms at one time, unchangeable; Earth ≈ 4000 years old — rejected by the evidences of evolution |
| **Spontaneous generation / Abiogenesis** | Life from non-living matter (rotting meat, mud) — Aristotle; **van Helmont** (dirty clothes + wheat → mice) |
| Disproof of spontaneous generation | **Francesco Redi (1668)** — no fly maggots in covered meat; **Spallanzani (1767)** — sealing boiled broth; **Louis Pasteur (1859–62)** — **swan-neck flask** — "life comes only from pre-existing life" (**Biogenesis**) |
| **Panspermia / Cosmozoic theory** | Seeds of life (spores) came from outer space — **Arrhenius**; some astronomers still hold it |
| **Chemical evolution** | **A. I. Oparin (Russia, 1924)** and **J. B. S. Haldane (Britain, 1929)** — reducing atmosphere; energy (UV, lightning, volcanic heat) turned simple inorganic → organic molecules → polymers → **pre-cells**; the sea = "**hot dilute soup**" (Haldane) |

- **Oparin** — **coacervates** (colloidal droplets); **Sidney Fox** — **microspheres** (from proteinoids); these are called **protobionts / progenotes**.
- **Stanley Miller and Harold Urey (1953)** — **CH₄, NH₃, H₂ and water vapour** in a closed flask; at **800°C** according to NCERT; **electric spark discharge** → **amino acids** (glycine, alanine, aspartic acid, etc.) formed. Other experiments also produced sugars, nitrogenous bases, pigments and fats. (Amino acids were also found in a meteorite — Murchison, 1969.)
- **Sequence:** inorganic → simple organic → complex polymers (proteins, nucleic acids) → **RNA world** (self-replicating ribozymes; term coined by **Walter Gilbert, 1986**) → membrane-bound pre-cell → **prokaryotic cell** (anaerobic, heterotrophic — "heterotroph hypothesis") → chemo/photoautotrophs → O₂ → aerobes → eukaryotes (≈ 1.5–2 billion years ago, endosymbiosis) → multicellular organisms.

## 2. Lamarckism

- **Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744–1829)**, France; book ***Philosophie Zoologique* (1809)**; the word "Biology" (1802 — independently along with Treviranus); the word "**invertebrate**".
- **Main principles:** (1) an internal tendency of organisms to increase in size; (2) **new needs** → new organs; (3) **use and disuse** — organs develop with use and degenerate with disuse; (4) **inheritance of acquired characters**.
- **Examples:** **long neck of the giraffe** (from the effort to eat high leaves), loss of legs in snakes, webbed feet of water birds, strong arms of a blacksmith (not inherited — a criticism).
- **Disproof — August Weismann:** cut the tails of mice for about **20–22 generations** (books give both numbers) — still the young had tails; **Germplasm theory (1892)** — only changes in the **germ cells (germplasm)** are inherited, not those in the **somatoplasm**. Other examples — pierced ears and noses of Indian women, circumcision.
- **Neo-Lamarckism** — Kammerer (midwife toad — later accused of fraud), McDougall (learning in rats), **Castle-Phillips**; some call modern **epigenetic inheritance** "Lamarck-like" (but it is limited).

## 3. Darwinism — theory of natural selection

- **Charles Robert Darwin (1809–1882)**; voyage of **HMS Beagle** **1831–1836** (South America, **Galapagos Islands** 1835, Australia); book "***On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection***" — **24 November 1859**.
- **Alfred Russel Wallace** — reached the same conclusion independently in the **Malay Archipelago**; joint Darwin-Wallace paper at the **Linnean Society**, London, in **1858**. **Wallace line** — boundary between the Asian and Australian fauna.
- Influences: **Thomas Malthus** — "*An Essay on the Principle of Population*" (1798; population grows geometrically, food arithmetically); **Charles Lyell** — "*Principles of Geology*" (Earth is ancient, uniformitarianism); artificial selection (pigeon breeding).
- "**Survival of the fittest**" — a phrase of **Herbert Spencer** (Darwin adopted it in later editions).
- **Other books:** *The Descent of Man* (1871), *The Variation of Animals and Plants under Domestication* (1868) — containing the **pangenesis** hypothesis of heredity ("gemmules"), which turned out to be wrong.

**Elements of Darwinism:**
1. **Overproduction (prodigality / geometric increase)** — even a slow breeder like the elephant can theoretically increase enormously in number.
2. **Limited resources → struggle for existence** — intraspecific, interspecific, environmental.
3. **Variations** — no two organisms are alike; some are heritable.
4. **Natural selection / survival of the fittest** — those with favourable variations survive more and **leave more offspring** — **"fitness" = reproductive fitness** (NCERT).
5. **Origin of new species** — by gradual accumulation of favourable variations.

**Shortcomings of Darwinism:** did not explain the **source** of variations or the **mechanism of inheritance** (Mendel's work was unknown); vestigial organs and over-specialisation (the huge antlers of the Irish elk) were hard to explain; did not explain the "arrival of the fittest" — only its survival.

## 4. Mutation theory — Hugo de Vries (1901)

- Sudden large changes in ***Oenothera lamarckiana*** (evening primrose) — "**mutations**"; new species arise in **a single jump (saltation)**, not by accumulation of small variations.
- NCERT: Darwin's variations are **small and directional**; de Vries' mutations are **random and directionless**.
- Criticism — the changes in *Oenothera* were mainly chromosomal aberrations; most mutations are harmful/ineffective.

## 5. Evidences of evolution

### 5.1 Comparative morphology and anatomy

| Evidence | Meaning | Examples | Type of evolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Homologous organs** | **Same basic structure and origin**, different function | Flipper of whale, wing of bat, forelimb of cheetah, arm of human (humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges); heart and brain of vertebrates; in plants the thorn of *Bougainvillea* and the tendril of *Cucurbita* (both stem modifications) | **Divergent evolution** — evidence of **common ancestry** |
| **Analogous organs** | **Same function**, different structure and origin | Wings of butterfly and bird; eye of octopus and mammal; flippers of penguin and dolphin; sweet potato (root) and potato (stem) | **Convergent evolution** — adaptation to similar habitats; **not** evidence of common ancestry |
| **Vestigial organs** | Functional in ancestors, now rudimentary and non-functional | In humans the **vermiform appendix**, **coccyx**, **nictitating membrane (plica semilunaris)**, **wisdom teeth**, ear-pinna muscles, body hair; pelvic remnants of the python; pelvic bone of the whale; wings of flightless birds | — |
| **Atavism** | Sudden reappearance of a lost ancestral trait | Tail in a human baby, extra nipples, dense body hair | — |

### 5.3 Embryology

- **Ernst Haeckel — recapitulation theory / Biogenetic law (1866):** "**Ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny**" — all vertebrate embryos have **pharyngeal gill slits**, a tail and a two-chambered heart.
- **Karl Ernst von Baer** disproved it — embryos never pass through the **adult** stages of other animals; they only resemble their embryos (NCERT). Modern view — **evolutionary developmental biology (Evo-Devo)**, conservation of **Hox genes**.
- Tadpole of the frog (fish-like), larva of *Herdmania* (chordate features) — clues to evolutionary relationships.

### 5.4 Palaeontology

- **Fossils** — remains/impressions of past organisms in rock layers; lower layer = older; **radioactive dating** — **¹⁴C (half-life ≈ 5730 years; useful up to ≈ 50,000 years)**, **uranium-lead** (U-238 → Pb-206, half-life 4.5 billion years), potassium-argon (K-40 → Ar-40) — **Libby** (¹⁴C, Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1960).
- **Evolution of the horse** (North America, Cenozoic, ≈ 60 million years): ***Eohippus* (Hyracotherium, Eocene, fox-sized, 4 toes on the forelimb, 3 on the hindlimb)** → ***Mesohippus*** (Oligocene, 3 toes) → ***Merychippus*** (Miocene, 3 toes, middle one large; grazing teeth) → ***Pliohippus*** (Pliocene, **one toe** — the first one-hoofed horse) → ***Equus*** (Pleistocene — modern). Trends — increase in size, reduction of toes, high-crowned teeth (hypsodont), longer legs.
- **Dollo's law (1893)** — evolution is **irreversible**; **Cope's rule** — tendency for body size to increase along a lineage; **Williston's law** — the number of repeated parts decreases and their specialisation increases.
- **Birbal Sahni** — father of Indian palaeobotany (BSIP Lucknow, 1946).

### 5.5 Biogeographical, biochemical and molecular evidence

- **Adaptive radiation** — varied forms in different habitats from one ancestor (divergent evolution): **Darwin's finches** (Galapagos — beaks for eating seeds, insects, cactus), **Australian marsupials** (NCERT); when more than one adaptive radiation occurs, convergence produces similar forms in **placental mammals and marsupials** (wolf–Tasmanian wolf, flying squirrel–flying phalanger) — **convergent evolution**.
- **Continental drift** — Pangaea → Gondwana/Laurasia (Wegener, 1912) — confinement of marsupials to Australia, distribution of lungfishes.
- **Molecular evidence** — **cytochrome c** (identical in human and chimpanzee; 1 difference between human and rhesus; ≈ 44 differences between human and yeast), haemoglobin, universal genetic code, ATP, DNA sequences; **molecular clock** (Zuckerkandl and Pauling, 1962); **precipitin test (Nuttall, 1904)** — antibodies against human serum react most strongly with chimpanzee serum.
- **Artificial selection** — dog breeds, the cabbage family (cabbage, cauliflower, broccoli, kohlrabi from *Brassica oleracea*) — large changes in a short time through human selection (NCERT).

### 5.6 Direct evidence of natural selection — Industrial melanism

- **Peppered moth *Biston betularia*** — in England (Manchester) before the Industrial Revolution (before 1850) **white (light)** moths were more common; pollution blackened tree trunks with soot and **destroyed lichens** → dark (**melanic, *carbonaria***) moths stayed hidden while white moths were visible to birds → **dark moths became more common**. After the Clean Air Act (1956) white moths increased again.
- **Bernard Kettlewell (1950s)** — **mark-release-recapture** experiments — in polluted (Birmingham) and unpolluted (Dorset) woods.
- Conclusion (NCERT): **"No variant is completely superior — fitness depends on the environment"**; lichens are **indicators of air pollution (SO₂)**.

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** Bernard Kettlewell's experimental observations: polluted area — 19% light moths recaptured, 40% dark moths recaptured; unpolluted area — 12.5% light, 6% dark. The most appropriate conclusion? (A) Fitness is environment-dependent (B) Light moths have a higher mutation rate (C) Dark moths are always fitter than light ones (D) Predation has no effect on survival. — Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(A)**. Note the reversal in the data — in the polluted wood dark moths survived ≈ 2 times more (40 vs 19), in the unpolluted wood light moths ≈ 2 times more (12.5 vs 6); so the "always" in (C) is wrong.

**Other examples:** **antibiotic resistance in bacteria**, **DDT resistance in mosquitoes**, insecticide resistance in insects, herbicide resistance in weeds — NCERT: "**evolution by anthropogenic action**"; the mutations behind these are **already present**, the chemical only **selects** them (Lederberg's replica plating, Luria-Delbrück).

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** When an insect population is exposed to an insecticide, resistant individuals increase over generations despite the initially low frequency of the resistance allele. The best explanation? (A) Directed mutations induced by the insecticide (B) Selection acting on pre-existing resistance alleles (C) Genetic drift in a large population (D) Removal of resistant individuals after migration. — Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(B)** — (A) is Lamarckian thinking; genetic drift is effective in a **small** population, not a large one.

## 6. Neo-Darwinism and the modern synthetic theory

- **Neo-Darwinism** — originally the Darwinism of **Weismann** and others (without Lamarckism, with the germplasm idea); later the term was also used for the modern synthesis.
- **Modern synthetic theory (1930–1950)** — a synthesis of Darwin's natural selection with Mendelian and **population genetics**. Main contributors: **R. A. Fisher** (*The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection*, 1930), **J. B. S. Haldane**, **Sewall Wright** (genetic drift, adaptive landscape), **Theodosius Dobzhansky** (*Genetics and the Origin of Species*, **1937**; "Nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution"), **Ernst Mayr** (*Systematics and the Origin of Species*, 1942 — biological species concept), **Julian Huxley** (*Evolution: The Modern Synthesis*, **1942** — the name comes from here), **G. G. Simpson** (palaeontology, *Tempo and Mode in Evolution*, 1944), **G. L. Stebbins** (plants).
- **Five factors of evolution (NCERT — those that disturb Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium):** (1) **gene migration/gene flow**, (2) **genetic drift**, (3) **mutation**, (4) **genetic recombination** (during gametogenesis), (5) **natural selection**. In addition, **reproductive isolation** makes a new species permanent.
- **Unit of evolution** — the **population**; evolution = **change in the gene frequencies of a population** (gene pool).

### 6.1 Variations

| Basis | Types |
|---|---|
| Cells | **Somatic** — not inherited (acquired); **Germinal** — inherited |
| Nature | **Continuous** — small, gradual (height, skin colour — polygenic; Darwin gave them importance); **Discontinuous** — sudden, large (mutations; "sports") |
| Source | **Mutation** (the **original source** of new variation), **recombination** (crossing over, independent assortment, random fertilisation — the **largest immediate source** of variation), gene flow, polyploidy, horizontal transfer (bacteria) |
| Effect | **Adaptive**, neutral, harmful |

- **Kimura's neutral theory (1968)** — at the molecular level most changes are **selectively neutral** — fixed by drift.

### 6.2 Types of natural selection (NCERT figure)

| Type | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Stabilising** | Individuals with **intermediate** values are favoured; both extremes are removed; curve becomes narrower, peak higher | Human **birth weight** (≈ 3–3.5 kg babies survive best) |
| **Directional** | **One extreme** is favoured; the mean shifts | Industrial melanism, giraffe's neck (Darwinian explanation), antibiotic resistance |
| **Disruptive** | **Both extremes** are favoured, the middle is removed; two peaks → possibility of speciation | African seed-eating finch (*Pyrenestes*) — large and small beaks; marine snails |

- **Sexual selection** — peacock's tail, deer antlers (Darwin, 1871); **balanced polymorphism** — sickle-cell heterozygote advantage (malaria areas).

## 7. Hardy–Weinberg principle (1908)

- **G. H. Hardy** (English mathematician) and **Wilhelm Weinberg** (German physician) — independently.
- **Statement:** in a large, randomly mating population, in the absence of evolutionary forces, **allele and genotype frequencies remain constant from generation to generation** — **genetic equilibrium**.
- **Equations:** **p + q = 1** (alleles); **p² + 2pq + q² = 1** (genotypes) — the binomial expansion of (p + q)². p² = AA, 2pq = Aa, q² = aa.
- **Conditions:** (1) **large population** (no drift), (2) **random mating**, (3) **no mutation**, (4) **no migration/gene flow**, (5) **no natural selection** (all genotypes equally fit).
- Deviation from equilibrium = **a measure of evolution** (NCERT). Equilibrium is reached after just one generation of random mating (for autosomal genes).

### Solved problems

**Problem 1:** In a population 16% of individuals show the recessive phenotype (aa). Find p, q and the heterozygotes.
q² = 0.16 → **q = 0.4**; p = 1 − 0.4 = **0.6**; AA = p² = **0.36 (36%)**; Aa = 2pq = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 = **0.48 (48%)**; check 0.36 + 0.48 + 0.16 = 1 — correct

**Problem 2:** Albinism occurs in 1 in 10,000. Frequency of carriers?
q² = 1/10,000 → q = **1/100 = 0.01**; p = 0.99; 2pq = 2 × 0.99 × 0.01 = **0.0198 ≈ 1/50** — i.e. about one carrier in every ≈ 50; carriers are ≈ **198 times** more common than affected people — that is why harmful recessive alleles stay "hidden" in a population.

**Problem 3:** Among 1000 individuals, AA = 490, Aa = 420, aa = 90. Find p, q; is the population in equilibrium?
p = (2 × 490 + 420) / 2000 = 1400/2000 = **0.7**; q = (2 × 90 + 420)/2000 = 600/2000 = **0.3**. Expected: p² = 0.49 → 490, 2pq = 0.42 → 420, q² = 0.09 → 90 — exactly equal to the observed → **in equilibrium** — correct

**Problem 4:** In a population AA = 0.50, Aa = 0.20, aa = 0.30. In equilibrium?
p = 0.50 + 0.20/2 = **0.60**; q = 0.30 + 0.10 = **0.40**. Expected AA 0.36, Aa 0.48, aa 0.16 — observed is different (fewer heterozygotes) → **not in equilibrium** (probably inbreeding/assortative mating). After one generation of random mating the expected frequencies 0.36 : 0.48 : 0.16 will be reached (p and q stay the same).

**Problem 5 (X-linked):** Colour blindness in men is 8%. Colour blindness and carriers among women?
Males are hemizygous → frequency in males = **q = 0.08**; in females q² = 0.0064 = **0.64%** (NCERT's 0.4% is of the same order); carrier females 2pq = 2 × 0.92 × 0.08 = **0.147 ≈ 14.7%**.

**Problem 6 (multiple alleles — ABO):** Group O is 36%; frequency of Iᴬ is p, of Iᴮ is q, of i is r. Then r² = 0.36 → **r = 0.6**. If group A (p² + 2pr) = 0.45 → p² + 1.2p − 0.45 = 0 → p = [−1.2 + √(1.44 + 1.8)]/2 = (−1.2 + 1.8)/2 = **0.3**; q = 1 − 0.6 − 0.3 = **0.1**; group B = q² + 2qr = 0.01 + 0.12 = **0.13**; AB = 2pq = **0.06**; check 0.36 + 0.45 + 0.13 + 0.06 = 1.00 — correct

## 8. Genetic drift, gene flow and isolation

- **Genetic drift / Sewall Wright effect** — random change in gene frequency by chance in a **small population**; an allele may be completely lost or become **fixed**.
  - **Founder effect** — a few individuals settle in a new place; frequencies differ from the parent population — **Ellis-van Creveld syndrome** (polydactyly, dwarfism) in the **Amish community** (Pennsylvania), porphyria among white South Africans.
  - **Bottleneck effect** — a disaster makes the population very small — **cheetah**, **northern elephant seal**, Asiatic lion (Gir), one-horned rhinoceros — low genetic diversity.
- **Gene flow** — movement of alleles through migration; makes populations **similar** (opposes speciation).
- **Reproductive isolating mechanisms** — classification by **Dobzhansky**:

| Class | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Prezygotic** | **Habitat/ecological** | Different habitats in the same area (water vs land) |
| | **Temporal/seasonal** | Different breeding seasons/flowering times |
| | **Behavioural (ethological)** | Courtship dances, songs, firefly flashes, pheromones |
| | **Mechanical** | Incompatibility of genitalia; flower structure–pollinator |
| | **Gametic** | Sperm-egg recognition proteins (bindin of sea urchin) |
| **Postzygotic** | **Hybrid inviability** | The hybrid embryo dies |
| | **Hybrid sterility** | **Mule** (mare ♀ × donkey ♂; 2n = 63), hinny (horse ♂ × female donkey ♀), liger-tigon |
| | **Hybrid breakdown** | F₁ fertile, F₂ weak/sterile (cotton) |

## 9. Species concepts and speciation

| Species concept | Basis |
|---|---|
| **Morphological / typological** — Linnaeus | Similarity of morphology |
| **Biological species concept** — **Ernst Mayr (1942)** | "**Groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations which are reproductively isolated from other such groups**" — main criterion **reproductive compatibility / reproductive isolation**; not applicable to asexual organisms and fossils |
| Evolutionary (Simpson), phylogenetic (shared derived characters), ecological | — |

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** What is the main criterion of the biological species concept? (A) Morphological similarity (B) Geographical distribution (C) Reproductive compatibility (D) Genetic mutation. — Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(C)** — Mayr's definition is based on the ability to interbreed and reproductive isolation from other groups.

| Speciation | Geographical situation | Process / examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Allopatric** | Populations separated by a **geographical barrier** (mountain, river, sea) | Most common; mutation, drift, different selection → reproductive isolation; Darwin's finches, squirrels on the two sides of the Grand Canyon (*Abert* and *Kaibab*) |
| **Peripatric** | Small peripheral population | Includes the founder effect — Mayr |
| **Parapatric** | Adjacent areas, limited gene flow; gradient | The grass *Anthoxanthum* (metal-tolerant near mines) |
| **Sympatric** | In **the same area** | **Polyploidy** (instantaneous — plants; *Spartina anglica*, *Primula kewensis*), host specificity (apple maggot fly *Rhagoletis*), disruptive selection, cichlid fishes of African lakes |

- **Quantum speciation** — rapid, in a small population (Grant).
- **Pace of speciation:** **Phyletic gradualism** — Darwin; **Punctuated equilibrium** — **Niles Eldredge and Stephen J. Gould (1972)** — sudden rapid change between long stable periods; explains the "gaps" in the fossil record.

### 9.1 Micro-, macro- and megaevolution

| Level | Scope | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Microevolution** | **Within a species**, change in gene frequency at the population level; short term | Industrial melanism, antibiotic resistance |
| **Macroevolution** | **At and above the species level** — **speciation**, changes at genus and family level, **adaptive radiation** | Darwin's finches, cichlid fishes |
| **Megaevolution** | **Origin of higher taxonomic groups (classes, phyla)** — **major structural innovations**, long-term trends | Origin of birds from reptiles, origin of mammals, jaws of vertebrates, the seed habit in plants; term — **G. G. Simpson (1944)** |

(The terms "macroevolution/microevolution" — **Filipchenko (1927)**; **Goldschmidt** — "hopeful monsters", 1940.)

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** Which explanation most accurately distinguishes macroevolution from megaevolution on the basis of scale, processes and evolutionary outcomes? (A) Both are the same process, differing only in the geographical distribution of species (B) Macroevolution is only genetic change within a population over a short time, whereas megaevolution is small morphological change within a species (C) Megaevolution is limited to microevolutionary changes over a few generations, whereas macroevolution is only ecological interactions without genetic change (D) Macroevolution involves large-scale changes such as speciation and adaptive radiation, whereas megaevolution is the origin of higher taxonomic groups through major structural innovations and long-term evolutionary trends. — Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(D)**. Option (B) imposes the definition of microevolution on macroevolution.

## 10. Geological time scale (summary)

| Era | Periods — main events | Start (million years ago, approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| **Precambrian** (Archaean, Proterozoic) | Origin of life, prokaryotes, O₂, eukaryotes; **Ediacaran** biota (≈ 630–540 million years) | from 4600 |
| **Palaeozoic** — "age of invertebrates/fishes" | **Cambrian** (540) — **Cambrian explosion**, nearly all phyla; Ordovician (485) — first vertebrates (jawless fishes); Silurian (440) — first land plants; **Devonian (420) — "age of fishes"**, amphibians; **Carboniferous (360) — coal forests, first reptiles**; Permian (300) — **the largest mass extinction** (≈ 252 million years ago, ≈ 90% of marine species) | 540 |

## 11. Human evolution

- **Taxonomy:** order **Primates** → family **Hominidae** (great apes + humans; in older classification humans only) → genus ***Homo*** → species ***Homo sapiens***. Closest relatives — **chimpanzee and bonobo** (DNA ≈ 98–99% identical); human chromosome 2 = fusion of two ape chromosomes (apes 2n = 48, humans 46).
- **Hominid features:** **bipedal locomotion** (S-shaped vertebral column, bowl-shaped pelvis, foramen magnum placed lower and forward), large brain, opposable thumb, small canines, chin, language, culture.

| Form | Time (approx.) | Cranial capacity | Main facts (NCERT + modern) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***Dryopithecus*** | ≈ 15 million years ago | — | **Ape-like**, hairy, walked like gorillas and chimpanzees (NCERT) |
| ***Ramapithecus*** (now part of *Sivapithecus*) | ≈ 15 million years ago | — | **Man-like** (NCERT); **Shivalik hills** — **G. Edward Lewis (1932)**; now considered related to the orangutan lineage |
| ***Australopithecus*** | ≈ **2 million years ago** (early forms ≈ 4 million) | ≈ 400–500 cc | **East African grasslands**; hunted with stone weapons but **essentially ate fruit** (NCERT); **"Lucy"** — *A. afarensis* (≈ 3.2 million years, Ethiopia, **Donald Johanson, 1974**); **Taung child** — *A. africanus* (**Raymond Dart, 1924**) |
| ***Homo habilis*** ("**handy man**") | ≈ 2 million years ago | **650–800 cc** | First human-like being; stone tools; **probably did not eat meat** (NCERT); **Leakey family** (Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania) |
| ***Homo erectus*** ("upright man") | ≈ **1.5 million years ago** | **≈ 900 cc** | **Ate meat** (NCERT); use of fire; **Java man** (**Eugene Dubois, 1891** — *Pithecanthropus erectus*); **Peking man** (*Sinanthropus pekinensis*, Davidson Black, 1927); first to move out of Africa; *H. ergaster* |
| ***Homo neanderthalensis*** (Neanderthal) | **100,000 – 40,000 years ago** | **≈ 1400 cc** | **East and Central Asia** (NCERT; also Europe); **buried their dead**, tools (Mousterian), clothing of hides; Neander valley, Germany (1856) |
| ***Homo sapiens*** | NCERT: **arose in Africa**, spread across continents during the ice age **75,000–10,000 years** ago; modern: ≈ **300,000 years** (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, 2017) | ≈ 1350–1450 cc (average ≈ 1350) | **Cro-Magnon** (France, 1868) — early modern Europeans; **cave paintings ≈ 18,000 years ago** (NCERT); **agriculture ≈ 10,000 years ago**, human settlements (NCERT) |

- **"Out of Africa" / single-origin model** (mitochondrial Eve — Cann, Stoneking and Wilson, 1987) versus the **multiregional model**; the dominant view today — African origin + limited interbreeding with Neanderthals/Denisovans. **Svante Pääbo** — Neanderthal genome (Nobel Prize in Medicine 2022); non-Africans carry ≈ 1–2% Neanderthal DNA.
- **India:** **Narmada man** — **Hathnora, Madhya Pradesh (1982), Arun Sonakia** — India's oldest hominin skull fossil (*H. erectus narmadensis* / archaic *H. sapiens*); **Bhimbetka** rock shelters (MP, UNESCO) — prehistoric cave paintings.
- **Trends in human evolution:** increase in brain size, bipedalism, smaller jaws and teeth, reduced brow ridge (supraorbital ridge), emergence of the chin, tool culture (Palaeolithic → Mesolithic → Neolithic), language.

> **Memory trick:** "**Dryo-Rama-Australo-Habilis-Erectus-Neander-Sapiens**" — cranial capacity **"650–900–1400"** (habilis, erectus, Neanderthal).

## Traps and exceptions

- **Homologous = divergent, analogous = convergent** — do not reverse them. Sweet potato and potato are **analogous** (NCERT).
- The phrase "survival of the fittest" is **Spencer's**, not Darwin's; for Darwin fitness = **reproductive fitness**.
- **Weismann** disproved Lamarckism; **de Vries** gave the mutation theory (*Oenothera*).
- Miller obtained **amino acids**, not living cells or proteins; the atmosphere was **reducing** (no O₂).
- Genetic drift is strong in **small** populations; gene flow makes populations **similar**.
- In Hardy-Weinberg, get **q from q²** (q² = frequency of the recessive phenotype); do not take the phenotype % directly as q. For X-linked genes, male frequency = q.
- In NCERT *Ramapithecus* is "man-like" and *Dryopithecus* "ape-like" — modern research has changed the status of *Ramapithecus*, but give the NCERT answer in the exam.
- *Homo habilis* did **not eat meat**, *H. erectus* did (NCERT).
- Neanderthals lived **100,000–40,000** years ago and were contemporaries of *H. sapiens* — not ancestors but "cousins".
- The conclusion of Kettlewell's experiment is not "**dark moths are always superior**"; fitness is relative to the environment.
- **Punctuated equilibrium** — Eldredge and Gould (1972); **neutral theory** — Kimura (1968).
