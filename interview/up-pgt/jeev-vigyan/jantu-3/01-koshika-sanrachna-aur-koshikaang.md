This chapter takes the first part of syllabus Unit 8 "Cell Biology" — **prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells and cell organelles** — from NCERT 11 ("Cell: The Unit of Life") up to B.Sc./M.Sc. level. The UP PGT 2026 paper does not ask facts directly; it asks them as statement combinations and "best explanation" questions — so remember the **structure → function → exception** sequence for every organelle.

> **In simple words:** The cell is the smallest independent unit of life. Simple cells like bacteria (prokaryotic) have no membrane-bound nucleus; our cells and plant cells (eukaryotic) have a nucleus and membrane-bound "rooms" (organelles) — the mitochondrion is the powerhouse, the ribosome is the protein-making machine, the Golgi is the packing department, the lysosome is the waste-disposal centre, and the cytoskeleton is the framework and the tracks.

## 1. Discovery of the cell and the cell theory

| Year | Scientist | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1665 | **Robert Hooke** | Saw dead cells of cork, coined the word "cell"; book *Micrographia* |
| 1674–83 | **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** | First to see **living** cells (bacteria, protozoa, sperm) — "animalcules" |
| 1831 | **Robert Brown** | Discovered the **nucleus** in orchid cells |
| 1838 | **M. J. Schleiden** — botanist | All plants are made of cells |
| 1839 | **Theodor Schwann** — zoologist | A "thin outer layer" in animal cells (plasma membrane); proposed the **cell theory** |
| 1839 / 1846 | Purkinje / von Mohl | The term "**protoplasm**" |
| 1855 | **Rudolf Virchow** | "***Omnis cellula e cellula***" — all cells arise from pre-existing cells |
| 1931 | Knoll & Ruska | **Electron microscope** |

**Modern cell theory:** (1) All organisms are made of cells and cell products; (2) the cell is the basic unit of structure and function; (3) new cells arise by division of pre-existing cells; (4) genetic information (DNA) resides in the cell and is passed on during division; (5) metabolism takes place inside the cell.

**Exception:** Viruses — acellular, multiply only inside a host cell; hence the cell theory does not apply to them.

> **Common mistake:** Schwann gave the "cell theory", but the part "every cell from a pre-existing cell" is Virchow's (1855). Schleiden and Schwann could not explain how new cells are formed.

### Microscopes and units of measurement

| Unit | Value | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Micrometre (µm) | 10⁻⁶ m | Bacteria 1–2 µm (*E. coli* ≈ 2 × 0.5 µm), RBC ≈ 7 µm |
| Nanometre (nm) | 10⁻⁹ m | Plasma membrane ≈ 7.5 nm (75 Å), DNA diameter 2 nm |
| Angstrom (Å) | 10⁻¹⁰ m | — |

- Resolution of the light microscope ≈ **0.2 µm (200 nm)**; electron microscope down to the **0.1–0.5 nm** level.
- **TEM** (Transmission) — internal structure, thin sections; **SEM** (Scanning) — three-dimensional image of the surface. Heavy-metal stains in TEM — **uranyl acetate, lead citrate, osmium tetroxide**.
- **Phase-contrast microscope** (Zernike, Nobel 1953) — for viewing **living, unstained** cells.

**Size records:** Smallest cell — **Mycoplasma (PPLO)** ≈ 0.1–0.3 µm; largest single cell — **ostrich egg**; longest — **nerve cell**; largest in humans — **ovum**, smallest — sperm (by volume).

## 2. Prokaryotic vs eukaryotic cell

**Prokaryotes:** bacteria, blue-green algae (cyanobacteria), mycoplasma, archaea (PPLO too). The terms "Prokaryote/Eukaryote" were given by **Édouard Chatton (1925)**; the cell-level distinction was made clear by Stanier and van Niel (1962).

| Feature | Prokaryotic | Eukaryotic |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Membrane-less **nucleoid** | True nucleus with a nuclear envelope (double membrane) |
| DNA | Usually one, **circular**, naked (no histones; histone-like proteins in archaea) | Many **linear** chromosomes, with histones (nucleosomes) |
| Plasmids | Usually present | Usually absent (the 2 µm plasmid of yeast is an exception) |
| Ribosomes | **70S** (50S + 30S) | **80S** in cytoplasm (60S + 40S); 70S-type in mitochondria and chloroplasts |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes etc. |
| Respiratory enzymes | On the plasma membrane | In mitochondria |
| Cell wall | **Peptidoglycan (murein)**; pseudomurein/protein in archaea | Plants — cellulose; fungi — chitin; animals — absent |
| Flagellum | Single filament of **flagellin**, moves by rotation, not 9+2 | **9+2** microtubules, whip-like beating by dynein |
| Cytoskeleton | Simple (FtsZ, MreB — homologues of tubulin and actin) | Microtubules, microfilaments, intermediate filaments |
| Division | **Binary fission**, not mitosis | Mitosis/meiosis |
| Transcription-translation | Simultaneous (coupled), in the same compartment | Transcription in the nucleus, translation in the cytoplasm |
| Size | 1–10 µm | 10–100 µm |

**Special parts of the prokaryotic cell:**
- **Cell envelope** — glycocalyx (loose = slime layer; thick and tough = capsule) + cell wall + plasma membrane.
- **Gram stain (Christian Gram, 1884):** Gram-positive — thick peptidoglycan, purple; Gram-negative — thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane (LPS), pink.
- **Mesosome** — infoldings of the plasma membrane; older texts (NCERT) link them with wall formation, DNA replication and respiration, but **in the modern view they are considered artifacts produced by chemical fixation**.
- **Chromatophores** — pigment-containing membranes in cyanobacteria.
- **Pili** and **fimbriae** — attachment; **sex pilus (F pilus)** — conjugation.
- **Inclusion bodies** — phosphate granules (volutin), glycogen granules, **gas vacuoles** (blue-green and purple bacteria).

> **Exam angle:** "Where are 70S ribosomes found?" — prokaryotes + **mitochondria + chloroplasts**. Mammalian mitochondrial ribosomes are actually ≈ **55S**, but in the exam the answer is "70S-type".

## 3. Animal cell vs plant cell

| Feature | Animal cell | Plant cell |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Absent | Cellulose wall present |
| Plastids | Absent | Present (chloroplasts, chromoplasts, leucoplasts) |
| Vacuole | Small, temporary, many | One large central vacuole (bounded by the tonoplast) |
| Centrosome | Present | Usually absent in higher plants |
| Stored food | Glycogen | Starch |
| Lysosomes | Prominent | Few (the vacuole performs lysosome-like functions) |
| Cytokinesis | By a furrow, from outside inwards | By a **cell plate**, from the centre towards the periphery |
| Intercellular connections | **Gap junctions**, desmosomes, tight junctions | **Plasmodesmata** |

## 4. Plasma membrane — models and transport

| Model | Year | Scientists | Main point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lipid bilayer | 1925 | **Gorter and Grendel** | Lipid layer from RBCs covered twice the cell surface area |
| Sandwich (trilaminar) | 1935 | **Danielli and Davson** | Protein–lipid–protein |
| Unit membrane | 1959 | **J. D. Robertson** | All membranes are trilaminar, ≈ **75 Å** (25 + 25 + 25 Å) |
| **Fluid mosaic** | **1972** | **Singer & Nicolson** | A fluid "sea" of lipid bilayer with proteins floating in it like "icebergs"; lateral movement |

**Composition:** The human RBC membrane has ≈ **52% protein, 40% lipid** (NCERT). Lipids are mainly **phospholipids** (polar heads outside, non-polar tails inside) + cholesterol (animals). Proteins — **peripheral (extrinsic)** and **integral (intrinsic)**; transmembrane proteins. Carbohydrates on the outer surface — glycoproteins/glycolipids (cell recognition, blood group antigens).

- **Flip-flop** (movement of a lipid from one layer to the other) is very slow — done by the enzyme **flippase**; lateral diffusion is rapid. Hence the membrane stays **asymmetric**.
- Fluidity increases with unsaturated fatty acids, shorter chains and higher temperature; cholesterol acts as a "fluidity buffer".

### Transport across the membrane

| Type | Energy | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Simple diffusion | No | O₂, CO₂, steroids — along the concentration gradient |
| Osmosis | No | Water, through a semipermeable membrane; **aquaporins** (Peter Agre, Nobel 2003) |
| Facilitated diffusion | No | Glucose (GLUT), ion channels — carrier/channel proteins, shows saturation |
| Active transport | **ATP** | **Na⁺/K⁺ pump** — per ATP **3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in** (Jens Skou, Nobel 1997) |
| Secondary active (co-transport) | Ion gradient | Symport (Na⁺–glucose, intestine), antiport (Na⁺/Ca²⁺) |
| Endocytosis | ATP | Phagocytosis (solids), pinocytosis (fluid), receptor-mediated (LDL, clathrin) |
| Exocytosis | ATP | Secretion of hormones, neurotransmitters |

**Cell junctions:** tight junctions (prevent leakage — intestinal epithelium, blood-brain barrier), desmosomes (mechanical attachment — skin, heart), gap junctions (exchange of ions/small molecules through connexons — heart muscle).

## 5. Nucleus

- Discovered by **Robert Brown (1831)**; the material of the nucleus that takes up stain was named "**chromatin**" by **Flemming**.
- **Nuclear envelope** — two membranes with a **perinuclear space** of 10–50 nm between them; the outer membrane is continuous with the RER and bears ribosomes.
- **Nuclear pore** — a **nuclear pore complex** of ≈ 120 nm (≈ 30 types of nucleoporins); RNA goes out, proteins come in; import signal **NLS**.
- **Nuclear lamina** — a meshwork of **lamins** (type V intermediate filaments); during division, phosphorylation of lamins breaks down the envelope.
- **Nucleolus** — seen by **Fontana (1781)**; membrane-less; site of **rRNA synthesis** and assembly of ribosomal subunits. **Nucleolar organiser region (NOR)** — in humans on the satellite stalks of the acrocentric chromosomes **13, 14, 15, 21, 22**. Large in cells active in protein synthesis.
- Enucleate cells: **mature mammalian RBCs**, plant **sieve tube** cells, platelets. Multinucleate: skeletal muscle (syncytium), *Vaucheria*, *Rhizopus* (coenocyte).

## 6. Endomembrane system

According to NCERT, the ER, Golgi, lysosomes and vacuoles together form the **endomembrane system** (functions coordinated). **Mitochondria, chloroplasts and peroxisomes are not included** in it.

### 6.1 Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)

- First seen by **Porter, Claude and Fullam (1945)**; named by **Porter (1953)**.
- **Rough (RER)** — bears ribosomes (attached via ribophorins); synthesis of **secretory proteins** and membrane proteins; abundant in pancreas and plasma cells. In nerve cells, **Nissl granules** = RER.
- **Smooth (SER)** — synthesis of **lipids and steroid hormones** (adrenal cortex, interstitial/Leydig cells), **detoxification** in the liver (cytochrome P450), glycogen metabolism (glucose-6-phosphatase). In muscle it is the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** — Ca²⁺ store.
- **Glycosylation** of proteins (N-linked) begins in the RER and is completed in the Golgi (O-linked occurs in the Golgi). **Signal hypothesis** — **Günter Blobel** (Nobel 1999).

### 6.2 Golgi apparatus

- **Camillo Golgi (1898)** saw it in nerve cells of the owl/cat using silver staining ("internal reticular apparatus"). In plants the scattered units = **dictyosomes**.
- A stack of flat **cisternae** 0.5–1.0 µm in diameter; usually **4–8 cisternae** per stack. The **cis (forming, convex)** face is towards the ER, the **trans (maturing, concave)** face is away from the nucleus/towards the membrane.
- Functions: modification, sorting and packing of proteins (secretory vesicles), **formation of glycoproteins and glycolipids**, formation of lysosomes (mannose-6-phosphate tag), the **acrosome** of the sperm, and in plants the cell plate and wall polysaccharides (hemicellulose, pectin).

> **PYQ (UP PGT 2026):** In eukaryotic cells, dictyosomes take part in the processing and transport of biomolecules received from the endoplasmic reticulum. Which statement explains their role in cellular metabolism? (A) Dictyosomes are extensions of the ER and mainly carry out intracellular digestion (B) Dictyosomes modify, sort and pack macromolecules received from the ER into vesicles for transport and secretion (C) Dictyosomes make proteins and lipids independently without participation of the ER (D) Dictyosomes contain genetic material and control transcription in the nucleus. — Reasoned answer (official key not available): **(B)**. (A) is the function of lysosomes; "independently" in (C) and "genetic material" in (D) are wrong.

### 6.3 Lysosome

- **Christian de Duve, 1955** (Nobel 1974); "**suicide bags**".
- Single membrane, **acidic pH ≈ 4.5–5** inside (H⁺-ATPase pump), more than ≈ 50 **acid hydrolases** — lipases, proteases, carbohydrases, nucleases; marker enzyme **acid phosphatase**.
- Types: primary lysosome → secondary (digestive vacuole/heterophagosome) → residual body; **autophagosome** — digests the cell's own old organelles (**Yoshinori Ohsumi, Nobel 2016 — Autophagy**).
- **Autolysis** — resorption of the tail of the frog tadpole.
- Diseases: **lysosomal storage diseases** — **Tay-Sachs** (hexosaminidase A), **Pompe** (glycogen), Gaucher; **I-cell disease** (mannose-6-phosphate tag not added).

### 6.4 Vacuoles

In plants the central vacuole occupies **up to 90%** of the cell volume; its membrane is the **tonoplast** — active transport of ions, so the concentration in the cell sap is higher than in the cytoplasm. *Amoeba* — **contractile vacuole** (osmoregulation); protists — food vacuoles.

## 7. Mitochondria — "the powerhouse of the cell"

- Seen by **Kölliker (1880)** in insect muscle; **Altmann (1890)** — "bioblasts"; the name "**mitochondrion**" — **C. Benda (1898)**. Specific stain — **Janus green B** (vital stain).
- Size 0.2–1.0 µm (average 0.5 µm) in diameter, 1.0–4.1 µm in length; number depends on the activity of the cell (≈ 1000–2000 in a liver cell). **Absent in mature RBCs**.
- Double membrane: **outer** — smooth, highly permeable due to **porins**; **inner** — semipermeable, rich in **cardiolipin**, folded inwards as **cristae**; on the inner membrane are **F₀–F₁ particles** (oxysomes/elementary particles, ATP synthase — F₁ head, F₀ embedded in the membrane).
- **Matrix** — enzymes of the Krebs cycle (TCA) (succinate dehydrogenase is on the inner membrane — this is the **marker enzyme**), circular **mtDNA**, 70S-type ribosomes, tRNA.
- **Semi-autonomous** — own DNA, ribosomes, multiplication by binary fission; but most proteins come from nuclear genes.
- **Human mtDNA:** **16,569 bp**, **37 genes** (13 proteins, 22 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs); **maternal inheritance** — LHON (Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy).
- **Marker enzymes:** outer membrane — monoamine oxidase; intermembrane space — adenylate kinase; inner membrane — cytochrome oxidase/succinate dehydrogenase; matrix — malate dehydrogenase.

**Endosymbiotic theory — Lynn Margulis (1967):** mitochondria arose from aerobic bacteria like **α-proteobacteria**, and chloroplasts from **cyanobacteria**. Evidence — circular naked DNA, 70S ribosomes, binary fission, double membrane, bacteria-like composition of the inner membrane, sensitivity to antibiotics (chloramphenicol).

**Chloroplast** — for comparison: double membrane, **thylakoids → grana**, stroma lamellae, **RuBisCO** in the stroma (the most abundant protein on Earth), circular DNA. (Details in Botany II.)

## 8. Ribosomes, peroxisomes and glyoxysomes

**Ribosome** — seen by **George Palade (1953/55)** ("Palade granules"; Nobel 1974). Membrane-less; rRNA + protein. S = **Svedberg unit** (sedimentation coefficient) — **not additive** (50S + 30S = 70S).

| Ribosome | Large subunit (rRNA) | Small subunit (rRNA) |
|---|---|---|
| 70S (prokaryote) | **50S** — 23S + 5S | **30S** — 16S |
| 80S (eukaryote) | **60S** — 28S + 5.8S + 5S | **40S** — 18S |

- Association of subunits depends on **Mg²⁺**; many ribosomes on one mRNA = **polysome/polyribosome**.
- **23S rRNA (prokaryotes) / 28S rRNA (eukaryotes)** is itself the **peptidyl transferase** — a ribozyme.

**Peroxisome** — called "microbodies" by **Rhodin (1954)**; named peroxisome by **de Duve (1965)**. Single membrane; **catalase** (marker enzyme) and oxidases; produce and break down H₂O₂ (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂); β-oxidation of fatty acids (very long chain), detoxification in liver and kidney (alcohol). In plants, **photorespiration** — chloroplast–peroxisome–mitochondrion in the C₂ cycle. Disease: **Zellweger syndrome**.
**Glyoxysome** — **glyoxylate cycle** (isocitrate lyase, malate synthase) during germination of oil seeds (castor, groundnut) — fat → sugar; **absent in animals**.

## 9. Cytoskeleton, centrosome, cilia and flagella

| Filament | Diameter | Protein | Main functions | Inhibitors |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Microtubule** | **≈ 25 nm** (hollow, 13 protofilaments) | **α- and β-tubulin** (GTP) | Spindle, cilia-flagella, organelle transport (**kinesin** towards the + end, **dynein** towards the − end) | **Colchicine**, vinblastine (depolymerise); **taxol** (stabilises) |
| **Microfilament** | **≈ 7 nm** | **Actin** (G → F, ATP) | Muscle contraction, pseudopodia (amoeboid movement), **cytoplasmic streaming (cyclosis)**, cytokinesis furrow (contractile ring), microvilli | **Cytochalasin** (depolymerises), phalloidin (stabilises) |
| **Intermediate filament** | **≈ 10 nm** | Keratin (epithelium), vimentin, desmin (muscle), neurofilaments, lamins (nucleus) | Mechanical strength; most stable; **non-polar** | — |

> **Memory trick:** "**7–10–25**" — actin is the thinnest (7), intermediate in the middle (10), tubulin the thickest (25).

**Centrosome and centriole:** The centrosome has two centrioles lying at right angles; each is cylindrical with **9 triplets** of microtubules at the periphery — "**9 + 0**"; a proteinaceous **hub** in the centre and radial **spokes** joining it to the triplets — the "**cartwheel**" structure. Centrioles become **basal bodies** and give rise to cilia-flagella; in division they form spindle fibres (aster). Centriole duplication occurs in the **S phase**.

**Cilia and flagella:** **Axoneme** — **9 doublets** at the periphery + **2 singlet** microtubules in the centre = **9 + 2 arrangement**. **Dynein arms** from the A-tubule (ATPase — bending by sliding), **nexin** links, radial spokes, central sheath. Cilia are short and many (oar-like movement), flagella long and few (wave-like movement).
- **Kartagener syndrome / primary ciliary dyskinesia** — lack of dynein arms: respiratory infections, male infertility (immotile sperm), **situs inversus**.
- The primary cilium is **9 + 0** — sensory (kidney tubule, photoreceptors).

## 10. Other cell structures (quick)

- **Cytosol** — site of glycolysis, protein synthesis (free ribosomes), fatty acid synthesis.
- **Proteasome** — degradation of ubiquitin-tagged proteins (Nobel Chemistry 2004 — Ciechanover, Hershko, Rose).
- **Spherosomes** — fat storage in plants (oleosomes).
- **Centromere, kinetochore** — parts of the chromosome; in the next chapter.
- **Inclusion bodies** — non-living stores (glycogen, fat droplets, crystals).

## Traps and exceptions

- **Mammalian RBCs** have no nucleus, mitochondria or ribosomes — energy comes only from **anaerobic glycolysis**. (RBCs of the camel and llama are oval but still enucleate; RBCs of birds, reptiles and amphibians are nucleated.)
- "All prokaryotes have a cell wall" — wrong; **Mycoplasma is wall-less** (hence penicillin-resistant and pleomorphic).
- Lysosomes and peroxisomes both have a single membrane, but peroxisomal proteins are made on free ribosomes and imported directly — the peroxisome is not part of the endomembrane system.
- Do not add 70S + 80S; "S" is a sedimentation rate, not a sum of masses.
- The **cis** face of the Golgi faces the ER (receiving) and the **trans** face faces secretion — do not reverse them.
- Flagella: the bacterial flagellum is a solid filament of **flagellin** that rotates (driven by the proton-motive force); the eukaryotic flagellum is 9+2 and bends using ATP.
- **Triplets** in the centriole, **doublets** in the axoneme — "9+0 triplets" vs "9+2 doublets".
- Plant cells form a spindle even without centrioles (from the **MTOC** — microtubule-organising centre).
- The membrane richest in cholesterol is the plasma membrane; the inner mitochondrial membrane has very little cholesterol and much cardiolipin.

## Practice questions (with answers)

1. What is Virchow's famous statement? — ***Omnis cellula e cellula*** (every cell from a pre-existing cell).
2. Which organism is an exception to the cell theory? — **Virus** (acellular).
3. rRNA of the small subunit of the prokaryotic ribosome? — **16S** (in the 30S subunit).
4. Which rRNAs are in the eukaryotic 60S subunit? — **28S, 5.8S, 5S**.
5. Who gave the fluid mosaic model? — **Singer and Nicolson (1972)** (proteins floating in a lipid bilayer).
6. What are called suicide bags? — **Lysosomes** (autolysis by acid hydrolases).
7. Marker enzyme of mitochondria (inner membrane/matrix boundary)? — **Succinate dehydrogenase** (the only membrane-bound enzyme of the TCA cycle).
8. Which organelle is abundant in steroid hormone-producing cells? — **SER**.
9. What forms the acrosome of the sperm? — **Golgi apparatus**.
10. Thinnest cytoskeletal filament? — **Microfilament (actin, ≈ 7 nm)**.
11. Where is the 9+2 arrangement found? — **Axoneme of cilia and flagella**.
12. Which organelle causes the disappearance of the tadpole's tail? — **Lysosome** (autolysis/programmed death).
13. Eukaryotic organelles with 70S ribosomes? — **Mitochondria and chloroplasts** (evidence for endosymbiotic origin).
14. In which organelle does the glyoxylate cycle occur? — **Glyoxysome** (fat → sugar in oil seeds).
15. Tay-Sachs disease is a disorder of which organelle? — **Lysosome** (deficiency of hexosaminidase A).
16. Why do Gram-negative bacteria appear pink? — **Thin peptidoglycan layer**, so the crystal violet is washed out.
17. On which membrane is ATP synthase found? — **Inner mitochondrial membrane** (F₀–F₁ particles).
