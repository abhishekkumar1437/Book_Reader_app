This chapter is the grammar of organic chemistry. It covers NCERT class 10 ("Carbon and its Compounds"), class 11 ("Organic Chemistry — Some Basic Principles and Techniques") and class 12 haloalkane mechanisms, with B.Sc. points such as nucleophilicity in different solvents and IR frequencies. TRE asked many one-liners here: most stable carbocation, strongest acid, nucleophilic reagent, isomer of diethyl ether (TRE 2.0); isomers of chlorodiphenylmethane, nucleophilicity order, steam distillation of nitrophenols, SN1 substrate, IR C=O band and spectroscopy vs classical methods (TRE 1.0).

> **In simple words:** Organic reactions are electron traffic. Electron-rich species (nucleophiles) attack electron-poor centres (electrophiles). Groups that push or pull electrons (inductive and resonance effects) decide how stable an intermediate is, and that decides which product forms and how fast.

## 1. Basics

- **Wöhler (1828)** made urea (organic) from ammonium cyanate (inorganic), ending the "vital force" theory. Kolbe made acetic acid (1845), Berthelot methane (1856).
- Carbon is unique because of **catenation**, **tetravalency** and strong multiple bonds; over 10 million compounds are known.
- **Hybridisation of carbon:** sp³ (single bonds only, 109.5°), sp² (one double bond, 120°), sp (triple bond or two double bonds on one C, 180°).

| Compound / carbon | Hybridisation |
|---|---|
| CH₄, C of CH₃ | sp³ |
| C₂H₄, benzene, C of C=O, carbocation, free radical (planar) | sp² |
| C₂H₂, CO₂, C≡N, central C of allene CH₂=C=CH₂ | sp |
| Carbanion CH₃⁻ | sp³ (pyramidal) |

**Degree of unsaturation (double-bond equivalent)** = C − H/2 − X/2 + N/2 + 1. Benzene C₆H₆: 6 − 3 + 1 = **4** (3 C=C + 1 ring). C₂H₂: 2 − 1 + 1 = 2.

### Functional groups and homologous series

A **homologous series** is a family with the same functional group, successive members differing by **–CH₂–** (14 u), similar chemical properties and graded physical properties.

| Class | Functional group | Suffix / prefix | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | -oic acid | CH₃COOH ethanoic acid |
| Sulphonic acid | –SO₃H | -sulphonic acid | C₆H₅SO₃H |
| Ester | –COOR | alkyl -oate | CH₃COOC₂H₅ ethyl ethanoate |
| Acid chloride | –COCl | -oyl chloride | CH₃COCl ethanoyl chloride |
| Amide | –CONH₂ | -amide | CH₃CONH₂ ethanamide |
| Nitrile | –C≡N | -nitrile | CH₃CN ethanenitrile |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | -al | CH₃CHO ethanal |
| Ketone | >C=O | -one | CH₃COCH₃ propanone |
| Alcohol | –OH | -ol | C₂H₅OH ethanol |
| Amine | –NH₂ | -amine | CH₃NH₂ methanamine |
| Alkene / alkyne | C=C / C≡C | -ene / -yne | C₂H₄ ethene |
| Ether | –O– | alkoxy- | CH₃OCH₃ methoxymethane |
| Halide, nitro | –X, –NO₂ | halo-, nitro- (prefix only) | CH₃Cl chloromethane |

**Priority for choosing the principal group (suffix):** –COOH > –SO₃H > –COOR > –COCl > –CONH₂ > –CN > –CHO > >C=O > –OH > –NH₂ > C=C > C≡C. Halo, nitro, alkoxy are always prefixes.

## 2. IUPAC nomenclature

**Word roots:** meth (1), eth (2), prop (3), but (4), pent (5), hex (6), hept (7), oct (8), non (9), dec (10).

**Rules:**
1. Choose the longest chain containing the principal functional group and maximum multiple bonds.
2. Number to give the principal group the lowest locant; then multiple bonds; then substituents (**lowest set of locants**).
3. Name substituents in **alphabetical order** (ignore di, tri, sec, tert; but "iso" and "neo" are alphabetised).
4. When double and triple bonds are at equal distance, the double bond gets the lower number.

| Structure | IUPAC name |
|---|---|
| CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ | 2-Methylbutane |
| (CH₃)₃C–OH | **2-Methylpropan-2-ol** (tert-butyl alcohol) |
| (CH₃)₂CHOH | Propan-2-ol (isopropyl alcohol) |
| (C₂H₅)₂CHOH | Pentan-3-ol |
| CH₂=CH–CH₂–OH | Prop-2-en-1-ol (allyl alcohol) |
| CH₃CH(Cl)COOH | 2-Chloropropanoic acid |
| CH₃COCH₂CH₃ | Butan-2-one |
| CH₂=CH–C≡CH | But-1-en-3-yne |
| CH₃CH(OH)COOH | 2-Hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid) |
| HOOC–COOH | Ethanedioic acid (oxalic acid) |
| CH₂=CH–CN | Prop-2-enenitrile (acrylonitrile) |
| C₆H₅CH₂Cl | (Chloromethyl)benzene (benzyl chloride) |
| (C₆H₅)₂CH₂ | Diphenylmethane |

**Aromatic names:** C₆H₅OH phenol; C₆H₅NH₂ aniline (benzenamine); C₆H₅CH₃ toluene (methylbenzene); C₆H₅CHO benzaldehyde; C₆H₅COOH benzoic acid; o-, m-, p- = 1,2-, 1,3-, 1,4-.

**Common names worth knowing:** picric acid = **2,4,6-trinitrophenol**; salicylic acid = 2-hydroxybenzoic acid; aspirin = **acetylsalicylic acid** (2-acetoxybenzoic acid); anthranilic acid = o-aminobenzoic acid; TNT = 2,4,6-trinitrotoluene; chloroform = trichloromethane; acetone = propanone; formalin = 40% HCHO.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Which of the following is picric acid? (A) 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (B) o-aminobenzoic acid (C) m-nitrobenzoic acid". Official answer: **(A)**. (o-Aminobenzoic acid is anthranilic acid.)

## 3. Isomerism

**Isomers** have the same molecular formula but different arrangements.

### Structural isomerism

| Type | Differs in | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Chain | Carbon skeleton | n-butane and isobutane (C₄H₁₀) |
| Position | Position of group / multiple bond | Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol; but-1-ene and but-2-ene |
| **Functional** | Functional group | **Alcohols and ethers (C₄H₁₀O)**; aldehydes and ketones (C₃H₆O); acids and esters (C₂H₄O₂); nitrile and isonitrile |
| Metamerism | Alkyl groups on either side of a polyvalent group | Diethyl ether and methyl propyl ether; diethyl ketone and methyl propyl ketone |
| Tautomerism | Rapid H shift, two forms in equilibrium | Keto–enol: CH₃COCH₃ ⇌ CH₂=C(OH)CH₃; phenol–keto forms |
| Ring–chain | Open chain vs ring | Propene and cyclopropane (C₃H₆) |

**Isomer of diethyl ether (C₂H₅OC₂H₅ = C₄H₁₀O).** Any C₄ alcohol (butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol, **2-methylpropan-2-ol (CH₃)₃COH**) is a functional isomer; methyl propyl ether and methyl isopropyl ether are metamers. (CH₃)₂CHOH is C₃H₈O and (C₂H₅)₂CHOH is C₅H₁₂O — wrong formulas.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Isomer of diethyl ether is (A) (CH₃)₃C–OH (B) (CH₃)₂CHOH (C) (C₂H₅)₂CHOH". Official answer: **(A) (CH₃)₃C–OH**. Count the carbons first: only (A) has C₄H₁₀O.

### Counting isomers (frequent MCQ)

| Formula / question | Number |
|---|---|
| C₄H₁₀ | 2 |
| C₅H₁₂ | 3 (n-pentane, isopentane, neopentane) |
| C₆H₁₄ | 5 |
| C₇H₁₆ | 9 |
| C₃H₈O | 3 (2 alcohols + 1 ether) |
| C₄H₁₀O | 7 (4 alcohols + 3 ethers) |
| C₄H₉Cl (structural) | 4 |
| C₄H₈ (alkenes, structural) | 3 (but-1-ene, but-2-ene, 2-methylpropene); 4 counting cis/trans but-2-ene; plus 2 cyclic |
| Dichlorobenzene | 3 (o, m, p) |
| Trichlorobenzene | 3 |
| Monochloro products of n-pentane / isopentane / neopentane | 3 / 4 / 1 |
| **Monochloro derivatives of diphenylmethane** | **4** |

**Worked example — chlorodiphenylmethane.** Diphenylmethane is C₆H₅–CH₂–C₆H₅; the two rings are equivalent. Distinct H positions: (1) the CH₂ carbon → (C₆H₅)₂CHCl; (2) ortho, (3) meta, (4) para on a ring. Total **4 structural isomers**.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) asked: "How many structural isomers are possible if one hydrogen in diphenylmethane is replaced by chlorine? (A) 8 (B) 4 (C) 7". Correct: **(B) 4**. (No official key in our folder.)

### Stereoisomerism

**Geometrical (cis–trans) isomerism** needs restricted rotation (C=C or ring) and two different groups on each doubly bonded carbon. But-2-ene: cis (same side) and trans. Maleic acid (cis) and fumaric acid (trans). Cis isomers usually have higher dipole moment and b.p.; trans have higher m.p. (better packing) and are more stable. **E/Z system** uses CIP priorities (Z = higher priorities on the same side). But-1-ene and (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ do not show it.

**Optical isomerism** — **chiral** molecules (no plane or centre of symmetry, usually with an asymmetric carbon bearing four different groups) exist as non-superimposable mirror images (**enantiomers**). They rotate plane-polarised light (polarimeter): **dextrorotatory (d, +)** or **laevorotatory (l, −)**. Pasteur (1848) separated tartrate crystals; van 't Hoff and Le Bel (1874) proposed the tetrahedral carbon.

- **Racemic mixture** (dl, ±): equal amounts of enantiomers; optically inactive by external compensation. **Resolution** separates them.
- **Meso compound:** has chiral centres but an internal plane of symmetry → inactive (internal compensation): meso-tartaric acid.
- **Diastereomers:** stereoisomers that are not mirror images (different physical properties).
- Maximum stereoisomers = **2ⁿ** (n = chiral centres), fewer if meso forms exist. Tartaric acid: 2 chiral centres but **3** forms (d, l, meso). Open-chain glucose: 4 chiral centres → 16 stereoisomers.
- Examples of chiral molecules: lactic acid, butan-2-ol, 2-chlorobutane, glyceraldehyde, all α-amino acids except **glycine** (achiral).
- **Absolute configuration:** R/S (Cahn–Ingold–Prelog); **relative:** D/L (with reference to glyceraldehyde). d/l (sign of rotation) is unrelated to D/L.

**Conformations** — different arrangements by rotation about a single bond (not separate isomers). Ethane: **staggered** (most stable) and **eclipsed** (least stable); energy difference ≈ 12.5 kJ mol⁻¹ (torsional strain). Butane: anti (most stable) > gauche > eclipsed > fully eclipsed. Cyclohexane: **chair** (most stable, strain-free) > twist-boat > boat > half-chair.

## 4. Electronic effects

| Effect | What happens | Order / examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Inductive (I)** | Permanent polarisation transmitted through σ bonds; fades after ~3 carbons | **−I:** –NO₂ > –CN > –COOH > –F > –Cl > –Br > –I > –OCH₃ > –OH > –C₆H₅ > H. **+I:** –C(CH₃)₃ > –CH(CH₃)₂ > –C₂H₅ > –CH₃ > H; also –COO⁻, –O⁻ |
| **Resonance / mesomeric (R or M)** | Delocalisation of π or lone-pair electrons through conjugation | **+R** (donate): –OH, –OR, –NH₂, –NHR, –X, –O⁻. **−R** (withdraw): –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COR, –COOH, –SO₃H |
| **Electromeric (E)** | Temporary complete shift of π electrons when a reagent approaches | Occurs only in presence of reagent |
| **Hyperconjugation** (Baker–Nathan, no-bond resonance) | Delocalisation of σ electrons of C–H bonds on the carbon next to a carbocation, radical or double bond | More α-H → more stabilisation: (CH₃)₃C⁺ has 9 α-H |

Halogens are special: **−I > +R**, so they deactivate the benzene ring but are still ortho/para directors.

## 5. Bond fission and reactive intermediates

- **Homolytic fission** (each atom takes one electron) → free radicals; favoured by light, heat, peroxides, non-polar solvents.
- **Heterolytic fission** (one atom takes both electrons) → ions (carbocation + anion, or carbanion + cation); favoured by polar solvents.

| Intermediate | Charge / structure | Hybridisation and shape | Stability order |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbocation** (carbonium ion) | C⁺, 6 valence electrons | sp², planar, empty p orbital | **3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺**; resonance-stabilised ones (allyl, benzyl, especially **(C₆H₅)₃C⁺**) are very stable |
| **Carbanion** | C⁻, 8 electrons (one lone pair) | sp³, pyramidal | **CH₃⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3°** (alkyl +I destabilises); stabilised by –I/–R groups, benzyl, allyl |
| **Free radical** | C·, 7 electrons | sp² (nearly planar) | **3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃·**; allyl and benzyl very stable |
| Carbene | :CH₂, neutral, divalent | Singlet (sp²) or triplet | Very reactive (from CHCl₃ + base → :CCl₂) |
| Nitrene | R–N:, neutral | — | Intermediate in Hofmann/Curtius rearrangements |

**Why (C₆H₅)₃C⁺ is the most stable common carbocation:** its positive charge is delocalised by resonance over three benzene rings (about ten resonance structures). The allyl cation CH₂=CH–CH₂⁺ has only two resonance forms; tert-butyl (CH₃)₃C⁺ is stabilised only by +I and hyperconjugation.

Typical exam order: **(C₆H₅)₃C⁺ > (C₆H₅)₂CH⁺ > C₆H₅CH₂⁺ ≈ CH₂=CHCH₂⁺ ≈ (CH₃)₃C⁺ > (CH₃)₂CH⁺ > CH₃CH₂⁺ > CH₃⁺**. (Where benzyl/allyl sit relative to tert-butyl differs between books and between gas phase and solution; in MCQs choose the one with the most resonance.) Tropylium ion C₇H₇⁺ (aromatic) is even more stable. Vinyl (CH₂=CH⁺) and phenyl (C₆H₅⁺) cations are very unstable.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Which of the following is the most stable carbonium ion? (A) (CH₃)₃C⁺ (B) (C₆H₅)₃C⁺ (C) CH₂=CH–CH₂⁺". Official answer: **(B) (C₆H₅)₃C⁺**.

## 6. Nucleophiles and electrophiles

| Nucleophile ("nucleus-loving", Lewis base) | Electrophile ("electron-loving", Lewis acid) |
|---|---|
| Has a lone pair or π electrons to donate | Electron deficient; accepts an electron pair |
| Negative: OH⁻, RO⁻, CN⁻, X⁻, SH⁻, RS⁻, NH₂⁻, R⁻ (carbanion, Grignard), H⁻ (from LiAlH₄/NaBH₄) | Positive: H⁺, **NO₂⁺** (nitronium), Cl⁺, Br⁺, R⁺ (carbocation), RCO⁺ (acylium), NO⁺, ⁺SO₃H |
| Neutral: H₂O, ROH, NH₃, RNH₂, **R₃N**, R₂O, PR₃ | Neutral: **SO₃**, BF₃, AlCl₃, FeCl₃, ZnCl₂, :CCl₂ (carbene), carbonyl carbon |
| Alkenes, alkynes, arenes (π-donors) | — |

**Ambident nucleophiles** attack through two different atoms: CN⁻ (C or N; KCN gives nitrile RCN, AgCN gives isocyanide RNC), NO₂⁻ (KNO₂ gives alkyl nitrite R–ONO, AgNO₂ gives nitroalkane R–NO₂).

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Which of the following is a nucleophilic reagent? (A) R₃N (B) SO₃ (C) NO₂⁺". Official answer: **(A) R₃N** (lone pair on N). SO₃ and NO₂⁺ are electrophiles.

**Nucleophilicity trends**
- Same attacking atom: anion > its conjugate acid: OH⁻ > H₂O; RO⁻ > ROH; NH₂⁻ > NH₃.
- Across a period, nucleophilicity follows basicity: CH₃⁻ > NH₂⁻ > OH⁻ > F⁻.
- **Down a group in polar protic solvents (water, alcohols): I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻**; RS⁻ > RO⁻. Small F⁻ is heavily solvated (H-bonded), so it is a poor nucleophile; large I⁻ is polarisable and lightly solvated.
- **In polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF, acetone): F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻** (follows basicity, no H-bond solvation).
- Bulky bases (tert-butoxide) are strong bases but poor nucleophiles.
- **Leaving-group ability** (weaker base = better leaving group): I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻; so reactivity of haloalkanes **R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F**.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) asked: "The correct order of increasing nucleophilicity is (A) Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻ (B) Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < I⁻ (C) I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻". The standard answer (protic solvent) is **(A) Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻**. In aprotic solvents the order reverses, but MCQs assume protic. (No official key in our folder.)

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Substitution — free radical | CH₄ + Cl₂ —hν→ CH₃Cl + HCl (chain: initiation, propagation, termination) |
| Substitution — nucleophilic (SN1, SN2) | R–X + OH⁻ → R–OH + X⁻ |
| Substitution — electrophilic aromatic | Nitration, halogenation, sulphonation, Friedel–Crafts of benzene |
| Addition — electrophilic | Alkenes + HX, X₂, H₂O (H⁺), H₂SO₄ |

### SN1 and SN2

<div class="formula">

| Feature | SN1 (unimolecular) | SN2 (bimolecular) |
|---|---|---|
| Rate law | rate = k[R–X] (first order) | rate = k[R–X][Nu⁻] (second order) |
| Steps | Two: slow ionisation → carbocation, then fast attack | One concerted step, pentacoordinate transition state |

| Solvent | Polar protic (water, alcohols, formic acid) | Polar aprotic (acetone, DMSO, DMF) |
| Nucleophile | Weak nucleophile is fine | Strong nucleophile needed |

**Special cases**
- **Benzyl chloride (C₆H₅CH₂Cl)** ionises easily because the benzyl carbocation is resonance-stabilised → classic **SN1** substrate (it is a primary halide, so it can also react by SN2 with strong nucleophiles in aprotic solvents).
- **Chlorobenzene (aryl halide) and vinyl chloride** are very unreactive to both SN1 and SN2: the C–Cl bond has partial double-bond character (resonance), the carbon is sp², and the phenyl cation is unstable. They react only under drastic conditions (623 K, 300 atm NaOH — Dow process) or when activated by o/p –NO₂ groups.
- **Ethyl chloride** (1°) reacts by SN2.
- Neopentyl halides are very slow in SN2 (bulky).

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) asked: "Find the compound which undergoes nucleophilic substitution reaction exclusively by an SN1 mechanism. (A) Benzyl chloride (B) Chlorobenzene (C) Ethyl chloride". Intended answer: **(A) Benzyl chloride**. ("Exclusively" is too strong — benzyl chloride can also do SN2 — but of the three it is the only SN1 substrate; no official key in our folder.)

### Elimination

- **E2** (one step, strong base, anti-periplanar H and X) and **E1** (via carbocation).
- **Saytzeff (Zaitsev) rule:** the more substituted (more stable) alkene is the major product: 2-bromobutane + alc. KOH → but-2-ene (major, ~80%) + but-1-ene. **Hofmann rule:** bulky base or quaternary ammonium bases give the less substituted alkene.
- Substitution vs elimination: **aqueous KOH → substitution (alcohol); alcoholic KOH → elimination (alkene)**. High temperature and bulky strong bases favour elimination. 3° halides prefer elimination.

## 8. Acidity and basicity orders

**Rule:** anything that stabilises the conjugate base (anion) increases acidity: –I groups, –R groups, resonance, more s-character, and electronegativity. +I groups (alkyl) decrease acidity.

**Carboxylic acids**

| Comparison | Order (strongest first) |
|---|---|
| Effect of number of Cl | Cl₃C–COOH > Cl₂CH–COOH > ClCH₂–COOH > CH₃COOH |
| Effect of halogen | F–CH₂COOH > Cl–CH₂COOH > Br–CH₂COOH > I–CH₂COOH |
| Effect of position of Cl | **CH₃CHClCOOH (α) > ClCH₂CH₂COOH (β) > CH₃CH₂COOH** (–I fades with distance) |
| Alkyl (+I) effect | HCOOH (pKa 3.75) > C₆H₅COOH (4.20) > CH₃COOH (4.76) > CH₃CH₂COOH (4.87) |
| Substituted benzoic acids | p-NO₂ > m-NO₂ > benzoic > p-CH₃ > p-OCH₃; **ortho-substituted benzoic acids are stronger (ortho effect)** |
| Dicarboxylic | Oxalic acid strongest first ionisation |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) asked: "Which of the following is the strongest acid? (A) CH₃CH₂COOH (B) CH₃CHClCOOH (C) CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH". Official answer: **(B) CH₃CHClCOOH** — the α-Cl (−I) stabilises the carboxylate; the others carry only electron-donating alkyl groups.

**Other acidity orders**
- **Phenols:** picric acid (2,4,6-trinitrophenol, pKa ≈ 0.4) > 2,4-dinitrophenol > p-nitrophenol (7.1) > o-nitrophenol (7.2) > m-nitrophenol (8.3) > phenol (10.0) > m-cresol > p-cresol. Electron-withdrawing groups at o/p increase acidity most (–R + –I).
- **Phenol vs alcohol:** phenol (10) is far more acidic than ethanol (~16) because the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilised. Phenol does **not** react with NaHCO₃; carboxylic acids do (CO₂ effervescence).
- **General order:** RCOOH > ArOH (phenol) > H₂O > ROH > HC≡CH > NH₃ > CH₂=CH₂ > CH₃–CH₃.
- **Alcohols:** CH₃OH > 1° > 2° > 3° (in solution).
- **Hydrocarbons:** HC≡CH (pKa ≈ 25) > CH₂=CH₂ (≈ 44) > CH₄ (≈ 50) — more s-character in sp carbon holds the anion's electrons closer.

**Basicity of amines**
- Aliphatic amines are stronger bases than NH₃ (+I effect). In **aqueous solution** (solvation + steric effects): methylamines **(CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > NH₃**; ethylamines **(C₂H₅)₂NH > (C₂H₅)₃N > C₂H₅NH₂ > NH₃**. In the **gas phase** simply 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃.
- **Aniline is much weaker** (pKb 9.38) than NH₃ (4.75) — lone pair delocalised into the ring. Substituents: p-toluidine > aniline > p-nitroaniline; diphenylamine and triphenylamine are weaker still.
- **Amides** (RCONH₂) are almost neutral (lone pair delocalised onto C=O).

## 9. Purification of organic compounds

| Method | Principle | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Crystallisation | Different solubility at high and low temperature | Sugar, benzoic acid (from water) |
| Sublimation | Solid → vapour directly | Camphor, naphthalene, anthracene, benzoic acid |
| Simple distillation | Large b.p. difference | Chloroform (334 K) + aniline (457 K) |
| Fractional distillation | Small b.p. difference | Petroleum fractions, acetone + methanol |
| Distillation under reduced pressure (vacuum) | Liquid boils at lower T at low pressure; avoids decomposition | **Glycerol** (decomposes at its b.p.), glycerol from spent lye in soap industry, sugarcane juice concentration |
| **Steam distillation** | Liquid + water boil when **sum of vapour pressures** = atmospheric pressure, i.e. below 100 °C; for substances that are **steam-volatile and immiscible with water** | **Aniline**, **o-nitrophenol** (from p-nitrophenol), essential oils (eucalyptus, rose), turpentine, nitrobenzene, bromobenzene |
| Differential extraction | Different solubility in two immiscible solvents (separating funnel) | Extracting organic compound from water with ether |
| Chromatography | Different adsorption or partition between stationary and mobile phase | Plant pigments, dyes, amino acids, drugs |

**Why steam distillation separates o- and p-nitrophenol:** o-nitrophenol has an **intramolecular H-bond** (chelation), so it does not associate with other molecules or with water → lower b.p., steam-volatile. p-Nitrophenol forms **intermolecular H-bonds** → high b.p., not steam-volatile, stays behind.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) asked: "Which is the best suited method for the separation of para- and ortho-nitrophenols from 1 : 1 mixture? (A) Crystallization (B) Chromatography (C) Steam distillation". Correct: **(C) Steam distillation** (ortho distils over). Chromatography could also separate them on a small scale, but steam distillation is the standard textbook method. (No official key in our folder.)

### Chromatography

- Invented by **Mikhail Tswett (1906)** to separate plant pigments (chlorophylls, carotenoids). Name = "colour writing".
- **Adsorption chromatography:** column chromatography (alumina/silica gel column) and **thin-layer chromatography (TLC)** (silica on glass plate). Different compounds are adsorbed to different extents.
- **Partition chromatography:** **paper chromatography** — water held in the paper fibres is the stationary phase; the moving solvent is the mobile phase.
- Gas chromatography (GC) and HPLC are modern instrumental versions.
- **Retardation factor Rf = distance moved by the compound / distance moved by the solvent front** (always < 1). Colourless spots are seen with UV light, iodine vapour or ninhydrin (amino acids → purple).

**Worked numerical 1.** In TLC, solvent front 8.0 cm, spot 3.0 cm. Rf = 3.0/8.0 = **0.375**.

## 10. Detecting and estimating elements

**Lassaigne's test (sodium fusion extract):** organic compound fused with Na converts N, S, X into NaCN, Na₂S, NaX.

| Element | Test on extract | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | Boil with FeSO₄, acidify with H₂SO₄ | **Prussian blue** Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃ |
| Sulphur | Sodium nitroprusside | **Violet/purple** colour |
| Sulphur | Lead acetate + acetic acid | Black PbS |
| N and S together | FeCl₃ (NaSCN formed) | **Blood red** |
| Halogens | Boil with HNO₃ (removes CN⁻, S²⁻), add AgNO₃ | White AgCl (soluble in NH₃), pale yellow AgBr (partly soluble), yellow AgI (insoluble) |
| Halogens (quick) | **Beilstein test** — heat on copper wire | Green flame (not given by fluorides) |

Hydrazine (NH₂NH₂) has no carbon, so it fails Lassaigne's nitrogen test.

**Quantitative methods:** C and H — **Liebig's** combustion (CO₂ absorbed in KOH, H₂O in anhydrous CaCl₂); N — **Dumas** (N₂ gas measured) and **Kjeldahl** (N → (NH₄)₂SO₄ → NH₃ titrated; not for nitro, azo compounds or N in rings like pyridine); halogens and S — **Carius method** (fuming HNO₃ + AgNO₃ / BaCl₂); oxygen — usually by difference.

| Method | Formula |
|---|---|
| Kjeldahl % N | % N = 1.4 × (molarity × basicity of acid) × V(mL) / mass of compound (g) |
| Carius % Cl | % Cl = (35.5/143.5) × mass of AgCl × 100 / mass of compound |
| Carius % S | % S = (32/233) × mass of BaSO₄ × 100 / mass of compound |

**Worked numerical 2 (Kjeldahl).** 0.50 g of compound; ammonia evolved neutralised 10 mL of 0.5 M H₂SO₄. % N?
mmol H⁺ = 10 × 0.5 × 2 = 10 → 10 mmol NH₃ = 10 mmol N = 0.14 g. % N = 0.14/0.50 × 100 = **28%** (formula: 1.4 × 1 × 10/0.5 = 28).

## 11. Spectroscopy basics

- **Spectroscopic methods** (UV–visible, IR, NMR, mass) are fast, need only milligram or microgram samples, are usually non-destructive, and give structural detail. Classical (wet chemical) methods need more time and larger samples.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) asked which statement is correct; the right one is **(C) spectroscopic methods require less time and less amount of sample than classical methods**. (No official key in our folder.)

- **IR spectroscopy** detects functional groups by bond vibrations; positions are given as **wavenumber (cm⁻¹)**, usually 4000–400 cm⁻¹. Absorptions appear as dips ("peaks" pointing down) in % transmittance.

| Bond / group | Approximate wavenumber (cm⁻¹) | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| O–H (alcohol, H-bonded) | 3200–3600 | Broad, strong |
| O–H (carboxylic acid) | 2500–3300 | Very broad |
| N–H (amine, amide) | 3300–3500 | Medium (1° amine shows two bands) |
| C–H (sp, ≡C–H) | ~3300 | Sharp |
| C–H (sp², =C–H, aromatic) | 3000–3100 | — |
| C–H (sp³, alkane) | 2850–2960 | — |
| C≡N | 2210–2260 | Sharp, medium |
| C≡C | 2100–2260 | Weak |
| **C=O (carbonyl)** | **≈ 1700 (1650–1780)** | **Very strong, sharp** — ketone ~1715, aldehyde ~1725, ester ~1735–1750, acid ~1710, amide ~1650–1690, acid chloride ~1800 |
| C=C (alkene) | 1620–1680 | Medium-weak |
| C–O | 1000–1300 | Strong |
| Fingerprint region | below ~1500 (1500–400) | Unique for each compound |

Stronger bonds and lighter atoms vibrate at higher wavenumber: C≡C > C=C > C–C; C–H (~3000) > C–D (~2200).

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) asked: "A carbonyl group will cause a sharp dip at about ___ cm⁻¹. (A) 1700 (B) 2800 (C) 3400". Correct: **(A) 1700**. (2800 is aldehyde/alkyl C–H region; 3400 is O–H/N–H.)

**Worked numerical 3.** Convert 1700 cm⁻¹ to wavelength. λ = 1/1700 cm = 5.88 × 10⁻⁴ cm = **5.88 μm** (infrared).

- **UV–visible:** detects conjugated π systems (λmax increases with conjugation; β-carotene with 11 conjugated C=C is orange).
- **¹H NMR:** reference **TMS (tetramethylsilane), δ = 0 ppm**; aldehyde H ~9–10 ppm, carboxylic H ~10–12 ppm, aromatic H ~7 ppm.
- **Mass spectrometry:** gives molecular mass (molecular ion peak M⁺) and fragments.

## Traps and exceptions

- **Count carbons first** in isomer questions: (CH₃)₃COH (C₄H₁₀O) is the isomer of diethyl ether.
- Diphenylmethane gives **4** monochloro isomers (CH₂, o, m, p), not 7 or 8.
- **(C₆H₅)₃C⁺ beats (CH₃)₃C⁺ and allyl cation.** Carbanion stability is the reverse of carbocation stability.
- Carbocation is sp² planar; carbanion is sp³ pyramidal.
- **R₃N, NH₃, H₂O are nucleophiles; SO₃, BF₃, NO₂⁺ are electrophiles.**
- Nucleophilicity of halides: **I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ in protic solvents**, reversed in aprotic.
- **SN2 → inversion; SN1 → racemisation.** Benzyl/allyl/3° → SN1; methyl/1° → SN2. Aryl and vinyl halides → neither.
- Aqueous KOH substitutes; alcoholic KOH eliminates.
- α-Cl acid is stronger than β-Cl acid. Formic acid is stronger than acetic acid. Benzoic acid is stronger than acetic acid.
- Phenol does not give CO₂ with NaHCO₃; picric acid does (it is strong enough).
- In water, 2° amine is the strongest base among methylamines; 3° is not the strongest.
- **o-Nitrophenol is steam-volatile** (intramolecular H-bond); p-nitrophenol is not.
- Glycerol is purified by **vacuum distillation**, not steam distillation.
- Rf is always less than 1 and has no unit.
- Kjeldahl method fails for nitro, azo and pyridine nitrogen.
- C=O stretch is ~1700 cm⁻¹; O–H ~3400 cm⁻¹; fingerprint below 1500 cm⁻¹.
- Glycine is not optically active; meso-tartaric acid is optically inactive despite two chiral carbons.

## Quick revision

1. Wöhler (1828) synthesised urea from ammonium cyanate.
2. Homologues differ by –CH₂– (14 u).
3. Principal group priority: COOH > SO₃H > COOR > COCl > CONH₂ > CN > CHO > C=O > OH > NH₂.
4. Picric acid = 2,4,6-trinitrophenol; aspirin = acetylsalicylic acid.
5. (CH₃)₃COH = 2-methylpropan-2-ol, isomer of diethyl ether.
6. C₅H₁₂ has 3 isomers; C₆H₁₄ has 5.
7. Monochloro-diphenylmethane: 4 isomers.
8. Tartaric acid: 3 stereoisomers (d, l, meso).
9. Stereoisomers ≤ 2ⁿ.
10. Staggered ethane most stable; chair cyclohexane most stable.
11. −I order: NO₂ > CN > COOH > F > Cl > Br > I.
12. Hyperconjugation = no-bond resonance (Baker–Nathan).
13. Carbocation: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺; (C₆H₅)₃C⁺ most stable.
14. Carbanion: CH₃⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3°.
15. Nucleophiles: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, R₃N; electrophiles: NO₂⁺, SO₃, BF₃.
16. Protic solvents: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ nucleophilicity.
17. SN1: first order, racemisation, 3°/benzyl; SN2: second order, Walden inversion.
18. Saytzeff: more substituted alkene major.
19. CH₃CHClCOOH > ClCH₂CH₂COOH > CH₃CH₂COOH.
20. Acidity: RCOOH > phenol > H₂O > ROH > HC≡CH > NH₃.
21. Aqueous basicity: (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > NH₃ > aniline.
22. Steam distillation: aniline, o-nitrophenol, essential oils.
23. Tswett (1906) — chromatography; Rf = compound distance / solvent distance.
24. Lassaigne: Prussian blue (N), violet with nitroprusside (S), blood red (N + S).
25. IR: C=O ≈ 1700 cm⁻¹, O–H ≈ 3200–3600 cm⁻¹, C≡N ≈ 2250 cm⁻¹.
