This chapter is the foundation of the whole book: who discovered what in cell biology, how we see cells, and how a bacterial cell differs from an animal cell. The base is NCERT class 11 Chapter "Cell: The Unit of Life"; the B.Sc. layer is microscopy (resolution, TEM sample preparation, stains) and the fine structure of the bacterial cell. TRE asks it as one-liners — "Schleiden and Schwann proposed …", "in TEM, ultrathin sections are stained by …".

> **In simple words:** Every living thing is made of cells, and every cell comes from an older cell. Cells are too small for the eye, so we use microscopes — light microscopes show the cell and its nucleus, electron microscopes show the tiny parts inside. Bacteria (prokaryotes) have no true nucleus and no membrane-covered organelles; animals, plants, fungi and protists (eukaryotes) have both. Learn the names with years, the size scale, and the prokaryote–eukaryote table.

For the class 9–10 version of this topic, see [Cell Structure and Biomolecules](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-9-10-science/biology/cell-structure-and-biomolecules).

## 1. Units and the size scale

| Unit | Value | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Millimetre (mm) | 10⁻³ m | Eggs, large protozoa |
| Micrometre (µm, "micron") | 10⁻⁶ m | Cells, bacteria, mitochondria |
| Nanometre (nm) | 10⁻⁹ m | Membranes, ribosomes, viruses |
| Ångström (Å) | 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.1 nm | Membrane layers, atoms, DNA |

So **1 µm = 1000 nm = 10,000 Å** and **1 nm = 10 Å**. A membrane 75 Å thick is 7.5 nm.

| Cell or structure | Size (approximate) |
|---|---|
| **Mycoplasma (PPLO)** — smallest free-living cell | **0.1–0.3 µm** (NCERT: "as small as 0.3 µm") |
| Typical bacterium | 1–2 µm wide, 2–5 µm long (NCERT: 3–5 µm) |
| Human red blood cell | about **7 µm** diameter |
| Typical eukaryotic cell | 10–100 µm |
| Human egg (ovum) | about 100 µm (0.1 mm) — largest human cell, just visible to the eye |
| **Ostrich egg** | largest single cell (yolk about 15 cm) |
| **Nerve cell (neuron)** | **longest cell** (axon can be over 1 m) |
| Ribosome | 20–30 nm (about 25 nm for 80S) |
| Viruses | 20–300 nm (not cells) |

> **Memory trick:** "**M**ini **B**ugs **R**ide **E**ggs" — Mycoplasma < Bacteria < RBC < Egg.

## 3. The cell theory

**Schleiden (1838) and Schwann (1839)** together proposed the **cell theory (cell doctrine)**:
1. All living organisms are composed of cells and products of cells.
2. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

Their theory did not explain **how new cells form** (Schleiden wrongly thought cells crystallise out of a "free cell formation"). **Virchow (1855)** added: **all cells arise from pre-existing cells** (*Omnis cellula e cellula*).

**Modern cell theory (NCERT form):**
- All living organisms are composed of cells and products of cells.
- All cells arise from pre-existing cells.
- (Modern additions) The cell contains hereditary information (DNA) that is passed on during division; all cells have basically the same chemical composition; energy flow (metabolism) happens inside cells.

**Exceptions / problems with the cell theory:** **viruses** (no cell structure, live only inside host cells); **coenocytic** forms such as *Rhizopus*, *Vaucheria* and **syncytial** tissues like striated muscle fibres (many nuclei with no walls between them); mature **RBCs** of mammals and **sieve tubes** of plants (no nucleus, yet living); bacteria and some protists are a whole organism in one cell.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q130 asked: "Schleiden and Schwann proposed — (A) phenomenon of Brownian movement (B) protoplasm is physical basis of life (C) cell theory or cell doctrine." **Official answer: (C).** Brownian movement belongs to Robert Brown (1827, pollen particles); "physical basis of life" is Huxley's phrase.

> **Remember:** Robert Brown gave us **two** things — the **nucleus (1831)** and **Brownian movement (1827)**. Hooke saw **dead** cells; Leeuwenhoek saw **living** cells.

## 4. How we see cells — microscopy

### Resolution and magnification

- **Magnification** = how many times bigger the image is. **Resolution (resolving power)** = the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as two separate points. Resolution, not magnification, decides how much detail we see.
- **Abbe's formula:** limit of resolution **d = 0.61 λ / NA** (Rayleigh form), where λ = wavelength of light and NA = numerical aperture (n sin θ). Shorter wavelength → better (smaller) d.
- Visible light λ ≈ 400–700 nm, so the best light microscope resolves about **0.2 µm (200 nm)**. Electrons have a wavelength of a few picometres, so the EM resolves about **0.1–0.2 nm** in theory and about **1–2 nm** for biological samples.

<div class="formula">

| Instrument | Resolution (limit) | Useful magnification |
|---|---|---|
| Human eye | about 0.1 mm (100 µm) | — |
| Light (compound) microscope | about **0.2 µm** | up to about 1500× (oil immersion) |
| Scanning electron microscope (SEM) | about **1–10 nm** | up to about 100,000× or more |
| Transmission electron microscope (TEM) | about **0.1–0.2 nm** (atomic); ~1–2 nm for cells | up to about 1,000,000× |

</div>

### Types of light microscope

| Microscope | Principle | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Bright-field | Light passes through a stained section | Routine histology |
| **Phase-contrast** (Frits **Zernike**, 1930s; Nobel 1953) | Converts differences in refractive index into differences in brightness | **Living, unstained cells** — mitosis in live cells |
| Dark-field | Only scattered light reaches the eye | Thin bacteria such as *Treponema* (syphilis) |
| Fluorescence | Fluorescent dyes or GFP glow under UV/blue light | Locating particular proteins |
| **Confocal** (laser scanning) | Laser + pinhole removes out-of-focus light | Sharp optical sections, 3-D images |
| Polarising | Uses birefringence | Spindle fibres, muscle A-band ("anisotropic") |

The **A-band** of striated muscle is named because it is **anisotropic** (bright in polarised light) and the **I-band** because it is **isotropic** — see [Muscles, Skeleton and Movement](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/physiology/muscles-skeleton-and-movement).

### Electron microscopes

| Point | **TEM** (transmission) | **SEM** (scanning) |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Electron beam passes **through** an ultrathin section | Beam scans the **surface**; secondary electrons are collected |
| Image | 2-D, internal **fine structure (ultrastructure)** | **3-D surface view** |
| Sample | Ultrathin sections (about **50–100 nm**) | Whole specimen, dried and coated with gold/palladium |
| Stain/coat | **Heavy metals: uranyl acetate and lead citrate** | Sputter-coated with **gold** (or gold–palladium) |
| Resolution | Higher (~0.2 nm) | Lower (~1–10 nm) |
| Inventors | Knoll and Ruska, 1931–32 | Manfred von Ardenne (1937); commercial 1965 |

**Preparing a sample for TEM (B.Sc. sequence):**
1. **Fixation** — **glutaraldehyde** (fixes proteins) followed by **osmium tetroxide (OsO₄)** (fixes and stains lipids/membranes).
2. **Dehydration** — graded alcohol or acetone.
3. **Embedding** — in hard **epoxy resin** (Araldite, Epon) instead of paraffin wax.
4. **Sectioning** — **ultramicrotome** with a **glass or diamond knife**, giving ultrathin sections of about 50–100 nm (silver–gold interference colour); sections are picked up on a copper grid.
5. **Staining** — "**double staining**" with **uranyl acetate and lead citrate**. Heavy atoms scatter electrons, so stained parts look dark (**electron-dense**).
6. Special methods: **negative staining** (phosphotungstic acid / uranyl acetate around viruses and ribosomes), **freeze-fracture / freeze-etching** (splits the lipid bilayer to show membrane proteins — strong evidence for the fluid mosaic model), **shadow casting**.

**Stains for the light microscope** (for contrast with TEM):
- **Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E)** — the routine light-microscope stain: haematoxylin (basic) stains nuclei blue-purple; eosin (acidic) stains cytoplasm pink.
- **Toluidine blue** (often 1% in 1% **borax**) — stains **semi-thin (0.5–1 µm)** resin sections that are checked under the light microscope before cutting ultrathin sections for TEM.
- Others: acetocarmine and Feulgen (DNA — magenta, Feulgen 1924), Janus green B (vital stain for **mitochondria**), neutral red (vacuoles), Sudan III/IV (fats), iodine (starch), PAS (glycogen), Gram's stain (bacteria), Giemsa/Leishman (blood).

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q77 asked: "In transmission electron microscopy, ultrathin sections are stained by — (A) uranyl acetate and lead citrate (B) hematoxylin and eosin (C) toluidine blue in 1% borax." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (A).** H&E is for paraffin sections under the light microscope; toluidine blue–borax is for semi-thin sections viewed under the light microscope.

> **Memory trick:** **T**EM sections are "**U**-**L**ead" — **U**ranyl acetate + **L**ead citrate. **S**EM is **S**urface, coated with gold.

### Cell fractionation

Cells are broken (homogenised) and spun at increasing speeds — **differential centrifugation**. Heavier parts settle first: **nuclei** (about 600–1000 g) → **mitochondria, lysosomes, peroxisomes** (about 10,000–20,000 g) → **microsomes** (fragments of ER with ribosomes, about 100,000 g) → **ribosomes** (very high speed). **Density-gradient centrifugation** (sucrose or caesium chloride) separates by density — used by Meselson and Stahl. Sedimentation speed is given in **Svedberg units (S)** — named after **The Svedberg**, who built the ultracentrifuge (Nobel 1926). S values are **not additive** (50S + 30S = 70S).

## 5. Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

**Prokaryotes** (*pro* = before, *karyon* = nucleus): bacteria, cyanobacteria (blue-green algae), mycoplasma, archaebacteria. **Eukaryotes** (*eu* = true): protists, fungi, plants, animals. The terms were popularised by **Édouard Chatton (1925/1937)**.

<div class="formula">

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Usually 1–10 µm | Usually 10–100 µm |
| Nucleus | **No nuclear envelope** — DNA lies in the **nucleoid** (genophore) | True nucleus with double-membrane envelope and nucleolus |
| DNA | Single **circular** DNA, **no histones** (archaea have histone-like proteins); extra **plasmids** | Several **linear** chromosomes with **histones** (nucleosomes) |
| Membrane-bound organelles | **Absent** (no mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, chloroplasts) | Present |
| Ribosomes | **70S** (50S + 30S) | **80S** (60S + 40S) in cytoplasm; 70S in mitochondria and chloroplasts |
| Respiration site | Plasma membrane (and mesosomes) | Mitochondria |
| Cell wall | **Peptidoglycan (murein)** in bacteria; absent in mycoplasma | Cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), none in animals |
| Cytoskeleton | Simple (FtsZ, MreB — tubulin- and actin-like) | Well developed (microtubules, microfilaments, intermediate filaments) |
| Flagellum | Single protein **flagellin**, rotates, no 9+2 | **9+2** microtubules, whips, covered by membrane |
| Cell division | **Binary fission** (amitotic) | Mitosis and meiosis |
| Transcription–translation | **Coupled** (both in cytoplasm at the same time) | Separated (nucleus, then cytoplasm) |
| mRNA | Polycistronic; no cap, no poly-A tail, no introns (as a rule) | Monocistronic; capped, poly-A tailed, spliced |
| Sterols in membrane | Absent (except mycoplasma) | Present (cholesterol in animals) |

</div>

### The bacterial cell in detail

- **Cell envelope** = **glycocalyx** (outer) + **cell wall** + **plasma membrane**. Glycocalyx is a loose **slime layer** or a thick, tough **capsule** (capsule helps disease — e.g. smooth S-strain *Streptococcus pneumoniae* in Griffith's experiment).
- **Gram staining** (Christian **Gram**, 1884): crystal violet → iodine → alcohol → safranin. **Gram-positive** bacteria keep the violet colour (thick peptidoglycan, teichoic acids; e.g. *Bacillus*, *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*). **Gram-negative** bacteria lose it and look pink/red (thin peptidoglycan plus an **outer membrane** with lipopolysaccharide; e.g. *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Vibrio*).
- **Mesosome** — infolding of the plasma membrane (vesicles, tubules, lamellae). NCERT functions: cell wall formation, DNA replication and distribution to daughter cells, respiration, secretion, increasing membrane surface and enzyme content. (Modern note: many cell biologists regard mesosomes as an artefact of chemical fixation — mention only if asked.)
- **Chromatophores** — membranous extensions with pigments in cyanobacteria and photosynthetic bacteria.
- **Flagella** — filament + hook + **basal body**; rotate like a propeller. **Pili** (longer; sex pilus for conjugation) and **fimbriae** (small bristles for attachment) do not help in swimming.
- **Inclusion bodies** — reserve material lying free in the cytoplasm, not bounded by membranes: phosphate granules (volutin/metachromatic granules), cyanophycean granules, glycogen granules; **gas vacuoles** in blue-green, purple and green photosynthetic bacteria.
- **Plasmids** — small circular extra-chromosomal DNA giving special traits such as **antibiotic resistance**; used as **vectors** in genetic engineering.
- **Mycoplasma** — no cell wall, smallest cell, can live without oxygen; causes pleuropneumonia in cattle (PPLO = pleuropneumonia-like organisms).

A eukaryotic cell has three regions: **plasma membrane**, **cytoplasm** (cytosol + organelles + cytoskeleton) and **nucleus**. Organelles can be grouped as:
- **Endomembrane system** (functions are coordinated): **ER, Golgi complex, lysosomes, vacuoles** (and the nuclear envelope). **Mitochondria, chloroplasts and peroxisomes are NOT part of it** — their functions are not coordinated with the others.
- **Double-membrane organelles:** nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts.
- **Single-membrane organelles:** ER, Golgi, lysosome, vacuole, peroxisome, glyoxysome.
- **Non-membranous structures:** ribosomes, centrioles, cytoskeleton, nucleolus.
- **Semi-autonomous organelles** (own circular DNA + 70S ribosomes, divide by fission): **mitochondria and chloroplasts**.

Details of every organelle are in [Cell Organelles and Cytoskeleton](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/cell-genetics/cell-organelles-and-cytoskeleton).

### Plant cell versus animal cell

| Plant cell | Animal cell |
|---|---|
| Cellulose **cell wall** present | No cell wall |
| Plastids (chloroplast, chromoplast, leucoplast) present | Absent |
| One large **central vacuole** (tonoplast around it) | Small, temporary vacuoles, or none |
| **Centrioles absent** in higher plants (spindle forms without them — anastral) | **Centrioles present** (astral spindle) |
| Cytokinesis by **cell plate** (phragmoplast) | Cytokinesis by **furrowing** (cleavage) |
| Glyoxysomes in fatty seedlings | No glyoxysomes |
| Stores **starch** | Stores **glycogen** |
| Plasmodesmata connect cells | Gap junctions connect cells |

### Other cell types worth knowing

- **Archaebacteria** — prokaryotes with **ether-linked branched lipids** in the membrane, no peptidoglycan; halophiles, thermoacidophiles, methanogens (in cattle rumen; make **biogas**).
- **Mesokaryon** — dinoflagellates have permanently condensed chromosomes without typical histones; sometimes called "mesokaryotic".
- **Cells without nucleus:** mature mammalian RBC, platelets, lens fibre cells, sieve tube elements.
- **Multinucleate:** striated muscle fibre (syncytium), osteoclasts, *Opalina*, *Paramecium* (two nuclei — macronucleus and micronucleus).

## 6. Why cells stay small — surface area to volume

A cell takes in food and O₂ and throws out wastes through its **surface**, but its needs depend on its **volume**. When a cell grows, volume rises faster (as the cube of length) than surface (as the square), so the **surface-area-to-volume ratio falls**.

<div class="formula">

**Worked example (cube-shaped cells):**

| Side | Surface area (6 × side²) | Volume (side³) | SA : V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 µm | 6 µm² | 1 µm³ | **6 : 1** |
| 2 µm | 24 µm² | 8 µm³ | **3 : 1** |
| 4 µm | 96 µm² | 64 µm³ | **1.5 : 1** |

Doubling the side halves the SA : V ratio. So large cells either divide, become long and thin (neurons), flat (RBC — biconcave disc), or fold their surface (microvilli of intestinal cells).

</div>

The nucleus also controls only a limited volume of cytoplasm (**Kernplasma relation**, the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio of R. Hertwig) — another reason a cell divides when it becomes large.

### Shapes of cells and what they suit

| Shape | Example | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Biconcave disc | Human RBC | More surface for gas exchange; squeezes through capillaries |
| Long branched | Neuron | Carries impulses over long distances |
| Spindle | Smooth muscle fibre | Contracts along its length |
| Columnar with microvilli | Intestinal epithelium | Absorption |
| Irregular, changing | Amoeba, WBC | Amoeboid movement, phagocytosis |
| Flat (squamous) | Lining of alveoli, blood vessels | Short diffusion path |
| Tadpole-like with tail | Sperm | Swimming |

## Traps and exceptions

- Hooke's cells were **dead** (cork, only walls). Leeuwenhoek was the first to see **living** cells and bacteria.
- **Schleiden was a botanist, Schwann a zoologist.** Virchow added the "cells from cells" idea — the original theory lacked it.
- **Resolution** of a microscope is the **smallest distance** it can separate — a **smaller** number means **better** resolution. Magnification without resolution is "empty magnification".
- TEM gives **internal detail** of thin sections; SEM gives a **3-D surface**. A question on "3-D image of cell surface" → SEM.
- In TEM, dark areas are **electron-dense** (heavy metal stained) — e.g. the two dark lines of the unit membrane.
- **H&E is a light-microscope stain**, not an EM stain. Janus green B is a **vital** stain for mitochondria (stains living cells).
- Svedberg values are **not additive**: 60S + 40S = **80S**, not 100S.
- **Mitochondria and chloroplasts are not part of the endomembrane system.**
- **Mycoplasma** has **no cell wall** and is resistant to penicillin; it has sterols in its membrane (an exception for prokaryotes).
- Prokaryotes **do** have ribosomes and a plasma membrane; they lack only **membrane-bound** organelles.
- **Gram-negative** cells have the **thinner** peptidoglycan but an extra **outer membrane** — so they are more resistant to many antibiotics and lysozyme.
- Viruses are **not** cells and are an exception to cell theory.
