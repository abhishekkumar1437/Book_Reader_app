The plasma membrane decides what enters and leaves the cell, so TRE asks about both its **structure** (which model, how thick, what it is made of) and its **transport** (diffusion, carriers, pumps). NCERT class 11 gives the fluid mosaic model and the basic transport types; the B.Sc. layer adds the older models with their numbers, carrier kinetics, the sodium–potassium pump and vesicle traffic.

> **In simple words:** The membrane is a thin double layer of fat-like molecules (phospholipids) with proteins floating in it like icebergs in the sea. Small, fat-soluble things slip straight through; ions and sugars need protein "doors" (channels and carriers); moving something uphill against its gradient needs energy (ATP) and a "pump". Big things enter by being swallowed in a bubble of membrane (endocytosis) and leave the same way (exocytosis).

## 1. Chemical composition

- Main components: **lipids** (mostly **phospholipids**, plus **cholesterol** in animal cells and glycolipids), **proteins**, and a little **carbohydrate** (always on the **outer** face, attached to lipids or proteins as glycolipids/glycoproteins).
- **Human RBC membrane: about 52% protein and 40% lipid** (NCERT figure). The ratio varies by membrane:

| Membrane | Protein | Lipid | Remark |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Myelin sheath** | about 18% | about **79%** | Lipid-rich — insulates the axon |
| RBC plasma membrane | about 52% | about 40% | NCERT value |
| **Inner mitochondrial membrane** | about **76%** | about 24% | Protein-rich — electron transport chain, ATP synthase |

- **Phospholipids are amphipathic**: a **hydrophilic (polar) head** (phosphate + glycerol + choline/ethanolamine/serine) and two **hydrophobic (non-polar) fatty-acid tails**. In water they form a **bilayer** with heads outside and tails inside.
- Main membrane phospholipids: **phosphatidylcholine (lecithin)**, phosphatidylethanolamine (cephalin), phosphatidylserine (kept on the inner leaflet; appears outside in cells undergoing apoptosis), phosphatidylinositol, and **sphingomyelin** (rich in myelin).
- **Cholesterol** sits between phospholipids and acts as a **fluidity buffer** — it makes the membrane less fluid at high temperature and stops it from freezing solid at low temperature. Plants have phytosterols (stigmasterol, sitosterol); bacteria lack sterols (except mycoplasma).
- **Membrane proteins:** **integral (intrinsic)** — buried in or spanning the bilayer (**transmembrane** proteins such as channels, carriers, receptors); removed only by detergents. **Peripheral (extrinsic)** — loosely attached to the surface; removed by salt solutions.
- **Glycocalyx (cell coat)** — the sugar layer outside; used in **cell recognition**, blood group antigens (A, B), and adhesion.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q131 asked: "Which one of the following is a constituent of cell membrane? (A) Cholesterol (B) Glycolysis (C) Phospholipid (D) More than one of the above." **Official answer: (D)** — both cholesterol and phospholipid. Glycolysis is a metabolic pathway, not a molecule — a deliberate distractor.

## 2. Membrane models — the history

| Year | Scientist(s) | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| 1855 | Nägeli and Cramer | Coined "cell membrane" (plasma membrane term: Plowe 1931) |
| 1895–99 | **Charles Overton** | Fat-soluble substances enter cells easily → membrane is made of **lipid** |
| 1917 | Irving Langmuir | Lipids spread on water as a **monolayer** with heads in water |
| 1925 | **Gorter and Grendel** | Lipid extracted from RBC membranes covered **twice** the cell surface → **lipid bilayer** |
| 1935 | **Danielli and Davson** | **"Sandwich" (trilamellar) model**: lipid bilayer coated on both sides by continuous layers of globular protein (protein–lipid–lipid–protein); later added protein-lined pores |
| 1959 | **J. D. Robertson** | **Unit membrane model** — all biological membranes (plasma membrane and organelle membranes) share the same three-layered structure seen in EM |
| 1966 | Benson; Green | Lipoprotein subunit models (now rejected) |
| **1972** | **S. J. Singer and G. L. Nicolson** | **Fluid mosaic model** — accepted model today |

### Robertson's unit membrane (1959)

Under TEM after osmium staining, every membrane looks like a "railway track": **two dark (electron-dense) lines separated by one light (electron-lucent) line**.

<div class="formula">

**Unit membrane dimensions (the classic textbook values):**

- Outer dense band (protein + polar heads) = **20 Å (2 nm)**
- Middle light band (lipid tails) = **35 Å (3.5 nm)**
- Inner dense band = **20 Å (2 nm)**
- **Total thickness = 20 + 35 + 20 = 75 Å (7.5 nm)**

(Modern measurements give 6–10 nm for most membranes; 7.5 nm is the value examiners use.)

</div>

Robertson interpreted the dark bands as **protein layers** (as in Danielli–Davson) and the light band as the lipid bilayer, and said this "unit" is universal. Weakness: membranes differ greatly in function and protein content, which a single uniform unit cannot explain; freeze-fracture later showed proteins **inside** the bilayer.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q78 asked: "In unit membrane model, which of the following is the thickness of each dense band? (A) 20 Å (B) 25 Å (C) 30 Å." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (A) 20 Å.** The light middle band is 35 Å; the whole membrane is 75 Å.

> **Memory trick:** **"20–35–20 = 75"** — like a sandwich: two thin slices of bread (dense, 20 each) and a thick filling (light, 35).

### The three models side by side

| Point | Danielli–Davson (1935) | Robertson unit membrane (1959) | Singer–Nicolson fluid mosaic (1972) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lipid | Bilayer | Bilayer | Bilayer (fluid) |
| Protein position | Continuous sheets **on both surfaces** | Continuous layers on both surfaces (the dark bands) | **Globular proteins embedded** in and through the bilayer, patchy (mosaic) |
| Movement | Static | Static | **Lateral movement** of lipids and proteins |
| Same for all membranes? | — | **Yes — universal "unit"** | No — composition varies by membrane |
| Evidence | Surface tension of cells lower than pure oil | TEM "railway track" (dark–light–dark) | Freeze-fracture, Frye–Edidin cell fusion |
| Status | Rejected | Rejected (but the EM image is real) | **Accepted** |

Main points:
1. The membrane is a **fluid lipid bilayer** ("sea") in which **globular proteins are embedded** like a "mosaic" of icebergs.
2. **Lateral (sideways) movement** of lipids and proteins is fast — lipids change places with neighbours about 10⁷ times a second. Evidence: **Frye and Edidin (1970)** fused a mouse cell and a human cell; their differently labelled surface proteins mixed completely within about 40 minutes at 37 °C.
3. **Transverse "flip-flop"** (from one leaflet to the other) is **very rare** for lipids (needs flippase enzymes) and essentially never happens for proteins.
4. The membrane is **asymmetric**: the two leaflets differ in lipids; carbohydrates are only on the outside.
5. Fluidity depends on **temperature**, **unsaturated fatty acids** (kinks increase fluidity), short chains (more fluid) and **cholesterol** (buffer).

Fluidity is needed for cell growth, formation of intercellular junctions, **secretion**, **endocytosis**, cell division and movement of membrane proteins.

> **Remember:** NCERT asks "the quasi-fluid nature of lipid enables lateral movement of proteins within the overall bilayer — this is measured as **fluidity**."

## 3. Functions of the plasma membrane

Selective permeability; transport; receptors for hormones and neurotransmitters; cell recognition (glycocalyx); cell adhesion and junctions; electrical excitability (resting potential about −70 mV in neurons); enzymes (in bacteria, respiration happens on the membrane); and boundary formation for endocytosis and exocytosis.

## 4. Transport across the membrane

<div class="formula">

| Type | Energy (ATP)? | Direction | Protein needed? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple diffusion** | No | High → low concentration (down gradient) | No | O₂, CO₂, ethanol, urea, steroid hormones |
| **Osmosis** | No | Water from low solute (high water potential) → high solute | Through bilayer or **aquaporins** | Water uptake by cells |
| **Facilitated diffusion** | **No** | **Down** the gradient | **Yes — carrier or channel** | Glucose into RBC (GLUT1), ions through channels |
| **Primary active transport** | **Yes, directly** | **Against** the gradient (uphill) | Yes — **pump (ATPase)** | **Na⁺/K⁺ pump**, Ca²⁺ pump, H⁺ pump |
| **Secondary active transport** | Indirect (uses Na⁺ gradient made by ATP) | One substance uphill, coupled to Na⁺ moving down | Yes — co-transporter | Glucose with Na⁺ in intestine and PCT (SGLT) |
| **Bulk transport** | Yes | — | Membrane vesicles | Endocytosis, exocytosis |

</div>

### Simple diffusion

Movement of molecules from higher to lower concentration until equal (equilibrium). Rate depends on the **concentration gradient**, temperature, size of molecule, lipid solubility and membrane area (**Fick's law**: rate ∝ area × gradient / thickness). Non-polar and small molecules cross easily; ions and large polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) hardly cross the bare bilayer.

### Facilitated diffusion

Uses a membrane protein but **no ATP**; still goes **down** the gradient. Two kinds of proteins:
- **Channel proteins** — water-filled pores; may be always open or **gated** (voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels of neurons, ligand-gated acetylcholine receptors). **Aquaporins** — water channels (**Peter Agre**, Nobel 2003).
- **Carrier proteins (permeases, transporters)** — bind the molecule, change shape and release it on the other side (e.g. **GLUT** glucose transporters).

**How facilitated diffusion differs from simple diffusion:**
1. **Carrier/channel is required.**
2. **Specific (stereospecific)** — the carrier recognises a particular shape; D-glucose is carried, L-glucose is not.
3. **Saturation kinetics** — the rate rises with concentration only up to a maximum (V_max) when all carriers are busy; simple diffusion keeps rising in a straight line.
4. Can be **inhibited** by similar molecules (competition) and by protein poisons.
5. **Faster** than simple diffusion for the same gradient.

What they **share**: both need a **concentration gradient** and both are **passive** (no ATP).

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q79 asked: "Facilitated diffusion differs from simple diffusion by — (A) concentration gradient (B) stereospecific (C) requirement of a carrier." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (D) More than one of the above** — that is, (B) and (C). Concentration gradient is common to both, so (A) is not a difference.

### Osmosis and tonicity

**Osmosis** = diffusion of **water** across a **semi-permeable membrane** from a dilute solution (more water) to a concentrated solution (less water). The pressure needed to stop it is **osmotic pressure** (π = CRT, van 't Hoff). Term "osmosis" — Abbé Nollet (1748) first observed it; Dutrochet named it (1820s).

| Outside solution | Water moves | Animal cell (RBC) | Plant cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypotonic** (more dilute than cell) | **Into** the cell | Swells and bursts — **haemolysis** | Becomes turgid (wall prevents bursting) |
| **Isotonic** (same as cell; 0.9% NaCl for human cells) | No net movement | Normal | Flaccid |
| **Hypertonic** (more concentrated) | **Out of** the cell | Shrinks — **crenation** | **Plasmolysis** (protoplast pulls away from wall) |

- **Endosmosis** — water enters; **exosmosis** — water leaves. **Deplasmolysis** — plasmolysed cell recovers in water.
- Normal saline (0.9% NaCl) and 5% glucose are **isotonic** with human blood — used in drips.

### Active transport and the Na⁺/K⁺ pump

**Active transport** moves substances **against** their concentration gradient using energy, through specific **pumps** (carrier proteins with ATPase activity). It shows saturation and specificity like carriers, and stops if ATP production is poisoned (cyanide, low O₂).

<div class="formula">

**Sodium–potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase)** — discovered by **Jens Christian Skou (1957)**, Nobel Prize in Chemistry **1997**.

- For **each ATP** hydrolysed: **3 Na⁺ are pumped out** and **2 K⁺ are pumped in**.
- Net result: one positive charge leaves per cycle → the pump is **electrogenic** and helps make the inside negative.
- Keeps **Na⁺ high outside** and **K⁺ high inside** the cell; maintains the **resting membrane potential** (with K⁺ leak channels), cell volume and the Na⁺ gradient used for secondary transport.
- Mechanism: 3 Na⁺ bind inside → ATP phosphorylates the pump (P-type ATPase) → shape change releases Na⁺ outside → 2 K⁺ bind → dephosphorylation → K⁺ released inside.
- Inhibited by **ouabain** and **digitalis (digoxin)** — the basis of digoxin's action on heart muscle.
- Uses about one-third of a resting animal cell's ATP (up to two-thirds in nerve cells).

</div>

Other pumps: **Ca²⁺-ATPase** of sarcoplasmic reticulum (relaxes muscle by pumping Ca²⁺ back), **H⁺/K⁺-ATPase** of parietal cells of the stomach (makes HCl; blocked by omeprazole), **H⁺ pump** of lysosome and plant tonoplast (V-type ATPase).

**Kinds of carriers by direction:**
- **Uniport** — one substance, one direction (GLUT).
- **Symport (co-transport)** — two substances, **same** direction (Na⁺–glucose symporter SGLT in intestine and kidney PCT).
- **Antiport (counter-transport)** — two substances, **opposite** directions (Na⁺/K⁺ pump; Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger; Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger of RBC — the **chloride shift**).

> **Common mistake:** The Na⁺/K⁺ pump is an **antiport and primary active** transporter. Na⁺–glucose co-transport is **symport and secondary active** — it uses no ATP directly.

### Comparison: passive versus active

| Point | Passive (simple and facilitated) | Active |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Down the gradient | Against the gradient |
| Energy | Kinetic energy of molecules | ATP (or ion gradient) |
| Carrier | Only in facilitated | Always |
| Stops when | Concentrations become equal | ATP runs out |
| Effect of metabolic poisons | None | Stops |

## 5. Bulk transport — endocytosis and exocytosis

**Endocytosis** — the membrane folds in and pinches off a vesicle containing outside material.
- **Phagocytosis ("cell eating")** — solid particles, bacteria; forms a **phagosome** that fuses with lysosomes. By *Amoeba*, neutrophils, macrophages (Metchnikoff discovered phagocytosis, 1882).
- **Pinocytosis ("cell drinking")** — droplets of fluid; term by W. H. Lewis (1931).
- **Receptor-mediated endocytosis** — specific molecules bind receptors gathered in **clathrin-coated pits** (e.g. **LDL cholesterol**, iron–transferrin, insulin). **Brown and Goldstein** (LDL receptor; Nobel 1985). Defect in the LDL receptor → familial hypercholesterolaemia.
- **Potocytosis / caveolae** — small flask-shaped pits with caveolin.

**Exocytosis (emeiocytosis, cell vomiting)** — vesicles from the Golgi fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents: hormones, enzymes, neurotransmitters (acetylcholine at synapses), mucus. It also adds new membrane to the cell surface. **Transcytosis** = endocytosis on one side + exocytosis on the other (e.g. **IgG** crossing the placenta, IgA into milk).

> **In simple words:** Phagocytosis = eating, pinocytosis = drinking, exocytosis = throwing out. All need ATP and a flexible (fluid) membrane.

## 6. Modifications of the membrane and cell junctions

| Structure | What it is | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Microvilli** | Finger-like folds that increase surface; form the **brush border** | Intestinal epithelium, PCT of nephron |
| **Tight junction (zonula occludens)** | Membranes fused; **stops leakage** between cells | Intestine, **blood–brain barrier**, kidney tubules |
| **Adhering junction (zonula adherens)** | Belt of cadherins linked to actin; holds cells together | Epithelia |
| **Desmosome (macula adherens)** | Spot "rivet" with intermediate (keratin) filaments | Skin, heart muscle |
| **Hemidesmosome** | Half desmosome; anchors cell to basement membrane | Epidermis |
| **Gap junction** | Channels made of **connexons** (6 connexins each); let ions and small molecules pass — **electrical coupling** | **Cardiac muscle (intercalated discs)**, smooth muscle, some neurons |
| **Plasmodesmata** | Cytoplasmic bridges through plant cell walls | Plant cells |
| **Interdigitations** | Interlocking folds | Some epithelia |

NCERT class 11 (Animal Tissues): **tight junctions** stop substances leaking across a tissue; **adhering junctions** cement neighbouring cells together; **gap junctions** let cells communicate by connecting their cytoplasm for rapid transfer of ions, small molecules and sometimes big molecules.

## 7. Membrane potential (link to nerve physiology)

Because of the Na⁺/K⁺ pump and **more open K⁺ leak channels**, the inside of a resting neuron is about **−70 mV** compared with outside. When voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ rushes in (**depolarisation** — action potential); K⁺ then leaves (**repolarisation**). Details are in [Nervous System and Sense Organs](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/physiology/nervous-system-and-sense-organs).

## 8. Numbers that make transport easy to remember

### Ion concentrations inside and outside a mammalian cell (approximate)

| Ion | Outside (ECF), mM | Inside (cytosol), mM | Which side is higher |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Na⁺** | about 145 | about 12 | **Outside** (about 12 times) |
| **K⁺** | about 4 | about 140 | **Inside** (about 35 times) |
| Cl⁻ | about 110 | about 5–15 | Outside |
| Ca²⁺ | about 1–2 | about 0.0001 (10⁻⁷ M) | Outside (about 10,000 times) |

> **Memory trick:** "**Sodium sits outside, Potassium stays Put inside.**" The pump keeps it that way: it throws Na⁺ out and brings K⁺ in.

### Worked problem — counting pump cycles

A neuron must pump out **3000 Na⁺** ions that entered during activity. How many ATP and how many K⁺?

- Each cycle: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, 1 ATP.
- Cycles = 3000 ÷ 3 = **1000 cycles** → **1000 ATP** used, **2000 K⁺** brought in.
- Net charge moved out per cycle = 3 − 2 = 1 positive charge → **1000 net positive charges** leave (electrogenic effect).

### Worked problem — will the cell swell or shrink?

A human cell (internal osmolarity about 300 mOsm/L) is placed in:
- **150 mM NaCl** → NaCl gives 2 particles → about 300 mOsm/L → **isotonic** → no change (0.9% NaCl ≈ 154 mM).
- **100 mM NaCl** → about 200 mOsm/L → **hypotonic** → water enters → cell swells, may lyse.
- **300 mM NaCl** → about 600 mOsm/L → **hypertonic** → water leaves → cell shrinks (crenation).

### Kinetics — simple versus carrier-mediated transport

| Concentration outside | Simple diffusion rate | Facilitated diffusion rate |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Low | Low |
| Medium | Medium (rises in a straight line) | Rises, then bends |
| High | Keeps rising | **Levels off (saturation)** — all carriers busy |

The carrier curve looks like the Michaelis–Menten curve of an enzyme (see [Biomolecules and Enzymes](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/cell-genetics/biomolecules-and-enzymes)); it has a **K_m** (concentration at half-maximum rate) and a **V_max**.

## 9. Membrane receptors and signalling (B.Sc. outline)

- **Water-soluble hormones** (peptides like insulin, glucagon; amines like adrenaline) cannot cross the bilayer — they bind **receptors on the plasma membrane** and act through **second messengers**: **cAMP** (Earl Sutherland, Nobel 1971, with adrenaline/glucagon), IP₃ and DAG, Ca²⁺.
- **G-protein-coupled receptors** (seven-pass transmembrane proteins) — e.g. adrenaline receptor (Rodbell and Gilman, G-proteins, Nobel 1994).
- **Receptor tyrosine kinases** — e.g. insulin receptor.
- **Ion-channel receptors** — e.g. nicotinic acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction.
- **Lipid-soluble hormones** (steroids, thyroxine) cross the membrane and bind **intracellular (nuclear) receptors**, acting directly on genes.

Details of hormone action are in [Endocrine System and Hormones](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/physiology/endocrine-system-and-hormones).

## Traps and exceptions

- **Facilitated diffusion uses carriers but no ATP.** Do not call it active transport.
- A **concentration gradient** is needed for both simple and facilitated diffusion — it is **not** a difference between them.
- **Unit membrane dense band = 20 Å; light band = 35 Å; total 75 Å.** Do not mix them.
- Danielli–Davson = **sandwich** (1935); Robertson = **unit membrane** (1959); Singer–Nicolson = **fluid mosaic** (1972). Gorter–Grendel (1925) gave the bilayer, not the fluid mosaic.
- In the fluid mosaic model, **lateral** movement is common; **flip-flop is rare**.
- Carbohydrates of the membrane are **only on the outer surface**.
- Cholesterol is found in **animal** membranes; it is absent from most bacteria (mycoplasma excepted).
- **Na⁺/K⁺ pump: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, 1 ATP.** Many students reverse it.
- In a **hypertonic** solution, RBCs **shrink (crenate)**; in a **hypotonic** solution they **burst (haemolysis)**. Plant cells **plasmolyse** in hypertonic solution.
- **Pinocytosis** is drinking, **phagocytosis** is eating; both are forms of **endocytosis**.
- Gap junctions are **communication** junctions; tight junctions are **sealing** junctions; desmosomes are **anchoring** junctions.
- Myelin has the **most lipid**; the inner mitochondrial membrane has the **most protein**.

20. Who discovered phagocytosis? — **Élie Metchnikoff** (1882).
21. Which phospholipid appears on the outer leaflet when a cell undergoes apoptosis? — **Phosphatidylserine**.
22. What is transcytosis? — **Endocytosis on one side of a cell followed by exocytosis on the other** (e.g. IgG across the placenta).
