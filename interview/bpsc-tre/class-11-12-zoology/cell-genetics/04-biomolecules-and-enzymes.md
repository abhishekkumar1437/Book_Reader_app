Biochemistry questions in TRE are short: "which is a simple protein", "RNA lacks which base", "the energy-rich compound produced in respiration". NCERT class 11 "Biomolecules" is the base; the B.Sc. layer adds protein classification (simple versus conjugated — exactly what TRE 1.0 asked), sugar linkages, Chargaff calculations, the six (now seven) enzyme classes, Km and types of inhibition.

> **In simple words:** A cell is mostly water, plus four big families of molecules: **proteins** (chains of amino acids — workers and building blocks), **carbohydrates** (sugars and starches — fuel and structure), **lipids** (fats, oils, membrane molecules — stored energy and boundaries), and **nucleic acids** (DNA and RNA — information). **Enzymes** are protein catalysts that speed up reactions without being used up; each works best at a certain temperature and pH.

## 1. What a cell is made of

| Component | Percent of total cellular mass (NCERT, approximate) |
|---|---|
| **Water** | **70–90** |
| Proteins | 10–15 |
| Carbohydrates | 3 |
| Lipids | 2 |
| Nucleic acids | 5–7 |
| Ions | 1 |

- **Acid-soluble pool** (filtrate after grinding tissue in **trichloroacetic acid**) — small molecules (molecular weight below about 1000 Da): amino acids, sugars, nucleotides, ions — "micromolecules".
- **Acid-insoluble fraction** (retentate) — **proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides** and **lipids**. Lipids are small (below 800 Da) but come here because membranes break into insoluble vesicles. These are the "macromolecules" (above 10,000 Da, except lipids).
- **Most abundant protein in the animal body: collagen.** **Most abundant protein in the biosphere: RuBisCO.** **Most abundant organic compound on Earth: cellulose.**

## 2. Amino acids

- General formula: a central **α-carbon** bonded to **H**, an **amino group (–NH₂)**, a **carboxyl group (–COOH)** and a variable **R group**. **20** standard amino acids are coded by DNA (plus selenocysteine and pyrrolysine in a few proteins).
- All except **glycine** (R = H) are **optically active (chiral)**; proteins use **L-amino acids**.
- In water at neutral pH, an amino acid exists as a **zwitterion** (⁺H₃N–CH(R)–COO⁻). The pH at which net charge is zero is the **isoelectric point (pI)** — solubility is least there.

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Neutral, non-polar | Glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, proline (an imino acid), phenylalanine, methionine, tryptophan |
| Neutral, polar | Serine, threonine, cysteine, tyrosine, asparagine, glutamine |
| **Acidic** (monoamino-dicarboxylic) | **Glutamic acid**, **aspartic acid** |
| **Basic** (diamino-monocarboxylic) | **Lysine**, **arginine**, histidine |
| Aromatic | Phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan |
| Sulphur-containing | **Cysteine**, **methionine** (cystine = two cysteines linked by an S–S bond) |
| Hydroxy | Serine, threonine (tyrosine has an –OH on the ring) |

- **Essential amino acids** (cannot be made by humans; must come in food) — **9 for adults: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine**. **Arginine** is semi-essential (needed in diet by growing children).
- Amino acids are precursors of many molecules: tyrosine → thyroxine, adrenaline, melanin, dopamine; tryptophan → serotonin, niacin, melatonin; histidine → histamine; glycine → haem (with succinyl-CoA), creatine, glutathione.

> **Memory trick:** Essential amino acids — "**PVT TIM HaLL**": Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, (Arginine — semi), Leucine, Lysine.

## 3. Proteins

**Peptide bond** — a covalent –CO–NH– bond formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next, with loss of water (**dehydration/condensation**). Named by **Emil Fischer (1902)**. A protein is a **heteropolymer** of amino acids; the chain has an **N-terminal** (left, first amino acid) and a **C-terminal** end.

### Four levels of structure

<div class="formula">

| Level | What it is | Bonds that hold it | Famous example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | **Sequence** of amino acids | Peptide (covalent) bonds | **Insulin** — sequenced by **Frederick Sanger** (1953–55; Nobel 1958); 51 amino acids in two chains: A (21) and B (30) joined by 2 disulphide bonds |
| **Secondary** | Local folding into **α-helix** or **β-pleated sheet** | **Hydrogen bonds** between backbone C=O and N–H | α-helix — **Pauling and Corey (1951)**: **right-handed**, **3.6 amino acids per turn**, pitch 5.4 Å; **keratin** (hair, wool). β-sheet — **silk fibroin** |
| **Tertiary** | 3-D folding of the whole chain into a globular shape — gives **biological activity** | H-bonds, ionic bonds, **disulphide bridges**, hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals | **Myoglobin** — first 3-D protein structure (**John Kendrew, 1958**; 153 amino acids) |
| **Quaternary** | Two or more **polypeptide subunits** assembled together | Same weak bonds (sometimes S–S) | **Haemoglobin** — **2 α (141 aa each) + 2 β (146 aa each)** = 574 amino acids, solved by **Max Perutz** (Nobel 1962 with Kendrew) |

</div>

- **NCERT note:** in the primary structure the left end is the first amino acid (N-terminal) and the right end the last (C-terminal). Only the tertiary (or quaternary) structure is biologically active.
- **Anfinsen (1957)** unfolded and refolded **ribonuclease** to show that the primary sequence decides the 3-D shape (Nobel 1972).
- **Denaturation** — loss of secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure (by heat, strong acid/alkali, urea, heavy metals) with the primary structure intact; activity is lost (boiled egg albumin).
- **Collagen** — a **triple helix** of three chains rich in **glycine (every third residue), proline and hydroxyproline**; hydroxylation needs **vitamin C** (deficiency → scurvy).
- **Fibrous proteins** (insoluble, structural): collagen, keratin, elastin, fibroin, myosin. **Globular proteins** (soluble, functional): enzymes, haemoglobin, albumin, antibodies, insulin.

### Classification by composition — simple, conjugated, derived

| Class | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple proteins** | Give **only amino acids** on hydrolysis | **Albumins** (serum albumin, egg albumin/ovalbumin), **globulins** (serum globulins, myosin), **histones** (basic, in chromatin), **protamines** (in fish sperm), **glutelins** (glutenin of wheat), **prolamines** (gliadin, zein), **scleroproteins/albuminoids** (**collagen, keratin, elastin**) |
| **Conjugated proteins** | Protein + a non-protein **prosthetic group** | **Glycoproteins** (sugar) — **thyroglobulin**, mucin, immunoglobulins, FSH/LH; **Chromoproteins** (coloured group) — **haemoglobin** (haem), cytochromes, rhodopsin; **Lipoproteins** — LDL, HDL; **Nucleoproteins** — chromatin, ribosomes; **Phosphoproteins** — **casein** (milk), vitellin (yolk); **Metalloproteins** — **caeruloplasmin** (copper), ferritin (iron), carbonic anhydrase (zinc); **Flavoproteins** — FAD-containing enzymes |
| **Derived proteins** | Breakdown products | Proteoses, peptones, peptides; denatured proteins (coagulated albumin) |

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q87 asked: "Which of the following is an example of a simple protein? (A) Thyroglobulin (B) Haemoglobin (C) Caeruloplasmin." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (E) None of the above.** All three are **conjugated**: thyroglobulin is an iodinated **glycoprotein**, haemoglobin is a **chromoprotein** (haem), caeruloplasmin is a copper-containing **metalloprotein** (also a glycoprotein). Simple protein examples would be albumin, globulin, histone, keratin or collagen.

## 4. Carbohydrates

General formula (CH₂O)ₙ — "hydrates of carbon"; chemically **polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones**.

| Class | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Monosaccharides** | **Trioses**: glyceraldehyde (aldose), dihydroxyacetone (ketose). **Pentoses**: **ribose** (RNA), **deoxyribose** (DNA), ribulose. **Hexoses**: **glucose** (aldose — "dextrose", blood sugar), **fructose** (ketose — fruit sugar, sweetest natural sugar), **galactose** | Simplest sugars; cannot be hydrolysed further |
| **Disaccharides** (two monosaccharides joined by a **glycosidic bond**) | **Sucrose** = glucose + fructose (α1→β2) — **non-reducing**, cane sugar, transport sugar of plants. **Maltose** = glucose + glucose (α1→4) — malt sugar. **Lactose** = galactose + glucose (β1→4) — milk sugar. Cellobiose = glucose + glucose (β1→4) | Maltose and lactose are **reducing** (give Benedict's test) |
| **Oligosaccharides** | Raffinose (trisaccharide), sugar chains on glycoproteins | 3–10 units |
| **Polysaccharides** | See table below | Long chains; storage or structure |

| Polysaccharide | Monomer and bond | Where / job | Iodine test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Starch** = **amylose** (unbranched, α1→4, helical; ~20%) + **amylopectin** (branched, α1→4 with α1→6 at branch points; ~80%) | α-D-glucose | Plant storage | **Blue-black** (iodine sits inside the amylose helix) |
| **Glycogen** ("animal starch") | α-D-glucose, α1→4 with **more frequent α1→6 branches** (every 8–12 units) | Animal storage — **liver and muscle**; also fungi | **Red-brown** |
| **Cellulose** | **β-D-glucose, β1→4**, unbranched, straight chains with H-bonds | Plant cell wall; **cotton** is nearly pure cellulose | **No colour** (no helix) |
| **Inulin** | **Fructose** polymer | Dahlia tubers; used to measure **GFR** | — |
| **Chitin** | **N-acetylglucosamine**, β1→4 | **Exoskeleton of arthropods**, fungal cell walls — second most abundant polysaccharide | — |
| **Peptidoglycan (murein)** | NAG + NAM with short peptides | Bacterial cell wall | — |
| **Heparin** | Sulphated glucosamine + uronic acid | **Anticoagulant**, from mast cells and liver | — |
| **Hyaluronic acid** | Glucuronic acid + N-acetylglucosamine | Lubricant in joints, vitreous humour, "cement" of tissues | — |
| Chondroitin sulphate | — | Cartilage, bone | — |

## 5. Lipids

Lipids are **insoluble in water** and soluble in organic solvents (ether, chloroform). They are not strictly macromolecules (molecular weight usually under 800 Da).

- **Fatty acids** — a long hydrocarbon chain with a –COOH group. **Saturated** (no double bonds): **palmitic acid (C16, 16 carbons including the carboxyl carbon — NCERT)**, **stearic acid (C18)**. **Unsaturated**: **oleic acid** (C18, one double bond), **linoleic** (C18, two), **linolenic** (C18, three), **arachidonic** (C20, four).
- **Essential fatty acids** for humans: **linoleic** (ω-6) and **α-linolenic** (ω-3); arachidonic acid can be made from linoleic acid and is the precursor of **prostaglandins**.
- **Triglycerides (neutral fats)** — glycerol + 3 fatty acids (ester bonds). **Fats** are solid at room temperature (more saturated; animal fats); **oils** are liquid (more unsaturated; plant oils). Exception: **coconut oil and palm oil** are highly saturated.
- **Phospholipids** — glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate + a nitrogenous group: **lecithin (phosphatidylcholine)** — most common membrane lipid. **Sphingolipids** (sphingomyelin) — in nerve tissue.
- **Steroids** — four fused rings (cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene): **cholesterol** (only in animals; precursor of **steroid hormones**, **vitamin D** and **bile salts**), testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone, cortisol, aldosterone, ecdysone (insect moulting hormone).
- **Waxes** — long-chain fatty acid + long-chain alcohol: beeswax, lanolin (wool fat), cutin.
- **Lipoproteins** in blood: chylomicrons (largest, least dense — carry dietary fat), VLDL, **LDL ("bad cholesterol")**, **HDL ("good cholesterol"** — carries cholesterol back to liver).
- Tests: **Sudan III/IV** stain fats red; a grease spot on paper; **saponification** with alkali gives soap; **iodine number** measures unsaturation; rancidity is oxidation of unsaturated fats.

## 6. Nucleotides and nucleic acids

<div class="formula">

- **Nucleoside** = nitrogenous base + pentose sugar (e.g. adenosine, uridine, cytidine, guanosine, thymidine).
- **Nucleotide** = nucleoside + **phosphate** (e.g. adenylic acid, AMP).
- **Purines** (two rings): **adenine (A), guanine (G)**. **Pyrimidines** (one ring): **cytosine (C), thymine (T), uracil (U)**.
- **DNA** has deoxyribose and A, G, C, **T**. **RNA** has ribose and A, G, C, **U** — **RNA lacks thymine**.
- Nucleotides are joined by **3′→5′ phosphodiester bonds**; the chain has a 5′ (phosphate) end and a 3′ (OH) end.

</div>

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q134 asked: "RNA lacks which nitrogen base? (A) Thymine (B) Cytosine (C) Uracil." **Official answer: (A) Thymine.** Uracil replaces thymine in RNA. (A rare exception: tRNA contains a little ribothymidine as a modified base — not examined.)

**Chargaff's rules (Erwin Chargaff, 1950)** for double-stranded DNA:
1. **A = T** and **G = C** (molar amounts).
2. Total purines = total pyrimidines: **A + G = C + T**.
3. The ratio **(A + T)/(G + C)** varies from species to species (base composition is species-specific).

**Worked problems:**
- A DNA sample has **20% adenine**. Then T = 20%; A + T = 40%; G + C = 60%; so **G = 30%, C = 30%**.
- A DNA molecule of **1000 base pairs** has **300 cytosines**. Then G = 300; A + T = 2000 − 600 = 1400 bases; **A = T = 700**. Hydrogen bonds = (700 × 2) + (300 × 3) = 1400 + 900 = **2300**.
- **Single-stranded DNA** (bacteriophage **φX174**) and RNA do **not** follow Chargaff's A = T, G = C rule.

**Watson–Crick double helix (1953)** — based on X-ray pictures by **Rosalind Franklin** and **Maurice Wilkins**; Watson, Crick and Wilkins got the Nobel Prize in **1962**.
- Two **antiparallel** (one 5′→3′, other 3′→5′) polynucleotide strands, **right-handed** helix.
- **A pairs with T by 2 hydrogen bonds; G pairs with C by 3** — so GC-rich DNA melts at a higher temperature.
- Sugar–phosphate backbone outside, bases inside, stacked.
- **Diameter 2 nm (20 Å)**; **pitch 3.4 nm (34 Å)**; **10 bp per turn** (10.5 in solution); distance between base pairs **0.34 nm (3.4 Å)**.

| Form | Handedness | bp per turn | Remark |
|---|---|---|---|
| **B-DNA** | Right | 10 | Normal form in cells (Watson–Crick) |
| A-DNA | Right | 11 | Dehydrated DNA; RNA–DNA hybrids |
| **Z-DNA** | **Left** | 12 | Zig-zag backbone, GC repeats (Alexander Rich, 1979) |

**RNA types:** **mRNA** (messenger; copies the gene), **tRNA** (transfer; adaptor that brings amino acids; **clover-leaf** 2-D shape (Robert Holley, 1965), **inverted L** 3-D shape; anticodon loop and amino-acid acceptor end **CCA-3′**), **rRNA** (ribosomal; **most abundant RNA, about 80%** of cellular RNA), plus snRNA, miRNA, siRNA. tRNA is the smallest (about 75–90 nucleotides; also called soluble RNA).

## 7. ATP and the energy currency

- **ATP (adenosine triphosphate)** = adenine + ribose + three phosphate groups. Discovered by **Karl Lohmann (1929)**; called the "**energy currency**" of the cell by **Fritz Lipmann (1941)**.
- The last two phosphates are linked by **high-energy phosphoanhydride bonds**; hydrolysis ATP → ADP + Pi releases about **7.3 kcal/mol (30.5 kJ/mol)** under standard conditions.
- Made mainly in **mitochondria** by **oxidative phosphorylation** during **aerobic respiration** (also substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis and Krebs cycle; photophosphorylation in chloroplasts).
- Other high-energy compounds: GTP (protein synthesis), UTP (glycogen synthesis), CTP (lipid synthesis), **creatine phosphate** (phosphagen of vertebrate muscle — regenerates ATP quickly), arginine phosphate (invertebrates), phosphoenolpyruvate (highest-energy phosphate in glycolysis).

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q135 asked: "The energy-rich compound produced through respiration is — (A) ATP (B) ADP (C) pyruvic acid." **Official answer: (A) ATP.** Pyruvic acid is a product of glycolysis but not the energy carrier; ADP is the low-energy form.

## 9. Enzymes — what they are

- Word **"enzyme"** (Greek "in yeast") — **Wilhelm Kühne (1878)**. **Eduard Buchner (1897)** showed that a **cell-free yeast extract** ferments sugar ("zymase"; Nobel 1907). **James Sumner (1926)** crystallised **urease** from jack bean and proved enzymes are **proteins** (Nobel 1946).
- Almost all enzymes are proteins; **ribozymes** are catalytic RNAs (**Thomas Cech** and **Sidney Altman**, Nobel 1989) — e.g. self-splicing introns, RNase P, and the **peptidyl transferase (23S rRNA)** of the ribosome.
- Enzymes speed up reactions by **lowering the activation energy**; they do **not** change the equilibrium or the free-energy change (ΔG), and come out unchanged.
- Enzyme–substrate complex (E + S ⇌ ES → EP → E + P) — the **active site** binds the substrate.
- **Lock-and-key hypothesis** — **Emil Fischer (1894)**: the active site is rigid and exactly fits the substrate. **Induced-fit hypothesis** — **Daniel Koshland (1958)**: binding of substrate changes the shape of the active site to fit better (accepted today).
- **Turnover number** — molecules of substrate converted per enzyme molecule per second. NCERT: **carbonic anhydrase** speeds CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ about **10 million times** (about 600,000 molecules per second with enzyme versus about 200 per hour without). Catalase is among the fastest (millions per second).

### Cofactors

| Type | Bond to enzyme | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Prosthetic group** | **Tightly** bound (organic) | **Haem** in **peroxidase and catalase** (NCERT example); FAD in succinate dehydrogenase |
| **Coenzyme** | **Loosely**, transiently bound (organic; often vitamin-derived) | **NAD⁺ and NADP⁺** (from **niacin**), FAD/FMN (riboflavin), coenzyme A (pantothenic acid), TPP (thiamine), PLP (pyridoxine — transaminases), biotin (carboxylases), THF (folic acid) |
| **Metal ion activator** | Coordination bonds | **Zn²⁺** for **carboxypeptidase** (NCERT) and carbonic anhydrase; Mg²⁺ for kinases; Fe, Cu for cytochromes |

**Holoenzyme = apoenzyme (protein part) + cofactor.** The apoenzyme alone is inactive.

## 10. Classification of enzymes (IUBMB)

<div class="formula">

| EC class | Reaction type | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Oxidoreductases** (dehydrogenases, oxidases) | Oxidation–reduction (transfer of H or electrons): S reduced + S′ oxidised → S oxidised + S′ reduced | Alcohol dehydrogenase, lactate dehydrogenase, **cytochrome oxidase**, catalase, peroxidase |
| **2. Transferases** | Transfer of a group (not H) from one substrate to another | **Hexokinase** (phosphate), **transaminases** (amino group — GPT/ALT, GOT/AST) |
| **3. Hydrolases** | Breaking bonds by adding **water** — ester, ether, peptide, glycosidic, C–C, P–N bonds | **Pepsin, trypsin, amylase, lipase, urease**, phosphatases |
| **4. Lyases** | Removal of groups **without hydrolysis**, leaving double bonds (or adding to double bonds) | **Aldolase**, fumarase, **carbonic anhydrase**, decarboxylases |
| **5. Isomerases** | Inter-conversion of optical, geometric or positional **isomers** | Phosphoglucoisomerase, triose phosphate isomerase, mutases, epimerases |
| **6. Ligases** (synthetases) | **Joining** two molecules using **ATP** (C–O, C–S, C–N, P–O bonds) | **DNA ligase**, pyruvate carboxylase, glutamine synthetase, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase |
| **7. Translocases** (added 2018) | Moving ions or molecules **across membranes** | ATP synthase, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (re-classified) |

</div>

> **Memory trick:** "**O**ver **T**he **H**ill **L**ives **I**ndia's **L**ion **T**iger" — Oxidoreductase, Transferase, Hydrolase, Lyase, Isomerase, Ligase, Translocase. NCERT and most exam keys still use **six classes**.

## 11. Factors affecting enzyme activity

- **Temperature** — activity rises up to an **optimum** (about **37 °C** for human enzymes), then falls sharply because the enzyme is **denatured** by heat. **Low temperature** only inactivates **temporarily** (reversible) — the basis of refrigeration. Thermophile enzymes work at 70–100 °C (**Taq polymerase** from *Thermus aquaticus*, used in PCR). Q₁₀ ≈ 2 (rate doubles for every 10 °C rise, within limits).
- **pH** — each enzyme has an **optimum pH**: **pepsin about 2** (stomach), **salivary amylase about 6.8**, **trypsin about 8**, alkaline phosphatase about 9–10, acid phosphatase (lysosome) about 5.
- **Substrate concentration** — rate rises, then levels off at **V_max** when all active sites are occupied (saturation).
- **Enzyme concentration** — rate is proportional to enzyme amount when substrate is in excess.
- **Inhibitors and activators.**

<div class="formula">

**Michaelis–Menten equation (Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten, 1913):**

v = V_max × [S] / (K_m + [S])

- **K_m (Michaelis constant)** = the **substrate concentration at which the rate is half of V_max**.
- **Low K_m → high affinity** of enzyme for substrate (half-saturated at low [S]). High K_m → low affinity.
- K_m is a **constant for an enzyme–substrate pair** (at fixed pH and temperature); it does **not** depend on enzyme concentration. V_max **does** depend on enzyme concentration.

**Lineweaver–Burk (double-reciprocal) plot, 1934:** 1/v = (K_m/V_max)(1/[S]) + 1/V_max — a straight line.
- **y-intercept = 1/V_max**; **x-intercept = −1/K_m**; **slope = K_m/V_max**.

</div>

**Worked problem.** An enzyme has V_max = 100 µmol/min and K_m = 2 mM. Find v at different [S].

| [S] | Calculation | v | % of V_max |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 mM (= K_m) | 100 × 2 / (2 + 2) | **50** | 50% |
| 6 mM (= 3 K_m) | 100 × 6 / (2 + 6) | **75** | 75% |
| 18 mM (= 9 K_m) | 100 × 18 / (2 + 18) | **90** | 90% |
| 0.5 mM | 100 × 0.5 / 2.5 | **20** | 20% |

Rule of thumb: at [S] = K_m → ½ V_max; at 3 K_m → ¾; at 9 K_m → 90%. V_max is reached only at very high [S] (in theory, never exactly).

### Enzyme inhibition

<div class="formula">

| Type | How it works | K_m | V_max | Overcome by more substrate? | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Competitive** | Inhibitor **resembles the substrate** and competes for the **active site** | **Increases** (apparent) | **Unchanged** | **Yes** | **Malonate** inhibits **succinate dehydrogenase** (NCERT); **sulphonamides** vs PABA (bacterial folate); methotrexate on dihydrofolate reductase; **statins** on HMG-CoA reductase; ethanol given in methanol poisoning |
| **Non-competitive** | Binds a site **other than** the active site (on E or ES); changes enzyme shape | **Unchanged** | **Decreases** | No | Heavy metals (Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺, Ag⁺ binding –SH groups), cyanide on cytochrome oxidase (classically cited) |
| **Uncompetitive** | Binds **only the ES complex** | Decreases | Decreases | No | Lithium on inositol monophosphatase |
| **Irreversible** | Covalent binding destroys activity | — | Decreases | No | **DIFP/nerve gases** and organophosphate insecticides on **acetylcholinesterase**; **aspirin** acetylates cyclo-oxygenase; **penicillin** on bacterial transpeptidase |
| **Allosteric / feedback** | End product binds a regulatory (allosteric) site of the **first** enzyme of a pathway | Sigmoid (S-shaped) curve | — | — | Isoleucine inhibits threonine deaminase; ATP and citrate inhibit phosphofructokinase; CTP inhibits aspartate transcarbamoylase |

</div>

> **Memory trick:** "**Competitive Changes K**" — a **c**ompetitive inhibitor **c**hanges K_m, keeps V_max. "**Non-competitive Nicks the maximum**" — lowers V_max, keeps K_m.

### More enzyme terms

- **Zymogens (proenzymes)** — inactive precursors: pepsinogen (activated by HCl), trypsinogen (by enterokinase), prothrombin, chymotrypsinogen.
- **Isoenzymes (isozymes)** — different molecular forms of the same enzyme catalysing the same reaction: **lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) has 5 isozymes** (made of H and M subunits) — LDH-1 rises after a heart attack; creatine kinase (CK-MB in heart). Term by Markert and Møller (1959).
- **Allosteric enzymes** — regulatory site separate from the active site; sigmoid curve (Monod, Wyman and Changeux, 1965).
- **Coenzyme A** was discovered by Lipmann; **NAD** by Harden and Young (cozymase, 1906).
- **Abzymes** — catalytic antibodies. **Ribozymes** — catalytic RNA. **Constitutive** enzymes are always made; **inducible** enzymes are made only when the substrate is present (β-galactosidase of the lac operon).
- Enzymes used commercially: **streptokinase** (clot-buster from *Streptococcus*), **lipases** in detergents, **pectinase** for juice clarification, **rennet** (chymosin) for cheese, **restriction enzymes** and **DNA ligase** in genetic engineering.

## Traps and exceptions

- **Glycine** is the only standard amino acid that is **not optically active**.
- **Proline** is an **imino** acid; it breaks α-helices.
- **Haemoglobin, thyroglobulin and caeruloplasmin are conjugated proteins**, not simple. Albumins, globulins, histones and scleroproteins are simple.
- **Sucrose is non-reducing** (both anomeric carbons are tied up); maltose and lactose are reducing.
- **Cellulose (β1→4)** cannot be digested by humans; **starch and glycogen (α1→4, α1→6)** can.
- **Glycogen** gives **red-brown** with iodine; **starch** blue-black; **cellulose** no colour.
- **Chitin** is a polymer of **N-acetylglucosamine**, not glucose.
- Coconut oil is an oil that is mostly **saturated**.
- Lipids are not true macromolecules but appear in the **acid-insoluble** fraction.
- **RNA lacks thymine** (has uracil); DNA lacks uracil.
- **A–T: 2 H-bonds; G–C: 3 H-bonds.** Chargaff's rules apply only to **double-stranded** DNA.
- **Enzymes do not change equilibrium constant** — they only reach it faster.
- **K_m is not affected by enzyme concentration**; V_max is.
- **Competitive inhibitor: K_m up, V_max same; non-competitive: V_max down, K_m same.**
- High temperature **denatures** enzymes permanently; low temperature only **inactivates** them temporarily.
- **Ribozymes** are enzymes that are **not proteins**.
- NCERT gives **6 enzyme classes**; IUBMB added the 7th (**translocases**) in 2018.
- **Haem** of catalase/peroxidase is a **prosthetic group**; **NAD** is a **coenzyme**; **Zn²⁺** of carboxypeptidase is a **metal activator**.

## Quick revision

1. Water is 70–90% of cell mass; proteins 10–15%.
2. Most abundant animal protein — collagen; in biosphere — RuBisCO.
3. 20 amino acids; glycine is not optically active.
4. 9 essential amino acids for adults; arginine is semi-essential.
5. Peptide bond formed by dehydration; term by Emil Fischer.
6. Sanger sequenced insulin (51 aa: A 21 + B 30).
7. α-helix (Pauling and Corey) — right-handed, 3.6 residues per turn.
8. Myoglobin (Kendrew) — first tertiary structure; haemoglobin (Perutz) — quaternary, 2α + 2β.
9. Simple proteins — albumin, globulin, histone, keratin, collagen; conjugated — haemoglobin, thyroglobulin, caeruloplasmin, casein.
10. Sucrose = glucose + fructose (non-reducing); lactose = galactose + glucose.
11. Starch = amylose + amylopectin; glycogen is more branched; cellulose is β1→4.
12. Chitin — N-acetylglucosamine; inulin — fructose polymer.
13. Palmitic acid has 16 C; essential fatty acids — linoleic and linolenic.
14. Lecithin — main membrane phospholipid; cholesterol — precursor of steroid hormones, vitamin D, bile salts.
15. Purines A, G; pyrimidines C, T, U; RNA lacks thymine.
16. Chargaff: A = T, G = C, A + G = C + T.
17. B-DNA: 2 nm wide, 3.4 nm pitch, 10 bp/turn, right-handed; Z-DNA is left-handed.
18. rRNA is the most abundant RNA; tRNA is clover-leaf (Holley).
19. ATP — energy currency (Lipmann); hydrolysis gives about 7.3 kcal/mol.
20. Enzyme — Kühne 1878; Buchner — cell-free fermentation; Sumner — urease crystals.
21. Lock and key — Fischer; induced fit — Koshland.
22. Six classes: oxidoreductase, transferase, hydrolase, lyase, isomerase, ligase (+ translocase).
23. K_m = [S] at half V_max; low K_m = high affinity.
24. Competitive inhibition: K_m increases, V_max unchanged (malonate on succinate dehydrogenase).
25. Haem in catalase — prosthetic group; NAD — coenzyme; Zn²⁺ in carboxypeptidase — metal activator.

## Practice with answers

1. Which amino acid lacks a chiral carbon? — **Glycine** (R group is H).
2. What is a zwitterion? — **An amino acid carrying both + and − charges** (NH₃⁺ and COO⁻) at its isoelectric point.
3. Which bond stabilises the α-helix? — **Hydrogen bonds** between C=O and N–H of the backbone.
4. Which protein's 3-D structure was solved first? — **Myoglobin** (John Kendrew, 1958).
5. Is haemoglobin a simple or conjugated protein? — **Conjugated (chromoprotein — has haem)**.
6. Name a phosphoprotein of milk. — **Casein**.
7. Which polysaccharide forms the arthropod exoskeleton? — **Chitin**.
8. Which polysaccharide is used to measure GFR? — **Inulin**.
9. Which sugar is non-reducing? — **Sucrose**.
10. A DNA has 15% cytosine. What is the percentage of adenine? — **35%** (G = 15, so A + T = 70, A = 35).
11. How many hydrogen bonds join G and C? — **Three**.
12. Which RNA is most abundant in a cell? — **rRNA** (about 80%).
13. Who proved enzymes are proteins? — **James Sumner** (crystallised urease, 1926).
14. What is an apoenzyme? — **The protein part of an enzyme without its cofactor**.
15. In which class is DNA ligase placed? — **Ligases (class 6)**.
16. Carbonic anhydrase belongs to which class? — **Lyases (class 4)**.
17. What does a low K_m tell you? — **The enzyme has a high affinity for its substrate**.
18. In a Lineweaver–Burk plot, what does the y-intercept give? — **1/V_max**.
19. Effect of a competitive inhibitor on V_max? — **None — V_max is unchanged** (K_m rises).
20. Optimum pH of pepsin? — **About 2**.
