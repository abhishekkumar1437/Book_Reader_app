This chapter is NCERT class 12 "Human Health and Disease" (the immunity half) plus the B.Sc. immunology that TRE 1.0 touched (immunogen vs antigen). TRE 2.0 asked four straight questions here — which immunoglobulin crosses the placenta, who introduced the word vaccine, how AIDS is transmitted, which blood component makes antibodies — so learn the tables exactly.

> **In simple words:** Immunity is the body's power to fight germs. We are born with general defences (skin, stomach acid, white blood cells that eat germs, fever) — that is innate immunity. Later, when a germ enters, special white cells (B and T lymphocytes) learn to recognise that exact germ and remember it — that is acquired immunity. B cells make antibodies (Y-shaped proteins) that stick to germs; T cells kill infected cells and direct the response. Vaccines train this memory safely. HIV destroys the helper T cells, so the whole defence collapses — that is AIDS.

## 1. Types of immunity

| Innate (inborn, non-specific) | Acquired (adaptive, specific) |
|---|---|
| Present **at birth** | Develops **after exposure** to a pathogen or vaccine |
| **Non-specific** — same response to every invader | **Specific** to each antigen |
| **No memory** | Has **memory** — faster, stronger second response |
| Barriers, phagocytes, NK cells, interferons, inflammation, fever, complement | B and T lymphocytes, antibodies |
| Works within minutes to hours | Takes days the first time |

### 1.1 The four innate barriers (NCERT)

| Barrier | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Physical** | **Skin** (main barrier); **mucus coating** of the epithelium lining the respiratory, gastrointestinal and urogenital tracts traps microbes |
| **Physiological** | **Acid in the stomach** (HCl), **saliva** in the mouth, **tears** from the eyes (contain **lysozyme**) |
| **Cellular** | Certain WBCs — **polymorpho-nuclear leukocytes (PMNL — neutrophils)**, **monocytes**, **natural killer (NK) lymphocytes**, and **macrophages** in tissues — phagocytose and destroy microbes |
| **Cytokine** | **Interferons** secreted by **virus-infected cells** protect non-infected cells from further viral infection |

Other innate defences: **inflammation** (redness, heat, swelling, pain — histamine dilates vessels), **fever** (slows microbes), and the **complement system** (about 30 blood proteins that punch holes in bacteria).

### 1.2 Features of acquired immunity

1. **Specificity** — each antibody/T cell fits one antigen (epitope).
2. **Diversity** — billions of different B and T cell clones.
3. **Discrimination between self and non-self.**
4. **Memory** — the first encounter gives a **primary response** of low intensity; a later encounter with the same pathogen gives a highly intensified **secondary (anamnestic) response**, because memory cells were formed.

**Two arms:**

| Humoral immunity (antibody-mediated) | Cell-mediated immunity (CMI) |
|---|---|
| By **B lymphocytes** → plasma cells → **antibodies** in blood and lymph ("humor" = body fluid) | By **T lymphocytes** directly |
| Works against bacteria, toxins, viruses outside cells | Works against virus-infected cells, cancer cells, fungi, **graft rejection** |
| Can be transferred by serum | Transferred only by cells |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q82 asked: "Component of blood responsible for producing antibodies is — (A) lymphocyte (B) thrombocyte (C) RBC." **Official answer: (A) lymphocyte** — B lymphocytes, after becoming plasma cells.

## 2. Lymphoid organs

| Organ | Type | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Bone marrow** | **Primary** | Main lymphoid tissue; **all blood cells, including lymphocytes, are produced** here; **B cells mature** here (in mammals) |
| **Thymus** | **Primary** | Lobed organ near the heart, beneath the breastbone; **T cells mature** here; **quite large at birth and keeps reducing in size with age**, becoming very small by puberty and adulthood |
| **Spleen** | Secondary | Large bean-shaped organ; mainly lymphocytes and phagocytes; filters blood and traps blood-borne microbes; a large **reservoir of erythrocytes** ("graveyard of RBCs") |
| **Lymph nodes** | Secondary | Small solid structures along the lymphatic system; trap microbes from lymph and tissue fluid, and activate lymphocytes |
| **Tonsils, Peyer's patches** of small intestine, **appendix** | Secondary | Part of **MALT** |
| **MALT** (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue) | Secondary | Lymphoid tissue inside the lining of respiratory, digestive and urogenital tracts; makes up about **50% of the lymphoid tissue** in the human body |

In birds, B cells mature in the **bursa of Fabricius** (near the cloaca) — that is where the letter "B" comes from.

**Primary lymphoid organs** = where immature lymphocytes **differentiate** into antigen-sensitive lymphocytes. **Secondary lymphoid organs** = where they **meet antigens and proliferate** to become effector cells.

## 3. Immune cells

| Cell | Role |
|---|---|
| **Neutrophils** (most abundant WBC, 60–65%) | First phagocytes at infection site; pus is mostly dead neutrophils |
| **Monocytes → macrophages** | Phagocytes; **antigen-presenting cells (APCs)**; "HIV factory" in AIDS |
| **Dendritic cells** | Most efficient APCs; carry antigens to lymph nodes |
| **NK (natural killer) cells** | Innate killers of virus-infected and tumour cells |
| **B lymphocytes** | Become **plasma cells** (antibody factories) and memory B cells |
| **Helper T cells (T_H, CD4⁺)** | "Generals" — release cytokines that activate B cells and cytotoxic T cells; **target of HIV** |
| **Cytotoxic T cells (T_C, CD8⁺)** | Kill virus-infected and cancer cells; reject grafts |
| **Regulatory/suppressor T cells** | Stop the response when the job is over; prevent autoimmunity |
| **Memory B and T cells** | Long-lived; give secondary response |
| **Mast cells and basophils** | Release **histamine** and serotonin — allergy, inflammation |
| **Eosinophils** | Fight **parasitic worms**; rise in allergy |

Lymphocytes form about 20–25% of WBCs.

## 4. Antigens, immunogens and haptens

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Antigen** | Any substance that can **bind** specifically to an antibody or T-cell receptor |
| **Immunogen** | A substance that can **induce** an immune response by itself (all immunogens are antigens) |
| **Hapten** | A small molecule (e.g., penicillin, some drugs, dinitrophenol) that can bind an antibody (**antigenic**) but cannot provoke a response alone (**not immunogenic**) unless attached to a large carrier protein |
| **Epitope** (antigenic determinant) | The small part of an antigen that an antibody actually binds |
| **Paratope** | The antigen-binding site on the antibody |

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q65 asked: "Which of the following statements is true? — (A) All immunogens are antigens but all antigens are not immunogens. (B) All immunogens are antigens and all antigens are immunogens. (C) All immunogens are not antigens but all antigens are immunogens." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (A).** Haptens are the classic antigens that are not immunogens.

Good immunogens are usually **large (above about 10 kDa)**, **foreign**, chemically **complex** proteins or polysaccharides.

## 5. Antibody structure

- Antibodies are **immunoglobulins (Ig)** — **glycoproteins** found in the **gamma-globulin** fraction of plasma.
- Each antibody molecule has **four peptide chains**: **two small light (L) chains and two longer heavy (H) chains** — hence written as **H₂L₂**.
- The chains are held together by **disulphide (S–S) bonds**; the molecule is **Y-shaped** and flexible at the **hinge**.
- Each chain has a **variable (V) region** at the tip (differs from antibody to antibody; forms the **antigen-binding site**) and a **constant (C) region**.
- Each antibody has **two identical antigen-binding sites** (bivalent).
- The enzyme **papain** splits an antibody into **two Fab** (fragment antigen-binding) pieces and **one Fc** (fragment crystallisable) piece; **pepsin** gives one F(ab')₂. The Fc binds to receptors on phagocytes and activates complement.
- Light chains are of two types, **kappa (κ)** and **lambda (λ)**. Heavy chains are of five types — **γ, α, μ, δ, ε** — which decide the class (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, IgE).
- Structure worked out by **Rodney Porter and Gerald Edelman** — Nobel Prize **1972**.

## 6. The five antibody classes

| Class | Heavy chain | Form | Share of serum Ig | Special facts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **IgG** | γ | Monomer | **Most abundant, about 75–80%** | **Only class that crosses the placenta** → passive immunity to foetus/newborn; main antibody of the **secondary response**; longest half-life |
| **IgA** | α | Monomer in serum; **dimer in secretions** (secretory IgA) | 10–15% | Found in **colostrum, milk, saliva, tears, mucus**; protects mucous surfaces |
| **IgM** | μ | **Pentamer** — largest (about 900 kDa, "millionaire molecule") | 5–10% | **First antibody made in a primary response** and the first made by the foetus; natural anti-A and anti-B blood-group antibodies; best at agglutination and complement activation; does **not** cross placenta |
| **IgD** | δ | Monomer | Below 1% | On the surface of naive **B cells** as antigen receptor |
| **IgE** | ε | Monomer | Trace | **Allergy** (binds mast cells and basophils → histamine); defence against **parasitic worms**; raised in asthma, hay fever |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q120 asked: "Which immunoglobulin can cross the placenta and can provide passive immunity to new-born? — (A) IgG (B) IgA (C) IgM." **Official answer: (A) IgG.**

> **Memory trick:** Classes by serum amount — "**G A M D E**" (IgG > IgA > IgM > IgD > IgE). **G** for "Goes across placenta", **A** for "milk/All secretions", **M** for "Massive and first (priMary)", **E** for "allErgy".

## 7. Active and passive immunity

| | Active immunity | Passive immunity |
|---|---|---|
| How | Body **makes its own** antibodies after meeting an antigen | **Ready-made antibodies** are given |
| Speed | Slow to develop | **Immediate** |
| Duration | **Long-lasting** (memory) | Short (weeks to months) |
| Natural example | Recovery from an infection (measles) | **IgG across placenta**; **IgA in colostrum** |
| Artificial example | **Vaccination** | **Antitoxin / antiserum** — anti-tetanus serum (ATS), **snake antivenom** (made in horses), anti-rabies immunoglobulin |

NCERT points: the yellowish fluid **colostrum** secreted by the mother in the initial days of lactation has abundant **IgA** to protect the infant; the foetus receives antibodies (IgG) through the placenta. In a quick-acting emergency — tetanus, snakebite, rabies — **preformed antibodies (passive immunisation)** are injected because there is no time to wait for active immunity.

## 8. Vaccination and immunisation

The **principle of vaccination** is the property of **memory** of the immune system. A preparation of **antigenic proteins of the pathogen or inactivated/weakened pathogen (vaccine)** is introduced; antibodies and **memory B and T cells** form; on later exposure, the response is quick and massive.

| Vaccine type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Live attenuated** (weakened) | **BCG** (TB), **oral polio (OPV, Sabin)**, **MMR**, measles–rubella, yellow fever, rotavirus, chickenpox |
| **Killed / inactivated** | **Injectable polio (IPV, Salk)**, rabies (cell-culture), whole-cell pertussis, cholera, **Covaxin** (COVID-19, Bharat Biotech with ICMR–NIV) |
| **Toxoid** (inactivated toxin) | **Tetanus**, **diphtheria** |
| **Subunit / recombinant** | **Hepatitis B vaccine** — HBsAg produced in **yeast** by recombinant DNA technology (NCERT example); HPV vaccine |
| **Conjugate** | Hib, pneumococcal (PCV) — polysaccharide joined to a carrier protein |
| **Viral vector** | **Covishield** (ChAdOx1, Oxford–AstraZeneca, made by Serum Institute of India) |
| **mRNA** | Pfizer–BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines (Nobel 2023 to Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman); India's Gemcovac |
| **DNA** | **ZyCoV-D** (Zydus Cadila, 2021) — world's first DNA vaccine approved for humans |

India's Universal Immunisation Programme is covered in [Drugs, Cancer and Public Health](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/reproduction-health/drugs-cancer-and-public-health).

### 8.1 History of immunology — who did what

| Scientist | Contribution | Year |
|---|---|---|
| **Edward Jenner** (England) | Protected **James Phipps** against smallpox using material from **cowpox** sores of a milkmaid; first scientific vaccination; called the "**father of immunology**" | **1796** |
| **Louis Pasteur** (France) | Attenuated vaccines — **chicken cholera** (1879), **anthrax** (1881), **rabies** (1885, first given to the boy **Joseph Meister**); proposed the general term "**vaccine**" (from Latin *vacca* = cow) **in honour of Jenner** | 1879–1885 |
| **Robert Koch** (Germany) | **Koch's postulates**; discovered the anthrax bacillus (1876), **TB bacillus (1882)**, cholera vibrio (1883); Nobel 1905 | 1876–1883 |
| **Élie Metchnikoff** | **Phagocytosis** — cellular theory of immunity; Nobel 1908 (shared with Ehrlich) | 1882 |
| **Paul Ehrlich** | Side-chain theory of antibody formation; "magic bullet" Salvarsan for syphilis; Nobel 1908 | 1890s–1910 |
| **Emil von Behring** (with Kitasato) | **Diphtheria antitoxin** — serum therapy; **first Nobel Prize in Physiology/Medicine (1901)** | 1890 |
| **Karl Landsteiner** | ABO blood groups (1900–01); Nobel 1930; Rh factor (1940, with Wiener) | 1900 |
| **Macfarlane Burnet and Peter Medawar** | Acquired immunological tolerance; Burnet's **clonal selection theory** (1957); Nobel 1960 | 1950s |
| **Jonas Salk / Albert Sabin** | Killed injectable polio vaccine (1955) / live oral polio vaccine (about 1961) | 1955–61 |
| **Köhler and Milstein** | **Hybridoma technique — monoclonal antibodies** (1975); Nobel 1984 | 1975 |
| **Susumu Tonegawa** | Genetic basis of antibody diversity (gene rearrangement); Nobel 1987 | 1976 |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q116 asked: "The term 'vaccine' was introduced by — (A) Edward Jenner (B) Robert Koch (C) Louis Pasteur." **Official answer: (A) Edward Jenner.** Note the disagreement: Jenner named cowpox *variolae vaccinae* ("smallpox of the cow") in 1798, and that is the origin of the word; but most histories say it was **Pasteur who extended the term "vaccine/vaccination" to all such preparations in 1881, in Jenner's honour**. In the exam, follow the official key (Jenner) if Pasteur and Jenner both appear the same way; in teaching, tell both halves of the story.

**Smallpox** — the only human disease **eradicated** worldwide: last natural case in Somalia (Ali Maow Maalin, 1977); WHO declared eradication in **May 1980**. India's last case was in 1975.

## 9. Hypersensitivity and allergy

**Allergy** — the **exaggerated response** of the immune system to certain antigens present in the environment. The substances causing it are **allergens** — **mites in dust, pollen, animal dander**, certain foods.

- Antibodies produced are of the **IgE** type.
- Allergy is due to the release of chemicals like **histamine and serotonin** from the **mast cells**.
- Symptoms: sneezing, watery eyes, running nose and difficulty in breathing.
- Diagnosis: the patient is exposed to or injected with very small doses of possible allergens (skin-prick test).
- Treatment: **antihistamines, adrenaline and steroids** quickly reduce symptoms.
- NCERT: modern-day lifestyle has resulted in lowering of immunity and **more sensitivity to allergens** — children in metro cities suffer from allergies and asthma due to sensitivity to the environment, partly because of the protected environment provided early in life (the "hygiene hypothesis").
- **Anaphylactic shock** — severe whole-body allergy (bee sting, penicillin, peanuts); treated with **adrenaline (epinephrine)** injection.

**Types of hypersensitivity (Gell and Coombs; B.Sc.):**

| Type | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I — Immediate | **IgE** on mast cells → histamine | Hay fever, asthma, anaphylaxis |
| II — Cytotoxic | IgG/IgM against cell-surface antigens | Mismatched blood transfusion, **erythroblastosis foetalis** (Rh) |
| III — Immune complex | Antigen–antibody complexes deposit in tissues | Serum sickness, SLE, Arthus reaction |
| IV — Delayed (cell-mediated) | **T cells**, 24–72 h | **Tuberculin (Mantoux) test**, contact dermatitis, graft rejection |

## 10. Autoimmunity

Memory-based acquired immunity in higher vertebrates can distinguish foreign molecules from self molecules. Sometimes, due to genetic and other unknown reasons, the body **attacks self-cells**. This results in damage to the body and is called **autoimmune disease**.

| Autoimmune disease | Target |
|---|---|
| **Rheumatoid arthritis** (NCERT example) | Joint lining (synovium) |
| **Type 1 diabetes** | **β cells** of islets of Langerhans |
| **Myasthenia gravis** | Acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction |
| **Multiple sclerosis** | Myelin sheath in CNS |
| **Hashimoto's thyroiditis** | Thyroid → hypothyroidism |
| **Graves' disease** | Antibodies **stimulate** TSH receptors → hyperthyroidism |
| **SLE (lupus)** | Many organs; anti-nuclear (anti-DNA) antibodies |
| **Psoriasis, vitiligo** | Skin; vitiligo destroys melanocytes |
| **Addison's disease** | Adrenal cortex |
| **Pernicious anaemia** | Parietal cells / intrinsic factor |
| **Coeliac disease** | Small intestine lining (triggered by gluten) |

**Not autoimmune:** Alzheimer's disease (TRE 2.0 asked this in the 9–10 paper), AIDS (immunodeficiency), allergy (hypersensitivity to foreign antigens).

**Immunodeficiency:** primary (inborn) — **SCID**, e.g., due to **ADA (adenosine deaminase) deficiency**, treated by the **first clinical gene therapy (1990, a 4-year-old girl, Ashanti DeSilva)**; secondary (acquired) — **AIDS**, malnutrition, cancer chemotherapy.

## 11. AIDS and HIV

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Full form | **Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome** — a deficiency of the immune system acquired during a person's lifetime (not congenital) |
| First reported | **1981** (USA) |
| First case in India | **1986**, Chennai (detected by Dr Suniti Solomon) |
| Virus | **HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus)**, a **retrovirus** (lentivirus) with **RNA genome** and the enzyme **reverse transcriptase**; discovered by **Luc Montagnier and Françoise Barré-Sinoussi** (1983; Nobel 2008); Robert Gallo independently |
| Main target | **Helper T lymphocytes (T_H, CD4⁺)**; also macrophages |
| Incubation | Time lag between infection and appearance of AIDS symptoms — **a few months to many years, usually 5–10 years** |
| Screening test | **ELISA** (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay); confirmation by **Western blot**; viral load by **PCR** |
| Treatment | **Anti-retroviral drugs** — only **partially effective**; prolong life but cannot cure |
| India's body | **NACO** (National AIDS Control Organisation), set up in **1992** |
| World AIDS Day | **1 December** |

### 11.1 Life cycle of HIV in the body (NCERT sequence)

1. HIV enters the body → enters **macrophages**.
2. Its **RNA genome is copied into viral DNA** by **reverse transcriptase**.
3. The viral DNA gets **incorporated into the host cell's DNA** and directs the infected cells to produce virus particles.
4. **Macrophages continue to produce virus** and act like an "**HIV factory**".
5. Simultaneously HIV enters **helper T lymphocytes (T_H)**, replicates and produces progeny viruses.
6. Progeny viruses released into the blood attack other helper T cells. This is repeated, leading to a **progressive decrease in the number of helper T cells**.
7. The person suffers bouts of fever, diarrhoea and weight loss. With fall of T_H cells the person becomes **immunodeficient** and unable to fight even common infections — especially **opportunistic infections** by *Mycobacterium* (TB), viruses, fungi and parasites like *Toxoplasma*.

Normal CD4 count is about 500–1,500 cells per µL of blood; below **200** indicates AIDS (clinical rule).

### 11.2 Transmission and prevention

HIV spreads by:
- **sexual contact** with an infected person;
- **transfusion of contaminated blood** and blood products;
- **sharing infected needles** (intravenous drug abusers);
- from an **infected mother to her child through the placenta** (and at birth or by breastfeeding).

High-risk groups: people with multiple sexual partners, drug addicts who take drugs intravenously, people who repeatedly need blood transfusions, and children born to HIV-infected mothers.

HIV/AIDS does **not** spread by mere touch or physical contact, hugging, sharing food or utensils, swimming pools, or mosquito bites. Infected persons should not be isolated from family and society.

Prevention: safe blood (screening of blood in blood banks), disposable needles and syringes, free distribution of condoms, controlling drug abuse, safe sex, regular check-ups for susceptible populations; NACO and NGOs educate people. The **HIV and AIDS (Prevention and Control) Act, 2017** protects patients from discrimination.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q119 asked: "AIDS is transmitted by — (A) sexual intercourse (B) blood transfusion (C) placental transfusion." **Official answer: (D) More than one of the above** — all three are routes.

## 12. Transplantation immunology

| Graft type | Donor | Rejection? |
|---|---|---|
| **Autograft** | Same person (skin from thigh to face) | No |
| **Isograft (syngeneic)** | Genetically identical twin | No |
| **Allograft** | Another person of the same species | Yes, unless matched and suppressed |
| **Xenograft** | Another species (pig heart valve, pig kidney/heart trials) | Strong |

- When organs like heart, eye, liver or kidney fail, **transplantation** is the only remedy.
- Grafts from just any source cannot be made — the body recognises them as "non-self". **Tissue matching** and **blood group matching** are essential before any graft/transplant, and the patient has to take **immunosuppressants all his/her life**.
- The body can differentiate "self" and "non-self", and the **cell-mediated immune response (T cells)** is responsible for **graft rejection**.
- The molecules that decide matching are **MHC** proteins — in humans called **HLA (human leucocyte antigens)**, coded on **chromosome 6**.
- **Cyclosporin A**, an immunosuppressant used in organ transplant patients, is produced by the fungus ***Trichoderma polysporum*** (NCERT). Others — corticosteroids, tacrolimus.
- **Cornea** grafts are rarely rejected because the cornea has **no blood vessels** (immune-privileged).
- Milestones: first successful kidney transplant between identical twins (**Joseph Murray, 1954**; Nobel 1990); first human heart transplant (**Christiaan Barnard, Cape Town, 1967**); India's first successful heart transplant (**AIIMS, New Delhi, 1994**, Dr P. Venugopal).

## 13. Interferons and monoclonal antibodies

- **Interferons** — proteins released by **virus-infected cells** that protect neighbouring cells; discovered by **Isaacs and Lindenmann (1957)**. **α-interferon** is used as a **biological response modifier** in cancer immunotherapy.
- **Monoclonal antibodies** — identical antibodies made by one clone of **hybridoma cells** (B cell fused with a myeloma cancer cell). Used in diagnosis (pregnancy kits, blood typing), cancer detection and treatment, and transplant medicine.

## Traps and exceptions

- **IgG crosses the placenta**; **IgA** is in **colostrum**; **IgM** is the **first** and **largest**; **IgE** is for **allergy** and worms; **IgD** is a B-cell receptor.
- **IgG is the most abundant**; IgE is the least.
- Antibodies are **glycoproteins**, not steroids or lipids. Structure **H₂L₂**, held by **disulphide bonds**.
- **Interferons** come from **virus-infected cells** (cytokine barrier) — they are innate, not antibodies.
- **T cells mature in the thymus; B cells in bone marrow** (bursa in birds). Both are **produced** in bone marrow.
- Thymus is **largest at birth** and shrinks with age.
- **Graft rejection** is **cell-mediated** (T cells), not antibody-mediated.
- HIV is an **RNA** virus but makes **DNA** inside the host (reverse transcriptase — "reverse" of the central dogma).
- HIV attacks **helper T (CD4)** cells — not B cells, not RBCs. **Macrophages** are the "HIV factory".
- ELISA = screening; Western blot = confirmation.
- Snake antivenom and anti-tetanus serum = **artificial passive** immunity; vaccines = **artificial active**.
- All immunogens are antigens; **not** all antigens are immunogens (haptens).
- By the **official TRE 2.0 key**, "vaccine" was introduced by **Jenner**; many books credit **Pasteur** for generalising the term.
- **Alzheimer's is not autoimmune**; Graves' disease antibodies **stimulate** (not block) the thyroid.

6. Which bonds hold antibody chains together? — **Disulphide bonds**.
7. Which antibody is a pentamer? — **IgM**.
8. Which antibody is found in colostrum? — **IgA**.
9. Which antibody is linked with allergy? — **IgE**.
10. Which antibody crosses the placenta? — **IgG**.
11. Antibodies given in snakebite give what immunity? — **Artificial passive immunity**.
12. Who performed the first vaccination in 1796? — **Edward Jenner**.
13. Who developed the rabies vaccine? — **Louis Pasteur** (1885).
14. Which chemical from mast cells causes allergy symptoms? — **Histamine** (and serotonin).
15. Name the type of hypersensitivity shown in the Mantoux test. — **Type IV (delayed, cell-mediated)**.
16. Which enzyme does HIV use to make DNA from RNA? — **Reverse transcriptase**.
17. Which cells act as the HIV factory? — **Macrophages**.
18. Which test is used for HIV screening? — **ELISA**.
19. Which type of immunity rejects a transplanted kidney? — **Cell-mediated immunity** (T cells).
20. Which fungus gives cyclosporin A? — ***Trichoderma polysporum***.
