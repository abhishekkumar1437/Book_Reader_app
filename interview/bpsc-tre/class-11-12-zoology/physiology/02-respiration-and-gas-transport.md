This chapter covers NCERT class 11 "Breathing and Exchange of Gases" and climbs to Guyton-level points: compliance and surfactant, dead space and alveolar ventilation, the shape of the oxygen dissociation curve, Bohr and Haldane effects, and the chloride (Hamburger) shift. TRE 2.0 asked chloride shift (Q78), diffusion at the respiratory surface (Q79) and the role of the Krebs cycle (Q80).

> **In simple words:** **Breathing** is the physical movement of air in and out of the lungs. **Respiration** at the cell level is the chemical burning of food with O₂ to make ATP, releasing CO₂. Between them sits **gas transport**: blood carries O₂ from lungs to tissues mostly on haemoglobin, and carries CO₂ back mostly as bicarbonate. Gases always move by **diffusion** from high partial pressure to low partial pressure — no pump is involved.

Basic version: [Human Physiology — Digestion to Excretion (Class 9–10)](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-9-10-science/biology/human-physiology-digestion-to-excretion).

## 1. Respiratory organs in animals

| Animal group | Respiratory organ | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sponges, cnidarians, flatworms | **Simple diffusion** over the whole body surface | *Hydra*, *Planaria* |
| Earthworm | **Moist skin** (cutaneous respiration) | *Pheretima* |
| Insects | **Tracheal tubes** opening by spiracles; air reaches cells directly | Cockroach (10 pairs of spiracles) |
| Most aquatic arthropods and molluscs | **Gills** (branchial respiration) | Prawn, *Pila* (also has a pulmonary sac) |
| Fishes | **Gills** with **counter-current** flow of water and blood | Rohu; dipnoi (lung fishes) also have lungs |
| Amphibians | **Skin**, buccopharyngeal lining and **lungs** | Frog (skin is the main organ during hibernation) |
| Reptiles, birds, mammals | **Lungs** (pulmonary respiration) | Birds have **air sacs** giving one-way air flow |
| Scorpions, spiders | **Book lungs** | Scorpion |
| King crab (*Limulus*) | **Book gills** | — |

## 2. The human respiratory system

**Path of air:** external nostrils → nasal chamber → **pharynx** → **larynx** (voice box; cartilaginous; thyroid cartilage forms the **Adam's apple**) → **trachea** → right and left primary **bronchi** → secondary and tertiary bronchi → **bronchioles** → terminal bronchioles → respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → **alveoli**.

- **Conducting part** (nostrils to terminal bronchioles): cleans, warms and moistens air; no gas exchange. Lined by **pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium** with goblet cells.
- **Respiratory (exchange) part**: respiratory bronchioles and alveoli.
- The **trachea** is held open by **C-shaped (incomplete) rings of hyaline cartilage**; it divides at the level of the **5th thoracic vertebra**.
- The **epiglottis** closes the glottis during swallowing.
- **Lungs**: right lung **3 lobes**, left lung **2 lobes** (space for the heart — the cardiac notch). Each is covered by a double membrane, the **pleura**, with **pleural fluid** between the layers to reduce friction.
- The lungs lie in the air-tight **thoracic chamber**: dorsally the vertebral column, ventrally the sternum, laterally the ribs, below the dome-shaped **diaphragm**.
- About **300 million alveoli** (newer estimates up to ~480 million) give ~**70 m²** of exchange surface.

### The alveolus and the diffusion membrane

- **Type I alveolar cells (pneumocytes)** — thin squamous cells; ~95% of the surface; for exchange.
- **Type II alveolar cells** — secrete **surfactant** (mainly **dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine / lecithin**) which lowers surface tension so alveoli do not collapse. Premature babies lacking it develop **respiratory distress syndrome (hyaline membrane disease)**.
- **Alveolar macrophages ("dust cells")** — clean the alveoli.
- **Diffusion (respiratory) membrane** = alveolar squamous epithelium + its basement membrane (+ thin interstitium) + capillary endothelium with its basement membrane. Total thickness ~**0.5 µm** (NCERT says "much less than a millimetre").

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q79 asked: "Exchange of oxygen and CO₂ at the respiratory surface occurs through — (A) active transport (B) osmosis (C) diffusion." **Official answer: (C) diffusion.** Simple diffusion down the partial-pressure gradient; no ATP. Osmosis is diffusion of water, not gases.

## 3. Steps of respiration (NCERT)

1. **Breathing (pulmonary ventilation)** — atmospheric air in, alveolar air out.
2. **Diffusion of gases** across the alveolar membrane.
3. **Transport of gases** by blood.
4. **Diffusion of O₂ and CO₂** between blood and tissues.
5. **Cellular respiration** — use of O₂ by cells to break down food and release energy, with CO₂ produced.

### Breathing versus respiration

| Feature | **Breathing** | **Cellular respiration** |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Physical (mechanical) process | Biochemical (enzymatic) process |
| Site | Lungs and respiratory tract | Cytoplasm + **mitochondria** of every cell |
| Energy | Uses energy (muscle work) | **Releases** energy as ATP |
| Enzymes | None | Many (glycolysis, Krebs cycle, ETS) |
| Output | Exchange of air | ATP, CO₂, water |

## 4. Mechanism of breathing

Air moves because of a **pressure gradient** between the atmosphere and the lungs (intra-pulmonary pressure). The lungs themselves have no muscle; the **diaphragm** and the **intercostal muscles** (between the ribs) change the volume of the thorax.

| | **Inspiration (active)** | **Expiration (normally passive)** |
|---|---|---|
| Diaphragm | **Contracts**, flattens, moves down → thoracic volume ↑ (antero-posterior axis) | **Relaxes**, becomes dome-shaped |
| External intercostal muscles | **Contract** → ribs and sternum lift up and out (dorso-ventral axis) | Relax |
| Thoracic / pulmonary volume | Increases | Decreases |
| Intra-pulmonary pressure | Falls **below** atmospheric (~1–3 mmHg) | Rises **above** atmospheric |
| Air flow | Into lungs | Out of lungs |

- **Forced expiration**: **internal intercostal muscles** and **abdominal muscles** contract.
- **Forced inspiration**: accessory muscles — sternocleidomastoid, scalenes.
- **Intrapleural pressure** stays negative (about −4 to −5 mmHg; ~−756 mmHg absolute); if air enters the pleural space (**pneumothorax**) the lung collapses.
- **Compliance** — how easily the lungs stretch; low in fibrosis, high in emphysema.
- Healthy adult breathes **12–16 times per minute** (newborn ~40). A **spirometer** measures lung volumes.

> **Remember:** Mammals breathe by **negative-pressure** breathing (air is "pulled in"). Frogs use **positive-pressure** (buccal pump) breathing — they push air into the lungs by raising the floor of the mouth.

## 5. Lung volumes and capacities (with numbers)

<div class="formula">

| Volume | Meaning | Average adult value |
|---|---|---|
| **Tidal volume (TV)** | Air in or out in one normal breath | **~500 mL** (6000–8000 mL per minute) |
| **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)** | Extra air that can be inhaled by force after a normal inspiration | 2500–3000 mL |
| **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)** | Extra air that can be exhaled by force after a normal expiration | 1000–1100 mL |
| **Residual volume (RV)** | Air left in lungs even after forcible expiration | 1100–1200 mL |

| Capacity | Formula | Value |
|---|---|---|
| **Inspiratory capacity (IC)** | TV + IRV | ~3000–3500 mL |
| **Expiratory capacity (EC)** | TV + ERV | ~1500–1600 mL |
| **Functional residual capacity (FRC)** | ERV + RV | ~2300 mL |
| **Vital capacity (VC)** | ERV + TV + IRV (maximum air breathed out after a maximum breath in) | ~**3500–4600 mL** |
| **Total lung capacity (TLC)** | VC + RV = RV + ERV + TV + IRV | ~**5800–6000 mL** |

</div>

- **Residual volume cannot be measured by a simple spirometer** — so neither can FRC or TLC (use helium dilution or body plethysmography).
- **Dead space** — air in the conducting tubes that does not reach alveoli: ~**150 mL** (anatomical dead space).
- **Alveolar ventilation** = (TV − dead space) × breathing rate.
- VC is higher in athletes, men, mountain dwellers; lower in women, old age, lung disease.

### Worked numericals

1. **Pulmonary (minute) ventilation.** TV 500 mL, rate 12/min → 500 × 12 = **6000 mL/min (6 L/min)**.
2. **Alveolar ventilation.** Same person, dead space 150 mL → (500 − 150) × 12 = **4200 mL/min**. Lesson: slow deep breathing ventilates alveoli better than fast shallow breathing. If rate = 30/min with TV = 200 mL: minute ventilation still 6000 mL, but alveolar ventilation = (200 − 150) × 30 = **1500 mL/min** only.
3. **Vital capacity.** IRV 3000 + TV 500 + ERV 1100 = **4600 mL**. Add RV 1200 → TLC = **5800 mL**.
4. **FRC.** ERV 1100 + RV 1200 = **2300 mL** — the air that stays in the lungs at the end of a quiet breath, keeping blood gases steady between breaths.

## 6. Exchange of gases — partial pressures

**Partial pressure** = the pressure contributed by one gas in a mixture (pO₂, pCO₂). Atmospheric pressure at sea level is 760 mmHg; O₂ is ~21% → pO₂ ≈ 159 mmHg.

<div class="formula">

| Gas (mmHg) | Atmospheric air | Alveoli | Deoxygenated blood | Oxygenated blood | Tissues |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **O₂** | 159 | **104** | 40 | 95 | 40 |
| **CO₂** | 0.3 | **40** | 45 | 40 | 45 |

</div>

- At the alveolus: O₂ moves alveolus (104) → blood (40); CO₂ moves blood (45) → alveolus (40).
- At the tissues: O₂ moves blood (95) → tissue (40); CO₂ moves tissue (45) → blood (40).
- The CO₂ gradient is small (5 mmHg) but CO₂ is **20–25 times more soluble** than O₂, so it diffuses just as well.
- Factors affecting diffusion (Fick's law): partial-pressure gradient, solubility, **thickness** of membrane (↓ is better), **surface area** (↑ is better).

## 7. Transport of oxygen

- About **97%** of O₂ is carried by RBCs as **oxyhaemoglobin**; ~**3%** dissolved in plasma.
- **Haemoglobin** (Hb) — red iron-containing pigment in RBCs; 4 polypeptide chains (adult HbA = α₂β₂), each with a **haem** group containing **Fe²⁺**. Each Hb molecule carries **4 O₂** molecules.
- 1 g of Hb binds about **1.34 mL O₂**. Normal Hb **12–16 g/100 mL** blood → maximum O₂ content ~**20 mL per 100 mL** blood.
- Under normal resting conditions, every **100 mL of oxygenated blood delivers about 5 mL of O₂** to the tissues (NCERT).
- Binding of O₂ to Hb depends mainly on **pO₂**; also on pCO₂, H⁺ concentration and temperature.
- Binding is **cooperative**: once one O₂ binds, the others bind more easily — this makes the curve **sigmoid (S-shaped)**.

Key points on the curve:

| pO₂ (mmHg) | Where | Hb saturation |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | Arterial blood / alveoli | ~97–98% |
| 40 | Venous blood at rest | ~75% |
| ~27 | **P₅₀** (pO₂ at which Hb is 50% saturated) | 50% |
| 20 | Exercising muscle | ~30–35% |

- The **upper flat part** (pO₂ 60–100) is a safety margin: even at high altitude or mild lung disease, Hb stays mostly saturated.
- The **steep middle part** (pO₂ 10–40) means a small drop of pO₂ in tissues releases a lot of O₂.
- **Right shift** (curve moves right; P₅₀ rises; Hb releases O₂ more easily): ↑pCO₂, ↑H⁺ (↓pH), ↑temperature, ↑**2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG/DPG)**. This happens in active tissues.
- **Left shift** (Hb holds O₂ more tightly): ↓pCO₂, ↓H⁺, ↓temperature, ↓2,3-BPG, **foetal haemoglobin (HbF, α₂γ₂)**, **carbon monoxide**, **myoglobin** (hyperbolic curve, far left — stores O₂ in muscle).
- Memory for right shift: "**CADET, face Right!**" — **C**O₂, **A**cid, 2,3-**D**PG, **E**xercise, **T**emperature.

> **Exam angle:** At the alveoli conditions are high pO₂, low pCO₂, less H⁺, lower temperature → **oxyhaemoglobin forms**. At the tissues: low pO₂, high pCO₂, high H⁺, higher temperature → **O₂ dissociates**. This one NCERT sentence explains the whole curve.

### Bohr effect and Haldane effect

| | **Bohr effect** | **Haldane effect** |
|---|---|---|
| Statement | ↑CO₂ / ↑H⁺ **reduces Hb's affinity for O₂** → O₂ is released | **Binding of O₂ to Hb releases CO₂** (and H⁺) from blood; deoxygenated Hb carries more CO₂ |
| Where it matters | **Tissues** (O₂ unloading) | **Lungs** (CO₂ unloading); also helps CO₂ loading at tissues |
| Discovered by | Christian Bohr (1904) | J. S. Haldane (with Christiansen and Douglas, 1914) |
| In one line | CO₂ affects O₂ transport | O₂ affects CO₂ transport |

- **Carbon monoxide (CO)** binds Hb about **200–250 times** more strongly than O₂, forming **carboxyhaemoglobin** — CO poisoning (cherry-red skin), common with closed-room coal stoves.
- **Methaemoglobin** — iron oxidised to Fe³⁺; cannot carry O₂ (nitrate poisoning, "blue baby" from nitrate-rich water).

| **Carbamino-haemoglobin** (CO₂ bound to amino groups of Hb, not to iron) | **20–25%** |
| **Dissolved** in plasma | **~7%** |

</div>

### The chloride shift (Hamburger shift)

At the **tissues**:

1. CO₂ diffuses into the RBC.
2. **Carbonic anhydrase** (very high in RBCs) catalyses: **CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻**.
3. H⁺ is buffered by deoxy-Hb (H·Hb) — this is the Haldane/Bohr link.
4. HCO₃⁻ diffuses out of the RBC into **plasma** through the **band 3 (Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger)** protein.
5. To keep electrical neutrality, **Cl⁻ moves from plasma into the RBC** — this is the **chloride shift**. Venous RBCs therefore have more Cl⁻ and are slightly larger (they also take in water).

At the **lungs** everything reverses: HCO₃⁻ enters the RBC, Cl⁻ leaves (**reverse chloride shift**), H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O, and CO₂ diffuses into the alveoli.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q78 asked: "In the process of transport of CO₂, which phenomenon occurs between RBC and plasma? — (A) Absorption (B) Chloride shift (C) Osmosis." **Official answer: (B) Chloride shift.** Named after H. J. Hamburger (1892), so also called the Hamburger phenomenon.

## 9. Regulation of breathing

| Centre / receptor | Location | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory rhythm centre** (dorsal and ventral respiratory groups) | **Medulla oblongata** | Basic rhythm of breathing |
| **Pneumotaxic centre** | **Pons** (upper) | Limits inspiration → shortens it → raises rate |
| Apneustic centre | Pons (lower) | Prolongs inspiration (B.Sc.) |
| **Chemosensitive area** (central chemoreceptors) | Medulla, near the rhythm centre | Highly sensitive to **CO₂ and H⁺** (of CSF) — the main driver |
| **Peripheral chemoreceptors** — carotid bodies and aortic bodies | Carotid artery bifurcation; aortic arch | Sense **low O₂**, high CO₂ and H⁺; send signals via glossopharyngeal (IX) and vagus (X) nerves |
| **Stretch receptors** of the lungs | Bronchial walls | **Hering–Breuer reflex** — stop over-inflation via vagus |

- **CO₂ (through H⁺)** is the most important chemical regulator; **O₂ plays an insignificant role** under normal conditions (NCERT). Low O₂ becomes important at high altitude or in chronic lung disease.
- Breathing can be voluntarily controlled for a while (cerebral cortex), but rising CO₂ eventually forces a breath ("breaking point").
- **Hyperventilation** washes out CO₂ → alkalosis, dizziness. **Hypoventilation** raises CO₂ → acidosis.

### Special conditions

- **High altitude**: low pO₂ → hypoxia → **mountain sickness** (headache, nausea). Acclimatisation: more breathing, **more erythropoietin → more RBCs**, more 2,3-BPG.
- **Deep-sea diving**: high pressure dissolves N₂ in blood; rapid ascent → N₂ bubbles → **decompression sickness ("the bends", caisson disease)**.
- **Asphyxia** — O₂ lack with CO₂ excess (choking, drowning).

## 10. Respiratory pigments and breathing in other vertebrates (B.Sc.)

| Pigment | Metal | Colour (oxygenated) | Where | Animals |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Haemoglobin** | Iron (Fe²⁺, in haem) | Red | In RBCs (vertebrates); dissolved in plasma in some invertebrates | Vertebrates, earthworm (plasma), *Chironomus* larva |
| **Haemocyanin** | **Copper** | **Blue** (colourless when deoxygenated) | Dissolved in plasma/haemolymph | **Molluscs** (*Pila*, octopus) and many **arthropods** (prawn, crab) |
| **Haemerythrin** | Iron (non-haem) | Violet-pink | In cells | Some annelids (*Sipunculus*), brachiopods |
| **Chlorocruorin** | Iron | **Green** (dilute) / red | Plasma | Some polychaete worms (*Sabella*) |
| **Myoglobin** | Iron (one haem) | Red | Muscle cells | Vertebrates — very high in **whales and seals** (O₂ store for diving) |

- Insect blood (haemolymph) usually has **no respiratory pigment** — O₂ goes straight to cells by tracheae.
- **Birds**: lungs are small and rigid; **9 air sacs** act as bellows. Air flows **one way** through tiny tubes called **parabronchi**, with **cross-current** exchange — so birds extract O₂ better than mammals (useful at high altitude). Two breathing cycles are needed to move one breath of air through the system.
- **Fish gills**: water and blood flow in **opposite directions** (counter-current exchange) → up to ~80% of dissolved O₂ is extracted.
- **Frog**: no diaphragm, no ribs for breathing; uses the **buccal pump** (positive pressure); skin respiration dominates during hibernation and underwater.

**Worked numerical — O₂ delivery to the body:** cardiac output 5 L/min = 50 × 100 mL. Each 100 mL of arterial blood gives ~5 mL O₂ to tissues → 50 × 5 = **250 mL O₂ per minute** delivered at rest (matches resting O₂ consumption of ~250 mL/min). In heavy exercise, cardiac output may reach 25 L/min and each 100 mL may give ~15 mL O₂ → 250 × 15 = **3750 mL O₂ per minute**.

## 11. Respiratory disorders

| Disorder | Feature |
|---|---|
| **Asthma** | Spasm and **inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles** → wheezing, difficulty in breathing (often allergic) |
| **Emphysema** | **Alveolar walls damaged** → less exchange surface; main cause **cigarette smoking** |
| **Chronic bronchitis** | Long-term inflammation of bronchi, mucus, cough |
| **Occupational lung diseases** | Long exposure to dust → inflammation → **fibrosis**: **silicosis** (silica — stone crushing, mining), **asbestosis** (asbestos), anthracosis / black lung (coal dust), byssinosis (cotton dust) |
| **Pneumonia** | Alveoli filled with fluid; *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (bacterial) or viruses |
| **Tuberculosis** | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (Robert Koch, 1882) |
| **Respiratory distress syndrome** | Lack of **surfactant** in premature babies |
| **Hypoxia** | Low O₂ supply to tissues — hypoxic, anaemic, stagnant or histotoxic (cyanide) |

## 12. Link to cellular respiration (Krebs cycle)

Cellular respiration (detail in [Biomolecules and Enzymes](/interview/bpsc-tre/class-11-12-zoology/cell-genetics/biomolecules-and-enzymes)):

- **Glycolysis** (EMP pathway) — cytoplasm; glucose → 2 pyruvate; net 2 ATP + 2 NADH.
- **Link reaction** — pyruvate → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ (mitochondrial matrix; pyruvate dehydrogenase).
- **Krebs cycle** (citric acid / TCA cycle; Hans Krebs, 1937) — matrix; acetyl-CoA (2C) + oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C) → ... → oxaloacetate. Per acetyl-CoA: **2 CO₂, 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP/ATP**. It is the **common final pathway** for oxidising carbohydrates, fats and amino acids.
- **Electron transport system + oxidative phosphorylation** — inner mitochondrial membrane; NADH and FADH₂ give electrons; O₂ is the **final electron acceptor** → water; ATP synthase makes most ATP (~32–34 per glucose; modern estimate ~30–32; total "38" in old books).
- **Respiratory quotient (RQ)** = CO₂ released / O₂ used: carbohydrate **1.0**, fat **~0.7**, protein **~0.8–0.9**, anaerobic respiration ∞ (CO₂ without O₂ use, in yeast).

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q80 asked: "Krebs cycle is important for — (A) oxidation of carbohydrates (B) oxidation of amino acids (C) production of ATP by oxidative phosphorylation (D) More than one of the above." **Official answer: (C).** The idea: the cycle's main value is producing NADH and FADH₂, which drive **oxidative phosphorylation**, the main ATP source. Strictly, the Krebs cycle itself oxidises acetyl-CoA from both carbohydrates and amino acids, so (D) is defensible; but go with the key — BPSC took "production of ATP" as the purpose.

## Traps and exceptions

- **Inspiration is active; quiet expiration is passive** (only elastic recoil). Forced expiration uses internal intercostals and abdominal muscles.
- The diaphragm **contracts and flattens** during inspiration (it does not "rise").
- **Residual volume** cannot be exhaled and cannot be measured by a simple spirometer.
- **Vital capacity excludes RV**; **TLC includes RV**.
- **Gases move only by diffusion** — never by active transport.
- **CO₂ is carried mostly as bicarbonate (70%)**, not as carbamino-Hb.
- **Carbamino-Hb** binds CO₂ to the **globin amino groups**, not to the iron. CO and O₂ bind to the **iron**.
- **Bohr effect = CO₂/H⁺ on O₂ binding; Haldane effect = O₂ on CO₂ binding**. Students often swap them.
- In the chloride shift, **Cl⁻ goes into the RBC at the tissues**, and **HCO₃⁻ goes out**.
- **Foetal Hb has higher affinity** for O₂ (left shift) — so the foetus can take O₂ from the mother.
- **Myoglobin** has a hyperbolic (not sigmoid) curve — single chain, no cooperativity.
- The main breathing stimulus is **CO₂/H⁺ on the medulla**, not low O₂.
- **Pneumotaxic centre** is in the **pons**, not the medulla.
- Right lung has **3 lobes**, left has **2**.
- Tracheal rings are **C-shaped** (incomplete dorsally), of hyaline cartilage.

## Practice with answers

1. Which cells secrete surfactant? — **Type II alveolar cells (pneumocytes)**.
2. Which muscles contract during forced expiration? — **Internal intercostals and abdominal muscles**.
3. TV 500 mL, IRV 3000 mL, ERV 1000 mL, RV 1200 mL — vital capacity? — **4500 mL** (3000 + 500 + 1000).
4. Same values — total lung capacity? — **5700 mL** (VC + RV).

7. pO₂ in alveolar air? — **104 mmHg**.
8. Which form carries most CO₂ in blood? — **Bicarbonate ion in plasma (~70%)**.
9. Which enzyme speeds up CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃? — **Carbonic anhydrase** (zinc-containing).
10. Which ion enters RBCs at the tissues? — **Chloride (Cl⁻)** — chloride shift.
11. Effect of high temperature on the ODC? — **Shifts it to the right** (less affinity, more O₂ released).
12. Why does foetal blood take O₂ from maternal blood? — **HbF has higher O₂ affinity** (left-shifted curve; binds 2,3-BPG poorly).
13. Effect of O₂ binding on CO₂ carriage is called? — **Haldane effect**.
14. Which centre controls the basic rhythm of breathing? — **Respiratory rhythm centre in the medulla**.
15. Which receptors respond mainly to a fall in blood O₂? — **Carotid and aortic bodies** (peripheral chemoreceptors).
16. "The bends" in divers is due to bubbles of which gas? — **Nitrogen**.
17. Which disease results from long exposure to stone dust? — **Silicosis**.
18. Respiratory organ of the cockroach? — **Tracheae** (opening by spiracles).
19. Hb 15 g/100 mL, fully saturated — O₂ content? — **~20 mL O₂ per 100 mL** (15 × 1.34 = 20.1).
20. What is the final electron acceptor in aerobic respiration? — **Oxygen** (forms water).
