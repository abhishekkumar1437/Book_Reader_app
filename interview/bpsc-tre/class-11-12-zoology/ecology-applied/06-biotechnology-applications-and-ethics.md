This chapter covers NCERT class 12 "Biotechnology and its Applications" and the B.Sc. extras that TRE 1.0 liked: who made recombinant insulin and when, which was the first engineered hormone, who developed India's first Bt cotton hybrids, how a child's DNA fingerprint relates to the parents', who invented DNA fingerprinting, and which animal was the first clone from an adult cell. TRE 2.0 asked which region of DNA is compared in fingerprinting.

> **In simple words:** Once we can cut, join and copy genes, we can make useful things. Bacteria can make human insulin for diabetics. Cotton plants can make an insect-killing protein so that farmers spray less poison. Doctors can put a working gene into a patient's cells (gene therapy). Every person's DNA has a unique pattern of repeated pieces, so DNA can identify criminals or settle who is a child's father. A whole sheep (Dolly) was copied from one adult cell. Because all this is powerful, governments regulate it (GEAC in India) and protect their plants and knowledge from biopiracy.

## 1. Biotechnology in agriculture

Three options to increase food production (NCERT): (1) agrochemical-based agriculture, (2) organic agriculture, (3) **genetically engineered crop-based agriculture**. The Green Revolution tripled food supply, but it was not enough for a growing population, and agrochemicals are costly and harmful.

**Genetically modified organisms (GMOs)** — plants, bacteria, fungi and animals whose genes have been altered by manipulation.

**Benefits of GM plants (NCERT):**
- More **tolerant to abiotic stresses** (cold, drought, salt, heat).
- **Reduced reliance on chemical pesticides** (pest-resistant crops).
- Helped to **reduce post-harvest losses**.
- **Increased efficiency of mineral usage** by plants (prevents early exhaustion of soil fertility).
- **Enhanced nutritional value** of food — e.g. **Golden rice** (vitamin A–enriched; contains genes for **β-carotene** synthesis).
- Also used to make tailor-made plants that supply alternative resources (starches, fuels, pharmaceuticals) to industries.

### Bt crops

- ***Bacillus thuringiensis*** (Bt) — a soil bacterium that produces proteins that kill certain insects: **lepidopterans** (tobacco budworm, armyworm, **bollworms**), **coleopterans** (beetles) and **dipterans** (flies, mosquitoes).
- The bacterium forms **protein crystals** during a particular phase of growth; they contain a toxic **insecticidal protein (Bt toxin)** which exists as an **inactive protoxin** in the bacterium (so it does not kill the bacterium itself).
- Once an insect eats it, the **alkaline pH of the insect's gut solubilises the crystals** and the protoxin is converted to the **active toxin**. The toxin binds to the surface of **midgut epithelial cells**, creates **pores**, and causes **cell swelling and lysis** → death of the insect.
- The toxin is coded by **cry genes**. Proteins encoded by **cryIAc and cryIIAb control cotton bollworms**; **cryIAb controls corn borer**.
- Bt toxin is harmless to humans and most other animals because our stomach is **acidic** and we lack the specific gut receptors.

### Pest resistance by RNA interference (RNAi)

- **RNAi** — a method of **cellular defence in all eukaryotic organisms**: it **silences a specific mRNA** by a complementary **double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)** that binds to it and prevents translation. The source of the complementary RNA can be an infection by viruses with RNA genomes or **mobile genetic elements (transposons)** that replicate via an RNA intermediate.
- Discovered by **Andrew Fire and Craig Mello (1998)** in *Caenorhabditis elegans*; **Nobel Prize 2006**.
- **NCERT example**: the nematode ***Meloidogyne incognita*** infects the **roots of tobacco** plants and causes a great reduction in yield. Using ***Agrobacterium* vectors**, nematode-specific genes were introduced into the host plant so that it produced **both sense and antisense RNA**. These two complementary RNAs formed dsRNA, which initiated RNAi and **silenced the specific mRNA of the nematode**. The parasite could not survive in a transgenic host expressing the specific interfering RNA — the plant **protected itself**.

## 2. Biotechnology in medicine

Recombinant DNA technology lets us produce **safe and more effective therapeutic drugs** that do not induce unwanted immunological responses (as happened with products from non-human sources). At present about **30 recombinant therapeutics** have been approved for human use worldwide; in India, **12** are being marketed (NCERT; numbers today are far higher).

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q111 asked: "The most important use of recombinant DNA technology is — (A) cloning of rDNA or cDNA (B) cloning of specific gene (C) cloning of RNA." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (D) More than one of the above** — (A) and (B) are both correct uses; RNA is not cloned directly (it is first copied into cDNA).

### Genetically engineered insulin

- Insulin used for diabetes was earlier extracted from the **pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs**. It caused **allergy** or other reactions in some patients because of foreign protein.
- **Structure**: insulin has **two short polypeptide chains — chain A (21 amino acids) and chain B (30 amino acids)** — linked by **disulphide bridges**. In mammals, including humans, it is made as a **pro-hormone (proinsulin)** with an extra stretch, the **C-peptide**, which is removed during maturation.
- The challenge was to get insulin assembled into its mature form. **In 1983, Eli Lilly, an American company, prepared two DNA sequences corresponding to the A and B chains of human insulin**, introduced them into **plasmids of *E. coli*** to produce the chains separately. Chains A and B were extracted and combined by creating disulphide bonds to form **human insulin** (NCERT). It was marketed as **Humulin**.
- **History note:** the insulin genes were synthesised by **Genentech** (1978), and Humulin received **US FDA approval in October 1982**; NCERT gives **1983** for Eli Lilly's production — **use 1983 in the exam**.
- Insulin was the **first genetically engineered hormone (and first recombinant drug)** approved for humans.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q61 asked: "_____ in 1983 used recombinant DNA technology to produce insulin. — (A) Eli Lilly (B) Emily Lilly (C) Amy Sanger." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (A) Eli Lilly.**

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q62 asked: "Which of the following is the first genetically engineered hormone? — (A) Somatotropin (B) Insulin (C) Oxytocin." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (B) Insulin.** Recombinant human growth hormone (somatotropin) came later (1985).

### Gene therapy

- **Gene therapy** = a collection of methods to **correct a gene defect** diagnosed in a child/embryo. **Genes are inserted into a person's cells and tissues** to treat a hereditary disease; a normal gene is delivered to take over the function of the non-functional gene.
- **First clinical gene therapy — 1990**, for a **4-year-old girl** (Ashanti DeSilva, USA; team of **W. French Anderson**) with **adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency**.
- **ADA** is crucial for the **immune system**; its deficiency (caused by deletion of the ADA gene) causes **severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)**.
- Treatments: **bone-marrow transplantation**, **enzyme replacement therapy** (functional ADA given by injection) — neither is completely curative.
- **Gene therapy procedure**: **lymphocytes** from the patient's blood are grown in culture; a functional **ADA cDNA** is introduced using a **retroviral vector**; the cells are returned to the patient. Because these cells are **not immortal**, the patient needs **periodic infusions**.
- **Permanent cure**: if the gene isolated from marrow cells producing ADA is introduced into cells at **early embryonic stages** (NCERT).
- Today: CAR-T cell therapy for blood cancers, **Luxturna** (inherited blindness), **Zolgensma** (spinal muscular atrophy), and CRISPR-based **Casgevy** (sickle-cell disease, approved 2023).

### Molecular diagnosis

Conventional methods (serum and urine analysis) cannot detect a disease early. Recombinant DNA technology, **PCR** and **ELISA** help in early diagnosis (NCERT):

- **PCR** detects very low numbers of bacteria or viruses (HIV in suspected AIDS patients), **mutations in genes** of suspected cancer patients, and many genetic disorders — before symptoms appear.
- **Probe + autoradiography**: a single-stranded DNA/RNA tagged with a radioactive molecule hybridises to its complementary DNA in a clone of cells; a clone with a mutated gene does not appear on the photographic film.
- **ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay)** — based on the principle of **antigen–antibody interaction**. Infection can be detected by the presence of **antigens** (proteins, glycoproteins of the pathogen) or by **antibodies** synthesised against the pathogen. Developed by **Engvall and Perlmann (1971)**. Used for HIV screening, hepatitis, COVID antibody tests, pregnancy (hCG).

## 3. Transgenic animals

**Transgenic animals** have had their DNA manipulated to possess and express an extra (foreign) gene. Transgenic rats, rabbits, pigs, sheep, cows and fish have been produced, but **over 95% of all existing transgenic animals are mice** (NCERT).

| Purpose (NCERT) | Example |
|---|---|
| **Normal physiology and development** | Genes that regulate growth, e.g. **insulin-like growth factor**; studying the effect of introducing genes |
| **Study of disease** | Models for **cancer, cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's disease** |
| **Biological products** | Human protein **α-1-antitrypsin** to treat **emphysema**; products for **phenylketonuria (PKU)** and **cystic fibrosis**. **Rosie (1997)** — the **first transgenic cow** — produced **human protein-enriched milk (2.4 g per litre)** containing human **α-lactalbumin**, nutritionally more balanced for human babies than natural cow milk |
| **Vaccine safety** | Transgenic mice used to test the safety of the **polio vaccine** (could replace monkeys) |
| **Chemical safety testing (toxicity)** | Transgenic animals carrying genes that make them more sensitive to toxic substances give results faster |

- **First transgenic animal**: a mouse (Rudolf Jaenisch, 1974); "**super mouse**" carrying a rat growth-hormone gene grew to almost twice normal size (Palmiter and Brinster, 1982).
- **Transgenic fish**: GloFish (fluorescent zebrafish, 2003); AquAdvantage salmon (fast-growing, approved in USA 2015).

## 4. Animal cloning

- **Cloning** = making a genetically identical copy of a cell or an organism. **Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT)**: the nucleus of a body (somatic) cell is transferred into an **enucleated egg**; the egg is stimulated (electric pulse) to divide, and the embryo is placed in a surrogate mother.
- **Early milestones**: **Briggs and King (1952)** — nuclear transfer in the frog *Rana pipiens* (from embryonic cells); **John Gurdon (1962)** — cloned *Xenopus* frogs from tadpole **intestinal cell** nuclei (Nobel 2012, with **Shinya Yamanaka** for induced pluripotent stem cells).

## 5. DNA fingerprinting

### Principle

- About **99.9%** of the base sequence is the same in all humans; the differences (about 3 × 10⁶ bp in a genome of 3 × 10⁹ bp) make each person unique.
- **Repetitive DNA** — a small stretch of DNA repeated many times — differs between individuals. It can be separated from bulk genomic DNA as a **separate peak during density-gradient centrifugation**: the bulk DNA forms a major peak and the other small peaks are **satellite DNA**. Satellite DNA is classified by base composition, length of segment and number of repeats into **microsatellites, minisatellites** etc. These sequences normally **do not code for any protein** but form a large portion of the genome. They show a **high degree of polymorphism** and form the basis of DNA fingerprinting (NCERT).
- **VNTR (variable number of tandem repeats)** — a type of minisatellite; the copy number varies between individuals, so the **size of VNTR varies from 0.1 to 20 kb**.
- **Polymorphism** — variation at the genetic level; arises from mutations (inheritable mutations in germ cells). An allelic sequence variation is called a DNA polymorphism if more than one variant (allele) occurs in the population at a frequency greater than **0.01**.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q142 asked: "What are the differences in the specific regions of DNA sequence called during DNA fingerprinting? — (A) Non-repetitive DNA (B) Repetitive DNA (C) Satellite DNA." **Official answer: (B) Repetitive DNA.** NCERT: "DNA fingerprinting involves identifying differences in some specific regions in DNA sequence called as **repetitive DNA**." Satellite DNA is itself a kind of repetitive DNA, so some candidates marked (D); the official final key is **(B)** — follow NCERT's wording.

### Who developed it

- **Alec Jeffreys** (University of Leicester, UK) discovered the technique in **1984** (published 1985) using a **VNTR (minisatellite) probe**. First forensic use: **Colin Pitchfork case (1986–88)** — identified the murderer and freed an innocent suspect; first immigration case (1985).
- In India, **Lalji Singh** (1947–2017) of the **Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology (CCMB), Hyderabad** developed an indigenous DNA fingerprinting probe (from **Bkm sequences** of the banded krait snake) in **1988** — called the **"father of Indian DNA fingerprinting"**. The **Centre for DNA Fingerprinting and Diagnostics (CDFD)**, Hyderabad (1996) grew out of this work. Lalji Singh later became Vice-Chancellor of BHU.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q114 asked: "(Autoantibody) DNA fingerprinting was developed by — (A) Alec Jeffreys (B) A.K. Francoeur (C) R. Erickson." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (A) Alec Jeffreys.** (The word "Autoantibody" in the booklet looks like a printing error.)

### Steps (Southern blot hybridisation with VNTR probe)

1. **Isolation of DNA** (from blood, hair roots, skin, semen, saliva, bone — even a tiny sample, amplified by **PCR**).
2. **Digestion of DNA by restriction endonucleases**.
3. **Separation of DNA fragments by gel electrophoresis**.
4. **Transferring (blotting)** the separated fragments onto synthetic membranes such as **nitrocellulose or nylon** (Southern blotting).
5. **Hybridisation** using a **labelled VNTR probe**.
6. **Detection** of hybridised fragments by **autoradiography** — gives a pattern of dark bands (the "fingerprint").

**Reading a fingerprint.** A child inherits half of the chromosomes from each parent, so **about 50% of the child's bands match the mother and the rest match the father**. Identical twins have identical fingerprints. In the figure, the child's bands that did not come from the mother are present in the father but absent in the "other man", so the other man is excluded.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q64 asked: "The DNA fingerprinting pattern of a child is — (A) exactly similar to that of both of the parents (B) 100% similar to the mother's DNA print (C) 50% bands similar to father and the rest similar to mother." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (C).**

**Applications:** forensic science (crime scene identification), **paternity/maternity disputes**, identification of victims of disasters (and of **Rajiv Gandhi's assassin**, 1991, in India — commonly cited), immigration cases, determining **population and genetic diversities**, evolution and wildlife forensics (poached tiger parts), pedigree of livestock and pets.

## 6. Ethical issues, biosafety and law

### GEAC and the Indian regulatory system

- Genetic modification of organisms can have **unpredictable results** when such organisms are introduced into the ecosystem. The Indian Government has set up organisations such as **GEAC** (Genetic Engineering **Approval** Committee, renamed **Appraisal** Committee in **2010**), which make decisions on the **validity of GM research** and the **safety of introducing GM organisms for public services** (NCERT).
- GEAC works under the **Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change**, under the **Rules, 1989** (manufacture, use, import, export and storage of hazardous micro-organisms, genetically engineered organisms or cells) framed under the **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**.
- Other bodies: **RCGM** (Review Committee on Genetic Manipulation, under the **Department of Biotechnology, DBT** — set up **1986**), **IBSC** (Institutional Biosafety Committees) in every institution, State Biotechnology Coordination Committees and District Level Committees.
- International: **Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety** (adopted 2000, in force 2003) under the CBD — safe transfer and handling of living modified organisms (LMOs).

### Patents and biopiracy

- A **patent** is the right granted by a government to an inventor to prevent others from commercial use of his invention for a fixed period — in India **20 years**. **Indian Patents Act, 1970**, amended 1999, 2002 and **2005** (product patents in pharmaceuticals under the WTO **TRIPS** agreement, 1995). Section **3(d)** blocks "evergreening" (Novartis Glivec case, Supreme Court 2013).
- **Biopiracy** = the term used to refer to the **use of bio-resources by multinational companies and other organisations without proper authorisation from the countries and people concerned, without compensatory payment** (NCERT).
- Industrialised nations are rich financially but **poor in biodiversity and traditional knowledge**; developing and under-developed countries are rich in biodiversity and traditional knowledge related to bio-resources.

| Case | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Basmati rice** | In **1997** an American company (**RiceTec**) got patent rights on basmati rice through the **US Patent and Trademark Office**, allowing it to sell a "new" variety of basmati in the USA and abroad. This "new" variety had actually been **derived from Indian farmers' varieties** — Indian basmati crossed with semi-dwarf varieties — and claimed as an invention. India challenged it; most claims were withdrawn or struck down (2001). India has an estimated **2,00,000 varieties of rice**, of which **27 documented varieties of basmati** are grown in India; basmati is distinct for its unique aroma and flavour (NCERT) |
| **Turmeric** | US patent (1995) on turmeric for wound healing — **revoked in 1997** after the **CSIR** showed it was traditional knowledge (an ancient Sanskrit text and a 1953 Indian journal article) |
| **Neem** | European patent on a neem-based fungicide — **revoked (2000, upheld 2005)** after challenge by Vandana Shiva and others |
| Other | Attempts to patent uses, products and processes based on Indian traditional herbal medicines (turmeric, neem) (NCERT) |

- India's responses: the **Indian Parliament cleared the second amendment of the Indian Patents Bill** to take such issues into consideration — including patent terms, emergency provisions and research and development initiatives (NCERT); **Biological Diversity Act, 2002** (prior approval of the **National Biodiversity Authority** for foreigners to use Indian bio-resources; benefit-sharing); **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)** by CSIR (2001) — documents traditional knowledge in patent-office-friendly languages to block wrong patents; **Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act, 2001**.
- **Bioprospecting** (search for useful bio-resources) is legitimate when done with **prior informed consent** and **benefit-sharing** (Nagoya Protocol, 2010).

### Other ethical concerns

- Use of animals for producing pharmaceutical proteins and in research raises questions of **animal welfare**.
- Possible effects of GM crops on non-target organisms, **gene flow** to wild relatives (superweeds), development of **resistance** in insects (pink bollworm and Bt cotton), farmers' dependence on seed companies, and labelling of GM food.
- **Human cloning** and germ-line editing are banned or restricted in most countries (the 2018 "CRISPR babies" case in China led to the scientist's imprisonment).

## Traps and exceptions

- **Insulin by Eli Lilly — 1983** (NCERT); Humulin FDA approval was 1982 — give 1983 in the exam (TRE 1.0 key A).
- **First genetically engineered hormone = insulin** (TRE 1.0 key B); human growth hormone came later.
- Insulin chains: **A = 21, B = 30** amino acids; **C-peptide** is removed — it is not in mature insulin.
- **Bt toxin is a protoxin** activated by the **alkaline** gut pH of insects; **cryIAc and cryIIAb** — cotton bollworm; **cryIAb** — corn borer.
- **First Bt cotton hybrids — MMBL (Mahyco Monsanto)**, 2002: MECH-12, 162, 184 (TRE 1.0 key A).
- **Bt brinjal** is **not** cultivated in India (moratorium 2010); **Bt cotton** is the only GM crop widely grown.
- RNAi example: ***Meloidogyne incognita*** on **tobacco**, using *Agrobacterium* vectors and **dsRNA** — not Bt toxin.
- First gene therapy — **1990**, **ADA deficiency**, 4-year-old girl, **lymphocytes + retroviral vector**; not permanent.
- **ELISA** works on **antigen–antibody** interaction, not on DNA.
- **Over 95%** of transgenic animals are **mice**; **Rosie (1997)** — first transgenic cow, human α-lactalbumin milk (2.4 g/L).
- **Dolly (1996)** — first mammal cloned from an **adult** cell (TRE 1.0 key C); **Polly** was **transgenic**, cloned from fetal cells.
- DNA fingerprinting compares **repetitive DNA** (TRE 2.0 key B), especially **VNTRs**; it does not compare the genes that code for proteins.
- Child: **50% bands from mother, 50% from father** (TRE 1.0 key C).
- **Alec Jeffreys** — DNA fingerprinting (1984, TRE 1.0 key A); **Lalji Singh** — Indian DNA fingerprinting (CCMB).
- **GEAC** — "Approval" became "**Appraisal**" in 2010; under **MoEFCC**, not DBT. RCGM is under DBT.
- **Biopiracy** examples — basmati (1997, RiceTec), turmeric (revoked 1997), neem.

4. cryIAc, cryIIAb — cotton bollworm; cryIAb — corn borer.

## Practice with answers

1. What activates the Bt protoxin inside an insect? — **The alkaline pH of the insect midgut** (it solubilises the crystals; gut proteases then release the active toxin).
2. Which cry genes protect cotton from bollworms? — ***cryIAc* and *cryIIAb***.
3. Which company developed India's first Bt cotton hybrids? — **Mahyco Monsanto Biotech (India) Ltd** (TRE 1.0 key A).
4. Name the nematode controlled by RNAi in tobacco. — ***Meloidogyne incognita***.
5. Which company produced human insulin by rDNA in 1983? — **Eli Lilly** (TRE 1.0 key A).
6. How many amino acids are in chains A and B of insulin? — **21 and 30**.
7. What is removed from proinsulin to make insulin? — **C-peptide**.
8. Which was the first recombinant vaccine and in which host? — **Hepatitis B vaccine, in yeast**.
9. Deficiency of which enzyme was treated by the first gene therapy? — **Adenosine deaminase (ADA)**.
10. Which vector was used in ADA gene therapy? — **Retroviral vector**.
11. ELISA is based on which principle? — **Antigen–antibody interaction**.
12. What percentage of transgenic animals are mice? — **Over 95%**.
13. What did Rosie the cow's milk contain? — **Human α-lactalbumin (2.4 g/L)**.
14. From which cell was Dolly's nucleus taken? — **Mammary (udder) cell of a Finn Dorset ewe**.
15. Which region of DNA is compared in DNA fingerprinting? — **Repetitive DNA (VNTRs/satellite DNA)** (TRE 2.0 key B).
16. A child shows six bands; three match the mother. What should the other three match? — **The biological father**.
17. Who is known as the father of Indian DNA fingerprinting? — **Lalji Singh** (CCMB, Hyderabad).
18. Which committee in India approves the release of GM crops? — **GEAC** (Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee).
19. In which year did RiceTec get a US patent on basmati? — **1997**.
