This chapter covers NCERT class 12 "Biodiversity and Conservation" and extends it to B.Sc. conservation biology and the Bihar-specific facts that BPSC loves. TRE 2.0 asked three questions here (in-situ conservation, main reason for wildlife decline, India's hotspots) and TRE 1.0 asked where the Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary is, how viable material of endangered species is preserved, the government's step to protect wildlife and the dodo–*Calvaria* story.

> **In simple words:** Biodiversity is the variety of life — different genes within a species, different species, and different ecosystems. Tropical countries like India are very rich in it. We are losing species mainly because we destroy their homes (forests, wetlands). We protect species either in their natural home (national parks, sanctuaries, biosphere reserves) or outside it (zoos, seed banks, gene banks, frozen sperm and embryos). Bihar has one national park and tiger reserve (Valmiki), India's only dolphin sanctuary (Vikramshila, Bhagalpur) and several bird sanctuaries and Ramsar wetlands.

## 1. Biodiversity — meaning and levels

- The term **biodiversity** was popularised by the sociobiologist **Edward O. Wilson** (1988) (the word "biological diversity" was used earlier by Thomas Lovejoy and others; "BioDiversity" was coined by Walter G. Rosen in 1985).
- **Biodiversity** = the variety of living organisms at all levels of biological organisation.

| Level | Meaning | Example (NCERT) |
|---|---|---|
| **Genetic diversity** | Variation in genes within a species | ***Rauwolfia vomitoria*** in different Himalayan ranges varies in the amount and potency of **reserpine**; India has more than **50,000 genetically different strains of rice** and about **1,000 varieties of mango** |
| **Species diversity** | Variety of species in a region | The **Western Ghats** have greater amphibian species diversity than the **Eastern Ghats** |
| **Ecological diversity** | Variety of ecosystems | India's deserts, rainforests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries and alpine meadows — far more than a Scandinavian country like Norway |

**Ecologists' diversity measures (Whittaker, 1960s):** **alpha (α) diversity** — within a community; **beta (β) diversity** — between communities along a gradient; **gamma (γ) diversity** — total diversity of a whole region.

## 2. How many species are there?

| Figure | Value (NCERT and IUCN) |
|---|---|
| Species described (IUCN 2004) | **Slightly more than 1.5 million** |
| Estimated total (Robert May) | About **7 million** (other estimates 20–50 million) |
| Animals vs plants | More than **70% of all recorded species are animals**; plants (including algae, fungi, bryophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms) about 22% |
| Largest animal group | **Insects** — more than **70% of animals** (out of every 10 animals, 7 are insects) |
| Fungi | More species than fishes, amphibians, reptiles and mammals combined |
| India's share | **2.4% of the world's land area** but **8.1% of global species diversity** — one of the **17 megadiverse countries** |
| India's recorded species | About **45,000 plants** and **twice as many animals** (NCERT); ZSI's recent counts exceed **1,04,000 animal species** (check latest "Animal Discoveries") |
| Undiscovered in India | May be more than **1,00,000 plant and 3,00,000 animal species** still to be described (NCERT, using May's ratio) |

> **Remember:** Robert May's estimate of global species (about 7 million) is based on the proportion of insects to plants in the temperate versus tropical regions — not on direct counting.

## 3. Patterns of biodiversity

### Latitudinal gradient

Species diversity **decreases from the equator towards the poles**. Examples: **Colombia** (near the equator) has about **1,400 bird species**, **New York** (41° N) about **105** and **Greenland** (71° N) only about **56**; **India** (tropical) has more than **1,200 bird species**. The **Amazon rainforest** is the richest on Earth — more than 40,000 plant species, 3,000 fishes, 1,300 birds, 427 mammals, 427 amphibians, 378 reptiles and more than 1,25,000 invertebrates (NCERT).

**Why the tropics are richer (three hypotheses, NCERT):**
1. **Speciation is a function of time** — temperate regions were repeatedly glaciated, while tropics remained undisturbed for millions of years.
2. Tropical environments are **less seasonal, more constant and predictable** — this promotes niche specialisation.
3. **More solar energy** is available in the tropics → higher productivity → indirectly more diversity.

### Species–area relationship

**Alexander von Humboldt** observed that within a region, species richness increases with explored area, but only up to a limit. On a log scale the relation is a straight line.

<div class="formula">

**S = C A^Z**  or  **log S = log C + Z log A**

S = species richness · A = area · C = Y-intercept · **Z = slope (regression coefficient)**

**Z ≈ 0.1–0.2** for smaller areas (regardless of taxon or region); **Z ≈ 0.6–1.2** for very large areas such as entire continents (e.g. frugivorous birds and mammals of tropical forests on different continents: **Z = 1.15**).

</div>

**Worked example.** With Z = 0.25, a habitat reduced to one-tenth of its area keeps S₂/S₁ = (0.1)^0.25 ≈ 0.56 — that is, about **44% of species would eventually be lost**. (Use this logic for "why fragmentation causes extinction".)

### Importance of species richness (stability)

- **David Tilman**'s long-term outdoor plot experiments: plots with **more species showed less year-to-year variation in total biomass**, and greater diversity contributed to **higher productivity**.
- **Rivet popper hypothesis** — **Paul Ehrlich** (Stanford ecologist): the ecosystem is an aeroplane and species are the rivets. Losing a few rivets (species) may not affect flight safety, but as more are lost, the plane becomes dangerous; loss of rivets on the **wings (key species driving major functions)** is more serious than loss on the seats.

## 4. Why conserve biodiversity?

| Reason | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Narrowly utilitarian** | Direct economic benefits — food (cereals, pulses, fruits), firewood, fibre, construction material, industrial products (tannins, lubricants, dyes, resins, perfumes) and medicines. **More than 25% of drugs** sold in the world market are derived from plants, and **25,000 species of plants** contribute to traditional medicine of native peoples. **Bioprospecting** — exploring molecular, genetic and species-level diversity for products of economic importance |
| **Broadly utilitarian** | Ecosystem services: the **Amazon forest** is estimated to produce **20% of the total O₂** in Earth's atmosphere through photosynthesis (NCERT); pollination by bees, bumblebees, birds and bats; aesthetic pleasures; flood and erosion control |
| **Ethical** | Every species has an intrinsic value; we have a moral duty to pass on our biological legacy to future generations |

> **Common mistake:** The "Amazon produces 20% of the world's oxygen" line is NCERT's; ecologists note that the forest also consumes most of that oxygen in respiration. In the exam, give NCERT's figure.

## 5. Loss of biodiversity

- The **IUCN Red List (2004)** documents the **extinction of 784 species** in the last 500 years — **338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates and 87 plants** (NCERT).
- Recent extinctions: **dodo** (Mauritius), **quagga** (Africa), **thylacine** (Tasmanian wolf, Australia), **Steller's sea cow** (Russia), three subspecies of tiger (**Bali, Javan, Caspian**). In the last twenty years alone, 27 species have disappeared.
- At present, **12% of all bird species, 23% of mammals, 32% of amphibians and 31% of gymnosperms** face the threat of extinction (NCERT/IUCN).
- **Amphibians** appear to be the **most vulnerable** group.
- **Sixth mass extinction**: the present rate is estimated to be **100 to 1,000 times faster** than pre-human times, and humans are the cause.

**Effects of losing biodiversity (NCERT):** decline in plant production, lower resistance to environmental perturbations such as drought, and increased variability in ecosystem processes such as plant productivity, water use, pest and disease cycles.

### Causes — "The Evil Quartet"

The phrase "**evil quartet**" for the four major causes was coined by **Jared Diamond**.

| Cause | Detail and examples |
|---|---|
| **1. Habitat loss and fragmentation** — the **most important cause** | Tropical rainforests once covered more than **14% of Earth's land surface, now no more than 6%**; the **Amazon rainforest** ("lungs of the planet") is being cut for soybean cultivation and cattle grazing. Fragmentation hurts animals needing large territories (mammals, birds) and migratory species |
| **2. Over-exploitation** | **Steller's sea cow** and **passenger pigeon** were driven extinct by over-hunting; many marine fish populations are over-harvested |
| **3. Alien species invasions** | **Nile perch** introduced into **Lake Victoria** (East Africa) caused the extinction of **more than 200 species of cichlid fish**; **Parthenium** (carrot grass), **Lantana** and **water hyacinth (*Eichhornia*)** threaten native species; illegal introduction of the **African catfish *Clarias gariepinus*** for aquaculture threatens indigenous catfishes of Indian rivers |
| **4. Co-extinctions** | When a species becomes extinct, plants and animals obligately associated with it also become extinct — a host fish and its unique assemblage of parasites; a plant and its obligate pollinator (co-evolved mutualism) |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q125 asked: "The number of wildlife is continuously decreasing. What is the main reason? — (A) Hunting (B) Cutting down of forests (C) Destruction of habitat." **Official answer: (C) Destruction of habitat.** Cutting forests is one *form* of habitat destruction and hunting is over-exploitation; NCERT names **habitat loss and fragmentation** as the most important cause.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q119 asked: "Which of the following trees got nearly extinct with the extinction of the dodo animal? — (A) Calvaria (B) Palm tree (C) Cinchona." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (A) *Calvaria*** (*Sideroxylon grandiflorum*, "tambalacoque", Mauritius). The story (Stanley Temple, 1977) says *Calvaria* seeds germinated only after passing through the dodo's gut, so the tree declined after the dodo died out (1681) — a textbook example of **co-extinction / mutualism**. Later botanists questioned it (some seeds germinate without passage through a gut), but for the exam the answer is *Calvaria*. *Cinchona* is the quinine tree.

## 6. IUCN Red List categories

The **International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN)**, founded **1948**, headquarters **Gland, Switzerland**, maintains the **Red List** (the printed version was the **Red Data Book**, first issued 1964 for mammals and birds).

| Category | Code | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Extinct | **EX** | No reasonable doubt that the last individual has died (dodo, passenger pigeon) |
| Extinct in the Wild | **EW** | Survives only in captivity or outside its natural range |
| Critically Endangered | **CR** | Extremely high risk of extinction in the wild — e.g. **Great Indian Bustard**, **gharial**, white-rumped vulture, pygmy hog |
| Endangered | **EN** | Very high risk — e.g. **Bengal tiger**, **Asian elephant**, **Gangetic river dolphin**, red panda, lion-tailed macaque |
| Vulnerable | **VU** | High risk — e.g. **one-horned rhinoceros**, **snow leopard**, **gaur** (Bihar's state animal), sloth bear |
| Near Threatened | NT | Close to qualifying for a threatened category |
| Least Concern | LC | Widespread and abundant (house crow) |
| Data Deficient | DD | Not enough information |
| Not Evaluated | NE | Not yet assessed |

- **"Threatened"** = CR + EN + VU together.
- Criteria include population reduction, small geographic range, small population size and quantitative analysis of extinction probability.
- India's **Schedule I** of the Wildlife (Protection) Act gives the highest legal protection (tiger, dolphin, rhino, Great Indian Bustard, etc.).

> **Exam angle:** IUCN statuses are revised; check the current Red List before the exam for species often asked (tiger, lion, dolphin, rhino, Great Indian Bustard).

## 7. Biodiversity hotspots

- Concept of **Norman Myers (1988)**; criteria later set by **Conservation International**: a region must have at least **1,500 species of endemic vascular plants** (more than 0.5% of the world total) **and** must have lost **at least 70% of its original habitat**.
- Originally **25** hotspots (NCERT, 2000); now **36** worldwide. Together they cover **less than 2.5% of Earth's land area** (NCERT says less than 2%), yet hold very high species richness and endemism. Strict protection of hotspots could **reduce ongoing mass extinctions by almost 30%** (NCERT).
- **Endemic species** — found only in one region and nowhere else (lion-tailed macaque — Western Ghats; Nilgiri tahr; hoolock gibbon — North-East in India).

| India's hotspot | Area covered in India | Endemic examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Western Ghats and Sri Lanka** | Western Ghats (Gujarat to Kerala and Tamil Nadu) | Lion-tailed macaque, Nilgiri tahr, Malabar civet, many frogs and caecilians |
| **Himalaya** | Whole Indian Himalaya (NCERT's "Eastern Himalaya" was expanded to Himalaya) | Red panda, snow leopard, musk deer |
| **Indo-Burma** | North-East India (except Assam's Brahmaputra plains in some maps), Andaman Islands | Hoolock gibbon, golden langur, white-winged wood duck |
| **Sundaland** | **Nicobar Islands** | Nicobar megapode, Nicobar pigeon |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q126 asked: "Which of the following is a major ecological hot-spot of India? — (A) Eastern Himalayas and Gangetic Plain (B) Western Ghats and Eastern Himalayas (C) Trans-Himalayas and Deccan Peninsula." **Official answer: (B) Western Ghats and Eastern Himalayas** — exactly NCERT's older list of India's hotspots ("Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma and Himalaya"). The Gangetic Plain, Trans-Himalaya and Deccan are biogeographic zones, not hotspots.

### India's ten biogeographic zones (Rodgers and Panwar, 1988)

Trans-Himalaya, Himalaya, Desert, Semi-arid, **Western Ghats**, Deccan Peninsula (largest zone), **Gangetic Plain** (Bihar falls here, with a small Himalayan strip in West Champaran), Coasts, North-East, Islands.

## 8. Conservation — in situ and ex situ

<div class="formula">

**In situ** ("on site") = protecting species **in their natural habitat** — protects the whole ecosystem.

**Ex situ** ("off site") = protecting threatened species **outside** their natural habitat, under human care.

</div>

| In situ | Ex situ |
|---|---|
| **National parks** | **Zoological parks** and safari parks |
| **Wildlife sanctuaries** | **Botanical gardens** |
| **Biosphere reserves** | **Seed banks**, gene banks, **field gene banks** |
| Tiger reserves, elephant reserves | **Cryopreservation** of gametes, embryos and tissues at **−196 °C (liquid nitrogen)** |
| Conservation and community reserves | **Tissue culture** and micropropagation |
| **Sacred groves** | In-vitro fertilisation, embryo transfer, captive breeding (e.g. vultures at Pinjore, gharial at Kukrail) |
| **Ramsar wetlands**, marine protected areas | Pollen banks, DNA banks |

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q124 asked: "Which of the following is an in situ method of conservation? — (A) National park (B) Botanical garden (C) Tissue culture." **Official answer: (A) National park.** Botanical gardens and tissue culture are **ex situ**.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q70 asked: "How can viable material of endangered species be preserved? — (A) By cloning (B) By gene bank (C) By gene pool." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (B) By gene bank.** A **gene bank** stores seeds, pollen, sperm, eggs and embryos (cryopreserved) for long periods. A "gene pool" is just the total genes of a population (not a storage method); cloning can copy an individual but is not a routine preservation method.

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q67 asked: "Which of the following is a step taken by the Government to protect the wildlife? — (A) control the population of animals in the forest (B) proper food facilities for the animals in the forest (C) various projects to protect animals in the forest." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (C).** Examples: Project Tiger, Project Elephant, Project Crocodile, Project Dolphin, Project Cheetah.

### Sacred groves

Tracts of forest set aside by tribal communities, where all trees and wildlife are venerated and fully protected (NCERT): **Khasi and Jaintia Hills** (Meghalaya), **Aravalli Hills** (Rajasthan), **Western Ghats** of Karnataka and Maharashtra, and **Sarguja, Chanda and Bastar** areas (Madhya Pradesh/Chhattisgarh). In Meghalaya, sacred groves are the last refuges for many rare and threatened plants.

### Ex-situ institutions

| Institution | Place | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **NBPGR** (National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources) | New Delhi | National gene bank for crops |
| **NBAGR** (Animal Genetic Resources) | **Karnal** | Registers livestock breeds |
| **NBFGR** (Fish Genetic Resources) | **Lucknow** | Fish gene bank |
| **Svalbard Global Seed Vault** | Norway (Arctic) | "Doomsday vault" for seeds |
| LaCONES (CCMB) | Hyderabad | Laboratory for the Conservation of Endangered Species — frozen gametes, DNA bank |

| Type | Key features | First / largest |
|---|---|---|

## 10. Wildlife of Bihar

Bihar lies mostly in the **Gangetic Plain** biogeographic zone with a small Himalayan foothill (Shivalik) strip in **West Champaran**. Its forest cover is only about **7–8%** of its area (ISFR 2023), among the lowest in India — mainly in **West Champaran (Valmiki)** and the southern hills (**Kaimur, Rohtas, Munger, Jamui, Banka, Nawada, Gaya**).

### Protected areas

| Area | District | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Valmiki National Park and Tiger Reserve (VTR)** | **West Champaran** | **Bihar's only national park and only tiger reserve**; listed as India's **18th tiger reserve**; sanctuary 1978, national park around 1989–90 (dates vary in sources); about **900 km²** tiger reserve; borders **Chitwan NP (Nepal)** and **Sohagi Barwa (UP)**; between the **Gandak** river and the Someshwar–Dun hills; **sal** forests and Terai grasslands; **54 tigers** in the 2022 estimation (31 in 2018); also leopard, sloth bear, gaur, sambar, chital, hornbills; occasional one-horned rhinos from Chitwan |
| **Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary (VGDS)** | **Bhagalpur** | Established **1991**; about **50–60 km** stretch of the Ganga from **Sultanganj to Kahalgaon**; **India's only protected area specifically for the Gangetic dolphin**; also smooth-coated otters, gharial (occasional), freshwater turtles and waterbirds |
| **Kaimur Wildlife Sanctuary** | **Kaimur and Rohtas** | **Largest sanctuary of Bihar** (about 1,340 km²); Vindhyan plateau; dry deciduous forest; proposed **second tiger reserve** (NTCA in-principle approval 2024 — check final notification) |

### The Gangetic dolphin

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Name | ***Platanista gangetica*** (the Ganges river dolphin; the Indus dolphin is now treated as a separate species, *P. minor*); locally "**soons**" or "susu" (from the sound of its breathing) |
| Status | **National aquatic animal (5 October 2009)**; **Endangered (IUCN)**; **Schedule I** of the Wildlife (Protection) Act |
| Biology | A **mammal** — breathes air, surfaces every 30–120 seconds; **practically blind** (eyes lack a crystalline lens), so it navigates and hunts by **echolocation** (ultrasonic clicks); long beak with many teeth; females larger than males; feeds on fish and prawns |
| Habitat | Ganga–Brahmaputra–Meghna and Karnaphuli–Sangu river systems of India, Nepal and Bangladesh; prefers deep pools and eddies at river confluences |
| Threats | Dams and barrages (**Farakka**) fragmenting habitat, by-catch in fishing nets, poaching for **oil** (used as fish bait), pollution, sand mining, low water flow |
| Bihar | Bihar's stretch of the Ganga and its tributaries (Gandak, Kosi, Ghaghra, Son) holds one of the largest dolphin populations in India; the first all-India river dolphin estimate under Project Dolphin (released March 2025) counted about **6,300** river dolphins, with **Uttar Pradesh and Bihar** holding the most — verify the state figures; **National Dolphin Research Centre (NDRC)** at **Patna** (Patna University campus; opened 2024 — check) |
| Day | **National Dolphin Day — 5 October** (from 2022) |

## Traps and exceptions

- **National park = in situ**; botanical garden, zoo, seed bank, tissue culture, cryopreservation = **ex situ** (TRE 2.0 key A).
- **Main reason for wildlife decline = habitat destruction** (TRE 2.0 key C), not hunting.
- India's hotspots — **Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Sundaland (Nicobar)**; the Gangetic Plain is **not** a hotspot (TRE 2.0 key B).
- Hotspot criteria are **two together**: ≥ 1,500 endemic vascular plants **and** ≥ 70% habitat lost.
- **Gene bank** preserves viable material (TRE 1.0 key B); a gene pool is not a storage method.
- **Dodo → *Calvaria*** (TRE 1.0 key A) — co-extinction story; *Cinchona* is unrelated.
- **Nile perch — Lake Victoria — cichlids**; **African catfish *Clarias gariepinus*** — threat to Indian catfishes.
- India: **2.4%** land, **8.1%** species — do not reverse.
- **Z = 0.1–0.2** for small areas; **0.6–1.2** for continents.
- **Rivet popper — Paul Ehrlich**; stability experiments — **David Tilman**; hotspots — **Norman Myers**; species–area — **Humboldt**; estimate of 7 million species — **Robert May**; evil quartet phrase — **Jared Diamond**.
- **Valmiki** is a national park and tiger reserve, **not** a biosphere reserve. **Bihar has no biosphere reserve.**
- **Vikramshila sanctuary** is for **dolphins** (Bhagalpur, 1991) — not birds, and not in West Bengal or UP (TRE 1.0 key B).
- **Kaimur** is Bihar's **largest** sanctuary; **Valmiki** its only national park.
- **Kanwar Jheel** (Begusarai) — Bihar's **first** Ramsar site (2020).
- The Gangetic dolphin is a **mammal**, nearly **blind**, uses **echolocation**; national aquatic animal **2009**.
- **First national park** — Hailey (1936) = Corbett; **first biosphere reserve** — Nilgiri (1986).
