This chapter is the base of the whole ecology section: the parts of an ecosystem, how much energy plants trap, how that energy moves up food chains, how pyramids are drawn, what pond and desert ecosystems look like, and how communities change over time (succession). The level runs from NCERT class 12 (chapter "Ecosystem") to B.Sc. ecology (Lindeman, Odum, ecological efficiencies). TRE asks it as one-liners: "desert ecosystem is characterised by", "which pyramid is always upright", "10% law was given by".

> **In simple words:** An ecosystem is a set of living things (plants, animals, microbes) living together with their non-living surroundings (sunlight, water, soil, air). Green plants catch sunlight and make food. Animals eat the plants or each other. When anything dies, bacteria and fungi break it down and give the minerals back to the soil. Energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat — it flows one way. Minerals go round and round — they cycle. At each step of a food chain about 90% of the energy is lost, so food chains are short.

> **Remember:** Levels of organisation in ecology — **organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere**. A **population** is one species in an area; a **community** is all populations of an area.

## 2. Structure of an ecosystem

### Abiotic (non-living) components

- **Climatic factors**: light, temperature, rainfall, humidity, wind.
- **Edaphic factors** (soil): soil texture, pH, minerals, water-holding capacity.
- **Topographic factors**: altitude, slope, direction of slope.
- **Inorganic substances**: C, N, P, S, water, CO₂, O₂. **Organic substances**: proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, humus (they link biotic and abiotic parts).
- **Standing state** — amount of nutrients (C, N, P, Ca) present in the soil at a given time. **Standing crop** — mass of living material in a trophic level at a given time (measured as biomass or number per unit area).

### Biotic (living) components

| Component | Also called | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Producers** | Autotrophs, transducers (they convert light energy into chemical energy) | Green plants, phytoplankton, cyanobacteria; chemosynthetic bacteria in deep-sea vents | Make food |
| **Primary consumers** | Herbivores | Grasshopper, deer, cow, zooplankton | Eat producers |
| **Secondary consumers** | Primary carnivores | Frog, small fish, snake | Eat herbivores |
| **Tertiary consumers** | Top carnivores | Hawk, tiger, large fish | Eat carnivores |
| **Decomposers** | Saprotrophs, microconsumers, reducers | **Bacteria and fungi** | Break dead matter into inorganic nutrients |
| **Detritivores** | — | **Earthworm**, termites, millipedes, dung beetles | Eat and fragment detritus (dead remains) |

> **In simple words:** Decomposers digest food **outside** their body (they release enzymes on dead matter and absorb the products). Detritivores **eat** pieces of dead matter. Earthworm is a detritivore, not a decomposer in the strict sense.

### Stratification

The **vertical layering** of plants and animals in an ecosystem. A tropical forest has emergent trees, canopy, under-storey, shrubs, herbs and the forest floor. A pond has surface, water column and bottom layers. Stratification allows more species to share the same ground area.

## 3. Functions of an ecosystem

An ecosystem performs four basic functions (NCERT): **(1) productivity, (2) decomposition, (3) energy flow, (4) nutrient cycling.**

### 3.1 Productivity

| Term | Definition | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary production** | Amount of biomass (organic matter) produced by plants per unit area over a time period | g m⁻² or kcal m⁻² |
| **Productivity** | The **rate** of biomass production | g m⁻² yr⁻¹ or kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹ |
| **Gross primary productivity (GPP)** | Rate of production of organic matter **during photosynthesis** (total) | — |
| **Net primary productivity (NPP)** | GPP minus respiration loss (R) of the plants: **NPP = GPP − R** | Available to herbivores and decomposers |
| **Secondary productivity** | Rate of formation of new organic matter by **consumers** | — |

<div class="formula">

**NPP = GPP − R**

**Secondary productivity** = rate at which consumers build new body mass (assimilation − respiration).

**Ecological (Lindeman) efficiency** = energy at level n ÷ energy at level (n − 1) × 100 ≈ 10%

</div>

**Worked example 1.** A crop fixes 20,000 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹ by photosynthesis and uses 8,000 kcal in its own respiration. NPP = 20,000 − 8,000 = **12,000 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹**. If herbivores get 10% of NPP, they get **1,200 kcal**.

**Worked example 2.** NPP of a grassland = 6,000 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹; plant respiration = 2,000 kcal. GPP = NPP + R = **8,000 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹**.

**Key numbers (NCERT and standard texts):**

- Annual **NPP of the whole biosphere ≈ 170 billion tonnes** (dry weight) of organic matter.
- **Oceans** cover about 70% of the surface but produce only about **55 billion tonnes** — because nutrients (especially N and P) are scarce in open ocean, and light falls off with depth.
- Plants capture only **2–10% of photosynthetically active radiation (PAR)**; PAR is **less than 50%** of incident sunlight. Overall, about 1% of sunlight falling on Earth is fixed by plants.
- Productivity depends on the plant species, light, temperature, water, nutrients and photosynthetic efficiency.

| Ecosystem | Approximate NPP (g dry matter m⁻² yr⁻¹) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Tropical rainforest | ~2,000–2,200 | Highest among large land ecosystems |
| Estuaries, coral reefs, swamps and marshes | ~1,500–2,500 | Among the most productive ecosystems per unit area |
| Temperate forest | ~1,200 | — |
| Savanna / grassland | ~600–900 | — |
| Cultivated land | ~650 | Varies greatly with fertiliser and water |
| Open ocean | ~125 | Low per m², large in total area |
| Desert and tundra | < 100 (desert ~90, extreme desert ~3) | Lowest |

> **Exam angle:** "Most productive ecosystem per unit area" — **tropical rainforest** among land biomes; **coral reefs and estuaries** among all ecosystems. "Least productive" — **deserts** (and deep open ocean). "Largest total NPP" — tropical rainforest (land) and open ocean (because of huge area). These are approximate figures from Whittaker's tables; books differ slightly.

### 3.2 Decomposition

**Decomposition** is the breakdown of complex organic matter (detritus: dead leaves, bark, flowers, dead animals, faecal matter) into inorganic substances such as CO₂, water and nutrients.

| Step | What happens | Done by |
|---|---|---|
| **Fragmentation** | Detritus broken into small pieces | Detritivores (earthworm) |
| **Leaching** | Water-soluble inorganic nutrients go down into the soil and settle as unavailable salts | Physical process |
| **Catabolism** | Enzymes break detritus into simpler inorganic substances | Bacteria and fungi |
| **Humification** | Forms **humus** — dark, amorphous, colloidal, highly **resistant** to microbes; reservoir of nutrients | Microbes |
| **Mineralisation** | Humus is slowly degraded and **inorganic nutrients are released** | Microbes |

- Decomposition is **fast** when detritus is rich in **nitrogen and water-soluble sugars**, and in **warm, moist** conditions.
- It is **slow** when detritus is rich in **lignin and chitin**, and in **cold, dry or anaerobic** conditions (that is why peat and fossil fuels accumulate).
- Decomposition is largely an **oxygen-requiring** process.

### 3.3 Energy flow

- The **Sun is the only source of energy** for almost all ecosystems (deep-sea hydrothermal vent communities run on chemosynthesis).
- Energy flow is **unidirectional**: Sun → producers → herbivores → carnivores. It never returns to the Sun. This follows the **laws of thermodynamics**: the first law (energy is neither created nor destroyed, only converted) and the second law (every conversion loses some energy as heat, so order decreases).
- Each organism uses most of the energy it gets for its own **respiration and life activities**; this is lost as heat. Only the energy stored in new body tissue is available to the next level.

### 3.4 Lindeman's 10% law

**Raymond Lindeman (1942)** studied energy flow in **Cedar Bog Lake** (Minnesota, USA) and showed that only about **10%** of the energy at one trophic level is stored in the next. This is the **10% law** (the real value ranges from about 5% to 20%).

**Worked example 3 (going up).** Producers fix **50,000 J**. Primary consumers get 5,000 J; secondary consumers 500 J; tertiary consumers **50 J**.

**Worked example 4 (going down).** In the chain grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk, the hawk is at the **fifth trophic level** and stores 20 J. There are **four transfers** between grass and hawk, so the grass must have fixed 20 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = **2,00,000 J**.

**Worked example 5 (efficiency).** Herbivores store 1,200 kcal from 12,000 kcal of NPP. Efficiency = 1,200 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = **10%**.

**Worked example 6 (how many levels).** If producers fix 1,00,000 kcal and a top carnivore needs at least 10 kcal to survive, how many trophic levels are possible? 1,00,000 → 10,000 → 1,000 → 100 → 10: **five levels**. This is why food chains rarely go beyond **4–5 levels**.

> **Memory trick:** "Ten percent, ten times less" — each step up, divide by 10; each step down (to find what producers needed), multiply by 10.

### Ecological efficiencies (B.Sc.)

| Efficiency | Formula (in words) | Typical value |
|---|---|---|
| **Photosynthetic efficiency** | Energy fixed in GPP ÷ incident light (or PAR) | 1–5% (2–10% of PAR) |
| **Assimilation efficiency** | Energy assimilated ÷ energy ingested | Herbivores ~20–50%; carnivores ~80% |
| **Net production efficiency** | Energy stored in new tissue ÷ energy assimilated | Warm-blooded (homeotherms) ~1–3%; insects ~40% |
| **Lindeman (trophic-level) efficiency** | Production at level n ÷ production at level n − 1 | ~10% |

> **Exam angle:** Warm-blooded animals (birds, mammals) spend most energy on keeping body temperature, so their production efficiency is low; that is why a meat diet from mammals costs more plant energy.

## 4. Food chains, food webs and trophic levels

### Food chain

A **food chain** is a sequence of organisms in which each one eats the one before it and is eaten by the next.

| Type | Starts from | Example | Where dominant |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grazing food chain (GFC)** | Living green plants | Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk; Phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → large fish | **Aquatic** ecosystems (major conduit of energy) |
| **Detritus food chain (DFC)** | Dead organic matter (detritus) | Dead leaves → earthworm → bird; Detritus → bacteria/fungi | **Terrestrial** ecosystems — **much larger fraction of energy flows through the DFC** than through the GFC (NCERT) |
| **Parasitic food chain** | Host → parasites | Tree → bird → lice → bacteria | — (pyramid of number inverted) |

- The two chains are **linked**: some organisms of the DFC are prey of the GFC (a bird can eat an earthworm).
- **Trophic level** — the position (step) of an organism in a food chain: producers (T1), herbivores (T2), primary carnivores (T3), secondary carnivores (T4).
- An organism can occupy **more than one trophic level** at the same time (sparrow eats seeds as T2 and insects as T3). **Man** is an **omnivore** at T2, T3 and higher.

### Food web

A **food web** is a network of interconnected food chains. It is more realistic and makes the ecosystem **more stable**: if one prey becomes scarce, a predator can switch to another. A **keystone species** is one whose removal changes the whole community far more than its numbers would suggest (e.g. sea star *Pisaster* — Robert Paine, 1966; tiger in Indian forests; fig trees in tropical forests).

> **Exam angle:** Number of food chains in a food web: count every path from a producer to a top consumer that is not eaten by anyone. Practise on any NCERT diagram.

| Pyramid | Measures | Usually | Inverted in | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Number** | Individuals per unit area | Upright — **grassland**, pond | **Tree ecosystem** (one tree → many insects → fewer birds: spindle-shaped/partly inverted); **parasitic chain** (one host → many parasites: fully inverted) | Size of the producer is huge |
| **Biomass** | Dry weight of standing crop (g m⁻²) | Upright — **land** (forest, grassland) | **Sea / pond (aquatic)** — phytoplankton biomass less than zooplankton and fish | Phytoplankton have a very short life and fast turnover; the **standing crop** is small at any moment though **production** is high |
| **Energy** | Energy flow per unit area per unit time (kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹) | **Always upright** | **Never inverted** | 10% law; energy is lost at each step |

- The **pyramid of energy** gives the best picture of the functional nature of a community because it includes the **time factor** (rate of flow).
- The classic NCERT figure for the sea: phytoplankton **4**, zooplankton **21**, fish **25** units of biomass (an inverted pyramid of biomass from the English Channel data).

**Limitations of ecological pyramids** (NCERT):
1. They do not take into account the **same species belonging to two or more trophic levels**.
2. They assume a **simple food chain**, which almost never exists in nature; they do not accommodate a **food web**.
3. **Saprophytes (decomposers)** are not given any place, though they play a vital role.

> **Exam angle:** The standard one-liner is "Which pyramid is always upright? — **Pyramid of energy**." The real TRE 2.0 ecology questions (Q123–129, 136, 137) are quoted in chapters 02–04.

## 6. Major ecosystem types

### 6.1 Classification

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Terrestrial** | Forest (tropical rainforest, deciduous, coniferous/taiga), grassland (savanna, prairie, steppe), desert, tundra |
| **Aquatic — freshwater** | **Lentic** (still water): pond, lake, wetland. **Lotic** (running water): river, stream |
| **Aquatic — marine** | Ocean, coral reef, estuary (river meets sea; brackish water) |
| **Man-made (artificial)** | Crop field, aquarium, garden, reservoir |

### 6.2 Pond ecosystem (NCERT model)

A pond is a **shallow water body** in which all four basic components of an ecosystem are seen clearly, and it is **self-sustaining**.

| Component | Members |
|---|---|
| Abiotic | Water, dissolved inorganic (CO₂, O₂, nitrates, phosphates) and organic substances, soil deposit at the bottom; light, temperature, day length |
| Producers | **Phytoplankton** (diatoms, *Chlamydomonas*, *Volvox*, *Spirogyra*), algae, floating plants (*Lemna*, *Pistia*, *Eichhornia*), submerged plants (*Hydrilla*, *Vallisneria*), marginal plants (*Typha*, sedges) |
| Primary consumers | **Zooplankton** (*Daphnia*, *Cyclops*, rotifers), free-swimming and bottom-dwelling herbivores (snails, tadpoles) |
| Secondary/tertiary consumers | Insects, small fish, large fish (e.g. *Wallago*), frogs, water snakes, kingfisher, heron |
| Decomposers | Bacteria, fungi, flagellates — abundant at the bottom |

**Zones of a pond or lake:**
- **Littoral zone** — shallow edge where light reaches the bottom; rooted plants.
- **Limnetic zone** — open-water surface layer where light penetrates; phytoplankton.
- **Profundal zone** — deep water below light penetration; decomposers; low O₂.
- **Benthic zone** — the bottom sediment (benthos = bottom organisms).

**Thermal stratification** in deep temperate lakes (summer): **epilimnion** (warm upper layer), **metalimnion/thermocline** (rapid temperature fall), **hypolimnion** (cold bottom layer). In autumn and spring, mixing ("turnover") brings oxygen down and nutrients up. Water is densest at **4 °C**, so ice floats and life survives under it.

### 6.3 Desert ecosystem

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Rainfall | Very low — usually **less than 25 cm (250 mm) a year**, erratic |
| Temperature | **Extremes**: very hot days, cold nights; hot deserts (Thar, Sahara) and cold deserts (Ladakh, Gobi) |
| Vegetation | **Scarce**; xerophytes — cacti, *Opuntia*, *Euphorbia*, *Acacia*, *Prosopis*, khejri (*Prosopis cineraria*), *Calotropis*; deep roots, thick cuticle, sunken stomata, CAM photosynthesis, leaves reduced to spines |
| Animals | Camel, desert fox, kangaroo rat (never needs to drink — gets water from fat oxidation and has highly concentrating kidneys), Great Indian Bustard, chinkara, lizards, snakes; many are **nocturnal** and burrowing |
| Productivity | **Lowest** among land ecosystems |
| India | **Thar** (hot, Rajasthan), Rann of Kachchh (salt); Ladakh and Spiti (cold desert) |

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q93 asked: "Desert ecosystem is characterised by — (A) abundance of trees … biologically integrated communities (B) dominated by grasses and other herbaceous plants (C) extremely high or low temperatures, with low rainfall and scarce or no vegetation." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (C).** (A) describes a **forest**, (B) a **grassland**.

## 7. Ecological succession

**Ecological succession** is the gradual, fairly predictable change in the species composition of an area until a stable community in balance with the climate is reached — the **climax community**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sere** | The entire sequence of communities that replace one another in an area |
| **Seral stage (seral community)** | Each transitional community |
| **Pioneer species** | The first species to invade a bare area |
| **Climax community** | Final, stable, self-perpetuating community in equilibrium with the climate |
| **Primary succession** | On a bare area where **no soil existed** — bare rock, cooled lava, newly formed pond or reservoir, sand dune; **very slow** (hundreds to thousands of years to make soil) |
| **Secondary succession** | Where a community existed but was destroyed — abandoned farmland, burnt or cut forest, flooded land; **faster** because soil and seeds are present |
| **Autogenic / allogenic** | Change caused by the organisms themselves / by outside factors (fire, flood) |
| **Autotrophic / heterotrophic** | Dominated by green plants (usual) / by heterotrophs (e.g. decomposition of a log) |

### Types by habitat

| Succession | Starts in | Pioneers | Ends in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydrarch (hydrosere)** | Water (pond, lake) | **Phytoplankton** | **Mesic** (medium-moisture) climax forest |
| **Xerarch (xerosere)** | Dry area — **lithosere** on bare rock, **psammosere** on sand | **Lichens** (crustose → foliose) | Mesic climax |
| **Halosere** | Saline soil/water | Salt-tolerant plants | — |

**Stages of hydrarch succession (NCERT):** phytoplankton → **submerged plant stage** (*Hydrilla*, *Vallisneria*) → **submerged free-floating stage** → **floating (rooted-floating) stage** (*Nymphaea*/lotus, *Trapa*, *Azolla*, *Lemna*) → **reed-swamp stage** (*Typha*, *Phragmites*, *Scirpus*) → **marsh-meadow (sedge) stage** → **scrub (shrub) stage** → **forest (climax)**. Silt and dead plants slowly fill the pond, so it becomes land.

**Stages of xerarch succession on rock:** crustose lichens (secrete acids that dissolve rock and begin soil formation) → foliose lichens → **mosses** → annual then perennial herbs and grasses → shrubs → trees (forest).

> **Remember:** Both hydrarch and xerarch successions move towards **medium water conditions (mesic)** — neither too wet nor too dry.

**Trends during succession:** total biomass, species diversity, soil depth, humus and niche specialisation **increase**; food chains become food webs; nutrient cycles become more closed; the ratio of production to respiration (P/R) moves towards 1 at climax.

**Climax theories (B.Sc.):** **Monoclimax** (F.E. Clements, 1916 — one climax per climatic region); **polyclimax** (A.G. Tansley — several climaxes controlled by soil, topography, fire); **climax pattern** hypothesis (R.H. Whittaker, 1953).

## 8. Ecosystem services and homeostasis

- **Ecosystem services**: purifying air and water, reducing drought and floods, cycling nutrients, making fertile soil, wildlife habitat, pollination, carbon storage, aesthetic and cultural value.
- **Robert Costanza and colleagues (1997)** put a price of about **US $33 trillion a year** on the world's ecosystem services (NCERT), nearly twice the global GNP of that time. **Soil formation** accounts for about **50%** of this value; recreation and nutrient cycling are each less than 10%; climate regulation and wildlife habitat about **6% each**.
- **Homeostasis (cybernetic system)**: an ecosystem resists change and returns to balance by feedback (more prey → more predators → fewer prey). **Resistance** = ability to stay unchanged; **resilience** = ability to recover after disturbance.

## Traps and exceptions

- **Energy flows one way; nutrients cycle.** "Energy cycles in an ecosystem" is always false.
- **Pyramid of energy is never inverted.** Pyramid of **biomass** is inverted in the **sea**; pyramid of **number** is inverted in a **tree** and in a **parasitic** food chain.
- In **terrestrial** ecosystems more energy flows through the **detritus** food chain; in **aquatic** ecosystems the **grazing** chain is the major conduit.
- **Oceans** cover 70% of the surface but give only about one-third (55 of 170 billion tonnes) of global NPP.
- **NPP = GPP − R**, not GPP + R. NPP is what herbivores can use.
- Plants trap only **2–10% of PAR**, and PAR is less than half of sunlight.
- **Ecosystem** — Tansley (1935); **ecology** — Haeckel (1866); **10% law** — Lindeman (1942); **food chain/pyramid** — Elton (1927).
- **Earthworm** is a **detritivore**; bacteria and fungi are true **decomposers**.
- Decomposition is slow when detritus is rich in **lignin and chitin**, fast when rich in **nitrogen and sugars**.
- **Pioneers**: **lichens** on rock, **phytoplankton** in water. Both seres end in a **mesic** climax.
- **Secondary succession is faster** than primary because soil already exists.
- **Desert**: rainfall < 25 cm, temperature extremes, sparse vegetation (TRE 1.0 key C). Grass-dominated = grassland.
- **Standing crop** is the biomass present at one moment; **productivity** is a rate. A small standing crop (phytoplankton) can have a high productivity.

## Quick revision

1. Ecosystem — **A.G. Tansley, 1935**; ecology — **Ernst Haeckel, 1866**.
2. Four functions of an ecosystem — productivity, decomposition, energy flow, nutrient cycling.
3. **NPP = GPP − R**.
4. Global NPP ≈ **170 billion t**; oceans ≈ **55 billion t**.
5. Plants trap **2–10% of PAR**; PAR < 50% of sunlight.
6. Decomposition steps — fragmentation, leaching, catabolism, humification, mineralisation.
7. Humus — dark, colloidal, resistant to decay, nutrient reservoir.
8. **10% law — Lindeman, 1942**, Cedar Bog Lake.
9. Energy flow follows the **two laws of thermodynamics**; it is unidirectional.
10. Land: **DFC** carries more energy; water: **GFC** is the main conduit.
11. Food web — interconnected food chains; gives stability.
12. Pyramid of energy — **always upright**.
13. Pyramid of biomass inverted — **sea** (phytoplankton 4, zooplankton 21, fish 25).
14. Pyramid of number inverted — **tree** and **parasitic** chains.
15. Pyramids ignore food webs, multi-level species and saprophytes.
16. Pond zones — littoral, limnetic, profundal, benthic.
17. Desert — rainfall < 25 cm; extreme temperatures; scarce vegetation.
18. Pioneers — lichens (rock), phytoplankton (water).
19. Hydrarch: phytoplankton → submerged → floating → reed swamp → sedge meadow → scrub → forest.
20. Primary succession on bare rock is slow; secondary on abandoned fields is faster.
21. Monoclimax — Clements; polyclimax — Tansley.
22. Ecosystem services valued at **US $33 trillion/yr** (Costanza); soil formation ~50%.
23. Most productive per area — coral reefs, estuaries, tropical rainforests; least — deserts.
