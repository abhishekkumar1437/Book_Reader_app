This chapter covers NCERT class 12 "Organisms and Populations" plus the nutrient-cycling part of "Ecosystem", extended to B.Sc. population ecology: growth equations, r/K selection, estimation methods and the full nitrogen cycle. TRE 1.0 asked which method measures the density of mobile animals (capture–recapture), and TRE 2.0 asked what lightning turns atmospheric nitrogen into (oxides of nitrogen).

> **In simple words:** A population is a group of the same species living in one place — all the rohu fish in a pond, all the tigers in Valmiki. It has properties a single animal does not have: birth rate, death rate, sex ratio, age structure and density. Populations grow fast when food is unlimited, then slow down when the habitat is full. Species living together help, harm or ignore one another. Meanwhile carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus move from air, water and rock into living bodies and back again.

## 1. Organism and its environment

### Major abiotic factors

| Factor | Key idea | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Affects enzyme activity and metabolism | **Eurythermal** (tolerate a wide range — most animals); **stenothermal** (narrow range — corals, many tropical fish) |
| **Water** | Salinity matters for aquatic life (inland water < 5‰, sea 30–35‰, hypersaline lagoons > 100‰) | **Euryhaline** (wide salinity range — salmon, eel); **stenohaline** (narrow — most freshwater fish) |
| **Light** | Photoperiod controls flowering, migration, breeding | Deep-sea animals live in permanent darkness |
| **Soil** | Texture, pH, minerals decide plants and hence animals | — |

### Responses to abiotic factors

| Response | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Regulate** (homeostasis) | Keep internal conditions constant | Birds and mammals (**~99% of animals and nearly all plants are not regulators**); human body at 37 °C |
| **Conform** | Body temperature/osmotic concentration changes with surroundings | Most animals and all plants; **small animals rarely live in polar regions** (large surface area per volume loses heat fast) |
| **Migrate** | Move temporarily to a better habitat | Siberian cranes to **Keoladeo (Bharatpur)**; birds to Kanwar Jheel and Nagi–Nakti in Bihar |
| **Suspend** | Reduce activity for a period | **Hibernation** (winter sleep — bears, frogs), **aestivation** (summer sleep — snails, lungfish), **diapause** (zooplankton in lakes), seed dormancy, bacterial spores |

### Adaptations and ecological rules

- **Kangaroo rat** (North American desert) meets its water needs by **internal fat oxidation** and concentrates urine so little water is lost.
- **Desert plants**: thick cuticle, sunken stomata, **CAM** pathway (stomata open at night), leaves reduced to spines (*Opuntia* — photosynthesis by flattened stem).
- **Allen's rule**: mammals of colder climates have **shorter ears and limbs** to reduce heat loss.
- **Bergmann's rule**: animals of colder climates are **larger** (more volume, less surface per volume).
- **Seals** have a thick layer of fat (**blubber**) below the skin for insulation.
- **High altitude sickness** (nausea, fatigue, heart palpitations) at places like Rohtang Pass and Manasarovar — low O₂; the body adapts by **increasing RBC production**, reducing haemoglobin's binding affinity and **increasing breathing rate**.
- Archaebacteria in **hot springs and deep-sea hydrothermal vents** (above 100 °C).
- **Liebig's law of the minimum** (1840): growth is controlled by the nutrient in shortest supply. **Shelford's law of tolerance** (1913): organisms have a minimum, maximum and optimum for each factor.

## 2. Population attributes

| Attribute | Meaning | NCERT example |
|---|---|---|
| **Birth rate (natality)** | Number of births per individual per unit time | Pond had 20 lotus plants; 8 new plants added by reproduction in a year → birth rate = 8 ÷ 20 = **0.4 offspring per lotus per year** |
| **Death rate (mortality)** | Number of deaths per individual per unit time | 4 of 40 fruit flies died in a week → death rate = 4 ÷ 40 = **0.1 per fly per week** |
| **Sex ratio** | Ratio of females to males | 60% females, 40% males |
| **Age distribution** | Individuals in pre-reproductive, reproductive and post-reproductive groups | Shown as **age pyramids** |
| **Population density (N)** | Number (or biomass, or percent cover) per unit area | 200 Parthenium plants per m² vs one huge banyan tree — for the banyan, **percent cover or biomass** is a better measure than number |

### Age pyramids

<div class="formula">

**Expanding (growing)** — broad base, **triangular**: many young (India in the past, many developing countries).

**Stable** — **bell-shaped**: pre-reproductive ≈ reproductive.

**Declining** — **urn-shaped**: fewer young than adults (Japan, several European countries).

</div>

### Measuring density — methods

| Method | Used for | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Total count** | Large animals in small areas, trees | Count every individual (e.g. tigers in a reserve by camera traps) |
| **Quadrat method** | **Plants and sessile/slow animals** (barnacles, snails) | Square frames (1 m² etc.) placed at random; count individuals inside |
| **Capture–recapture (mark–recapture) method** | **Mobile animals** (fish, birds, rodents, insects) | Catch, mark, release; recapture later; estimate by Lincoln index |
| **Indirect indices** | Elusive animals | **Pugmarks** (old tiger census), **faecal pellets**, nests, calls; now **camera traps** and DNA from scat |
| **Relative density** | When absolute count is not needed | Fish caught per trap; birds seen per hour |

> **PYQ:** TRE 1.0 (2023) Q92 asked: "Which of the following methods is used for the measurement of population density for more mobile organisms? — (A) Capture–recapture method (B) Quadrat method (C) Simple counting method." **BPSC key (2nd provisional): (A).** Quadrats suit plants and fixed animals; simple counting fails when animals keep moving.

### Lincoln index (Lincoln–Petersen estimate) — worked numbers

<div class="formula">

**N = (M × C) ÷ R**

N = estimated population size · M = number caught, marked and released the first time · C = total caught the second time · R = marked animals among the second catch (recaptures)

</div>

**Worked example 1.** In a pond 60 fish are netted, tagged and released. A week later 50 fish are netted, of which 12 carry tags. N = (60 × 50) ÷ 12 = 3,000 ÷ 12 = **250 fish**.

**Worked example 2.** 100 rats marked; second trap catches 80, of which 20 are marked. N = (100 × 80) ÷ 20 = **400 rats**.

**Worked example 3.** 45 butterflies marked; second sample 60 with 9 marked. N = (45 × 60) ÷ 9 = **300**.

**Assumptions:** marks do not fall off or harm the animal; marked animals mix fully with the rest; no births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples; marked and unmarked animals are equally likely to be caught.

### Quadrat calculations

<div class="formula">

**Density** = total individuals of a species ÷ total number of quadrats studied

**Frequency (%)** = (number of quadrats in which the species occurs ÷ total quadrats studied) × 100

**Abundance** = total individuals of a species ÷ number of quadrats in which it occurs

</div>

**Worked example 4.** 10 quadrats are laid. Species X occurs in 4 of them with 2, 5, 3 and 6 plants (total 16). Density = 16 ÷ 10 = **1.6 per quadrat**; frequency = 4 ÷ 10 × 100 = **40%**; abundance = 16 ÷ 4 = **4 per quadrat of occurrence**.

## 3. Population growth

The size of a population changes by four processes: **natality (B)** and **immigration (I)** add; **mortality (D)** and **emigration (E)** remove.

<div class="formula">

**Nₜ₊₁ = Nₜ + [(B + I) − (D + E)]**

Population grows if (B + I) > (D + E).

</div>

**Worked example 5.** A deer population of 500 has 80 births, 30 deaths, 10 arrivals and 20 departures in a year. Next year N = 500 + (80 + 10) − (30 + 20) = **540**.

### Exponential growth (J-shaped curve)

When resources are **unlimited**, every species grows at its full innate potential — **exponential (geometric) growth**.

<div class="formula">

**dN/dt = rN**  · integral form **Nₜ = N₀ eʳᵗ**

r = **intrinsic rate of natural increase** = b − d (per capita birth rate minus death rate); e = 2.718

**Doubling time ≈ 0.693 ÷ r** (because ln 2 = 0.693)

</div>

- r values (NCERT): **Norway rat 0.015**, **flour beetle 0.12**; human population of India in 1981 had **r = 0.0205**.
- **Worked example 6.** A bacterial culture with r = 0.693 per hour doubles every **1 hour**; starting from 1,000 cells, after 5 hours N = 1,000 × 2⁵ = **32,000**.
- **Worked example 7.** If r = 0.0205 per year, doubling time = 0.693 ÷ 0.0205 ≈ **34 years**.
- **Darwin's example**: even a slow-breeding elephant pair, if all young survived, would leave about 19 million descendants in 750 years — the basis of his "struggle for existence".

### Logistic growth (S-shaped / sigmoid curve)

Resources are always limited. Growth starts slowly (**lag phase**), speeds up (**log or exponential phase**), slows down (**deceleration**) and levels off at the **carrying capacity (K)** — the **asymptote** — the maximum population the habitat can support. This is the **Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth** model (P.F. Verhulst 1838; Raymond Pearl and Reed 1920).

<div class="formula">

**dN/dt = rN × (K − N) ÷ K**

When N is small, (K − N)/K ≈ 1 → near-exponential growth. When N = K, growth = 0. **Fastest growth at N = K/2.**

</div>

**Worked example 8.** r = 0.4 per year, K = 1,000, N = 250. dN/dt = 0.4 × 250 × (1,000 − 250)/1,000 = 100 × 0.75 = **75 per year**. At N = 500 (K/2): 0.4 × 500 × 0.5 = **100 per year** (maximum). At N = 1,000: **0**.

> **Exam angle:** The **logistic model is considered more realistic** (NCERT). The difference between the J-curve and the S-curve is the **environmental resistance**. Fisheries use N = K/2 to set the **maximum sustainable yield**.

### Life-history strategies: r and K selection

Term from **Robert MacArthur and E.O. Wilson (1967)**, *The Theory of Island Biogeography*.

| Feature | **r-selected** | **K-selected** |
|---|---|---|
| Environment | Unstable, unpredictable | Stable, predictable |
| Body size | Small | Large |
| Offspring | **Many, small** | **Few, large** |
| Parental care | Little or none | Much |
| Maturity | Early | Late |
| Life span | Short | Long |
| Survivorship curve | **Type III** (high early death) | **Type I** (most live to old age) |
| Population | Fluctuates, often below K, J-type bursts | Stays near K |
| Examples | Bacteria, insects, many fish (oyster, cod), annual weeds | Elephant, whale, humans, tiger, large trees |

**Survivorship curves** (Raymond Pearl): **Type I** — low mortality until old age (humans, large mammals); **Type II** — constant death rate at all ages (many birds, *Hydra*, lizards); **Type III** — very high death of young (oysters, fish, most plants).

> **Remember:** The Pacific salmon and bamboo breed **only once** in their lifetime (**semelparous**); most birds and mammals breed many times (**iteroparous**). Some species produce many small young (oysters, pelagic fish), others few large young (birds, mammals) — NCERT.

## 4. Population interactions

| Interaction | Species A | Species B | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mutualism** | + | + | **Lichen** (fungus + alga/cyanobacterium); **mycorrhiza** (fungus + plant roots, e.g. *Glomus*); **fig and fig-wasp** (wasp pollinates, lays eggs inside the fig); *Rhizobium* + legumes; **Mediterranean orchid *Ophrys*** uses "**sexual deceit**" — a petal resembles a female bee and the male bee pollinates it (a "mutualism" exploited one-sidedly); termites and gut flagellate *Trichonympha*; zooxanthellae and corals |
| **Competition** | − | − | **Flamingos and resident fishes** compete for zooplankton in South American lakes; *Paramecium* species in a culture; **Abingdon tortoise** of the Galápagos became extinct within a decade after **goats** were introduced (better browsing efficiency); **Balanus** excluding *Chthamalus* on the Scottish coast (Connell) |
| **Predation** | + | − | Tiger–deer; sparrow eating seeds; **prickly pear cactus** in Australia brought under control by a **cactus-feeding moth** (*Cactoblastis*) — biological control |
| **Parasitism** | + | − | *Plasmodium*, tapeworm, liver fluke (needs two intermediate hosts — snail and fish); *Cuscuta* (dodder, no chlorophyll) on hedge plants; **brood parasitism** — the **koel (cuckoo)** lays eggs in the **crow's** nest |
| **Commensalism** | + | 0 | **Orchid** (epiphyte) on a mango branch; **barnacles** on a whale; **cattle egret** and grazing cattle (egrets catch insects stirred up); **clown fish** among the stinging tentacles of a **sea anemone** (NCERT calls it commensalism) |
| **Amensalism** | − | 0 | *Penicillium* secretes penicillin that kills bacteria; a large tree shading out herbs |

### Predation — details

- Predators keep prey numbers in check and maintain **species diversity** by reducing competition among prey. When starfish *Pisaster* was removed from a rocky intertidal community, **more than 10 species of invertebrates became extinct within a year** (Paine) because of increased competition.
- About **25% of all insects are phytophagous** (plant eaters).
- **Prey defences**: camouflage (cryptic colour — some frogs, insects); **monarch butterfly** is distasteful because, as a caterpillar, it feeds on a poisonous weed and stores the chemicals; **thorns** (*Acacia*, cactus); **cardiac glycosides** in *Calotropis* (cattle and goats never browse it); nicotine, caffeine, quinine, strychnine and opium are plant defence chemicals.
- **Lotka–Volterra** equations (1925–26) describe predator–prey cycles; the classic data are **snowshoe hare and lynx** (Hudson's Bay Company fur records).

### Parasitism — details

- **Ectoparasites** live on the surface (lice on humans, ticks on dogs, copepods on marine fish, *Cuscuta*). **Endoparasites** live inside (tapeworm, liver fluke, *Plasmodium*).
- Adaptations: loss of unnecessary sense organs, **adhesive organs or suckers**, loss of digestive system (tapeworm), **high reproductive capacity**, complex life cycles with intermediate hosts.
- Many parasites are **host-specific** — host and parasite tend to **co-evolve**.

### Competition — Gause's principle

- **Gause's competitive exclusion principle** (**G.F. Gause, 1934**): **two closely related species competing for the same limited resource cannot coexist indefinitely** — the competitively inferior one is eliminated. Experiment: ***Paramecium aurelia* and *P. caudatum*** grown together — *P. caudatum* died out.
- **Resource partitioning**: species avoid competition by choosing different times, places or food. **Robert MacArthur** showed that **five closely related species of warblers** living on the same tree avoided competition by feeding in different parts (branches) of the tree.
- **Interference competition** — one species directly harms another's feeding. **Exploitation competition** — one species uses up the shared resource.
- **Competitive release**: a species whose range is restricted by a competitor expands dramatically when the competitor is removed (Connell's barnacles).
- Competition is more likely between closely related species, but **unrelated species** can also compete (flamingos and fish).

> **Memory trick:** Signs "**+ +** mutual, **+ 0** commensal, **− 0** amensal, **+ −** predator/parasite, **− −** competition." Commensal = "come and share the table" (only one gains, the other is not affected).

## 5. Biogeochemical (nutrient) cycles

The movement of nutrient elements through the various parts of an ecosystem — rock, soil, water, air and living bodies — is **nutrient cycling** or **biogeochemical cycling**. Reservoirs make up deficits when inflow and outflow are unbalanced.

| Type | Reservoir | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Gaseous cycles** | **Atmosphere / hydrosphere** | **Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen**, water |
| **Sedimentary cycles** | **Earth's crust (rocks, soil)** | **Phosphorus, sulphur**, calcium, potassium |

Factors regulating the rate: soil, moisture, pH, temperature.

### 5.1 Carbon cycle

- Carbon makes up about **49% of the dry weight** of organisms; it is next only to water.
- Of the total global carbon, about **71% is dissolved in oceans** — the oceanic reservoir regulates the amount of CO₂ in the atmosphere.
- **Fossil fuels** are also a carbon reservoir.
- About **4 × 10¹³ kg of carbon is fixed in the biosphere by photosynthesis every year** (NCERT).
- Carbon returns to the atmosphere as CO₂ by **respiration** of producers and consumers, **decomposition** of litter and dead organic matter, **burning** of wood and fossil fuels, **forest fires** and **volcanic** activity.
- Carbon locked in **sediments** (limestone, shells, coral reefs — CaCO₃) leaves the cycle for very long periods.
- Human impact: **deforestation and burning of fossil fuels** have raised atmospheric CO₂ from about **280 ppm (pre-industrial)** to about **420+ ppm** (2024–25).

N₂ makes up about **78%** of the atmosphere, but plants cannot use N₂ directly. It must first be "fixed" into ammonia (NH₃) or nitrate (NO₃⁻).

| Step | Change | Agents |
|---|---|---|
| **Biological nitrogen fixation** | N₂ → NH₃ (enzyme **nitrogenase**; needs ATP and anaerobic conditions) | Free-living: ***Azotobacter***, ***Beijerinckia*** (aerobic), ***Clostridium*** (anaerobic), *Rhodospirillum*; cyanobacteria ***Anabaena***, ***Nostoc*** (in **heterocysts**); symbiotic: ***Rhizobium*** in legume root nodules, ***Frankia*** in *Alnus* (non-legume), *Anabaena* in the fern ***Azolla*** (rice fields), *Anabaena* in *Cycas* coralloid roots |
| **Atmospheric (lightning) fixation** | High temperature and pressure of lightning makes N₂ combine with O₂ → **nitric oxide (NO)** → nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) → with rain, **nitric acid (HNO₃)** → nitrates in soil | Lightning, also UV radiation |
| **Industrial fixation** | N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (Haber–Bosch process, ~450 °C, ~200 atm, iron catalyst) | Fertiliser factories |
| **Ammonification** | Dead organic nitrogen (proteins, urea) → NH₃ | Decomposer bacteria and fungi |
| **Nitrification** | NH₃ → **NO₂⁻** (nitrite) → **NO₃⁻** (nitrate) | ***Nitrosomonas*** and *Nitrococcus* (to nitrite); ***Nitrobacter*** (to nitrate) — chemoautotrophs |
| **Assimilation** | Plants absorb NO₃⁻ / NH₄⁺ and make amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids | Plants; animals get N by eating plants |
| **Denitrification** | NO₃⁻ → N₂ (back to air) | ***Pseudomonas***, ***Thiobacillus denitrificans*** — in waterlogged, anaerobic soil |

- Root nodules contain pink **leghaemoglobin**, which binds O₂ and protects nitrogenase from oxygen.
- Global estimates (approx.): biological fixation ~**140–175 million tonnes/year** (largest natural source); lightning ~5–10% of natural fixation; industrial fixation now roughly equals biological fixation.

> **PYQ:** TRE 2.0 (2023) Q137 asked: "During lightning, the high temperature and pressure created in the air convert nitrogen into — (A) chloroform (B) ammonia (C) oxides of nitrogen." **Official answer: (C) oxides of nitrogen.** Lightning makes N₂ react with O₂ to give NO and NO₂; these dissolve in rain as nitric/nitrous acids and reach the soil as nitrates. Ammonia is formed by **biological** (and industrial) fixation, not by lightning.

### 5.3 Phosphorus cycle

- Phosphorus is a major part of **nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), ATP, phospholipids of membranes**, bones, teeth and shells.
- **Natural reservoir: rocks** containing phosphates. **Weathering** releases small amounts of phosphate into the soil; plants absorb it; animals get it from plants; **phosphate-solubilising bacteria** release it from dead bodies.
- Differences from the carbon cycle (NCERT): **atmospheric input of phosphorus through rainfall is much smaller**; **gaseous exchange of phosphorus between organism and environment is negligible** (no respiratory release).
- Phosphates washed into the sea settle in sediments and are lost for a long time; **guano** (droppings of sea birds) and phosphate rock are mined for fertiliser.
- Excess phosphate from fertilisers and **detergents** in run-off causes **eutrophication** (see chapter 03).

### 5.4 Sulphur and water cycles (brief)

- **Sulphur**: reservoir in rocks (sulphates, pyrite) and sediments; also gaseous phase (SO₂, H₂S). Bacteria: ***Desulfovibrio*** reduces sulphate to H₂S; ***Thiobacillus*** and **Beggiatoa** oxidise sulphur and H₂S to sulphate. Human burning of coal releases SO₂ → acid rain.
- **Water cycle**: evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, run-off and infiltration; driven by solar energy. About 97% of Earth's water is in oceans; about 2% in ice caps and glaciers; less than 1% is fresh liquid water available.

### 5.5 Comparison table

| Feature | Carbon | Nitrogen | Phosphorus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type | Gaseous | Gaseous | **Sedimentary** |
| Main reservoir | Atmosphere (CO₂) and **oceans (71% of carbon)** | **Atmosphere (78% N₂)** | **Rocks (phosphates)** |
| Entry into living world | Photosynthesis | Nitrogen fixation | Absorption from soil after weathering |
| Return | Respiration, decomposition, combustion | Ammonification, denitrification | Decomposition (phosphate-solubilising bacteria) |
| Human disturbance | Fossil fuels, deforestation → global warming | Fertilisers, vehicles (NOₓ) → eutrophication, acid rain | Fertilisers, detergents → eutrophication |

## 6. Community attributes (B.Sc. extras)

- **Species richness** — number of species; **evenness** — how equally individuals are spread among species.
- **Diversity indices**: **Shannon–Wiener index (H′)** and **Simpson's index** — higher value, more diverse community.
- **Dominant species** — most abundant or controlling; **ecotone** — transition zone between two communities (e.g. forest–grassland edge, estuary); **edge effect** — greater number and density of species at the ecotone.
- **Ecological niche** — the functional role and position of a species, including what it eats, when and where it is active. **Two species cannot occupy the same niche** for long (Gause).
- **Ecological equivalents** — unrelated species occupying similar niches in different regions (kangaroo in Australia, bison in North America).

## Traps and exceptions

- **Capture–recapture** is for **mobile animals**; **quadrat** for plants and sessile animals (TRE 1.0 key A).
- In the Lincoln index **N = M × C ÷ R** — divide by the **recaptured marked** number, not by the total second catch.
- **Logistic** growth is the **realistic** one; exponential occurs only when resources are unlimited.
- Growth rate in the logistic model is **maximum at K/2**, not at K.
- **r** in growth equations is the **intrinsic rate of natural increase**, not "rate of reproduction" alone (r = b − d).
- **Clown fish–sea anemone** is given as **commensalism** in NCERT (some books call it mutualism).
- **Orchid on a mango branch** — commensalism, not parasitism (it makes its own food).
- **Cuckoo (koel)** shows **brood parasitism**, not commensalism.
- **Lightning** makes **oxides of nitrogen** (NO, NO₂) — not ammonia (TRE 2.0 official answer C).
- *Nitrosomonas* → **nitrite**; *Nitrobacter* → **nitrate**; *Pseudomonas* → back to **N₂**.
- **Phosphorus** and sulphur cycles are **sedimentary**; phosphorus has **no significant gaseous phase**.
- **71% of carbon** is dissolved in the **oceans**; **78%** of air is nitrogen — do not swap them.
- **Frankia** fixes nitrogen in non-leguminous *Alnus*; **Azolla–Anabaena** is used as biofertiliser in **rice** fields.
- Gause's experiment used ***Paramecium aurelia* and *P. caudatum*** — not *Amoeba*.

## Quick revision

1. Population attributes — natality, mortality, sex ratio, age distribution, density.
2. Lotus example: birth rate = 8/20 = **0.4**; fruit-fly death rate = 4/40 = **0.1**.
3. Age pyramids — triangular (expanding), bell (stable), urn (declining).
4. Mobile animals → **capture–recapture**; plants → **quadrat**.
5. Lincoln index **N = MC/R**.
6. Nₜ₊₁ = Nₜ + [(B + I) − (D + E)].
7. Exponential: **dN/dt = rN**, J-curve; Nₜ = N₀eʳᵗ.
8. Logistic: **dN/dt = rN(K − N)/K**, S-curve, Verhulst–Pearl.
9. K = carrying capacity; fastest growth at **K/2**.
10. r for India 1981 = **0.0205**.
11. r/K selection — **MacArthur and Wilson (1967)**.
12. Gause's competitive exclusion — **1934**, *Paramecium*.
13. MacArthur's warblers — resource partitioning.
14. Abingdon tortoise died out after **goats** were introduced (competition).
15. Prickly pear controlled by a **moth** in Australia (predation/biocontrol).
16. *Ophrys* orchid — sexual deceit; fig–wasp — mutualism.
17. Koel in crow's nest — **brood parasitism**.
18. Carbon = **49%** of dry weight; **71%** of carbon in oceans; **4 × 10¹³ kg** fixed per year.
19. Lightning → **oxides of nitrogen** → nitrates.
20. Nitrogenase protected by **leghaemoglobin**.
21. Denitrifiers — *Pseudomonas*, *Thiobacillus*.
22. Phosphorus reservoir — **rocks**; no gaseous phase of note.
23. Allen's rule — short limbs in cold; Bergmann's — large body in cold.

## Practice with answers

1. 80 fish are marked; later 100 are caught, of which 16 are marked. Population size? — **500** (80 × 100 ÷ 16).
2. Which method estimates the density of tigers or fish best? — **Capture–recapture** (or camera-trap recapture for tigers).
3. In 20 quadrats, a species occurs in 5 with a total of 30 plants. Frequency? — **25%** (5/20 × 100).
4. Same data — abundance? — **6** (30 ÷ 5).
5. Population of 1,000 with 120 births, 70 deaths, 20 immigrants, 10 emigrants. Next year? — **1,060**.
6. Which curve results from limited resources? — **Sigmoid (S-shaped, logistic)**.
7. dN/dt = rN(K − N)/K; at N = K, what is growth? — **Zero**.
8. Doubling time when r = 0.05 per year? — **About 13.9 years** (0.693 ÷ 0.05).
9. Elephants and whales are which type of strategists? — **K-selected**.
10. Which survivorship curve do oysters show? — **Type III** (very high early mortality).
11. Who gave the competitive exclusion principle? — **G.F. Gause (1934)**.
12. Barnacles on a whale show which interaction? — **Commensalism**.
13. *Penicillium* killing bacteria is an example of? — **Amensalism**.
14. What does lightning convert atmospheric nitrogen into? — **Oxides of nitrogen** (TRE 2.0 official answer).
15. Which bacterium converts ammonia to nitrite? — ***Nitrosomonas***.
16. Which nitrogen-fixer lives in *Azolla*? — ***Anabaena azollae***.
17. Which cycle is purely sedimentary with negligible gaseous phase? — **Phosphorus cycle**.
18. Percentage of global carbon dissolved in the oceans? — **About 71%**.
19. The transition zone between two communities is called? — **Ecotone** (with edge effect).
20. Which rule says animals of colder climates have shorter limbs and ears? — **Allen's rule**.
